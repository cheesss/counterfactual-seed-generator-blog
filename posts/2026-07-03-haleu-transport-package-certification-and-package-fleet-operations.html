HALEU transport-package certification and package fleet operations

The obvious bottleneck in advanced-reactor fuel is enrichment

The obvious bottleneck in advanced-reactor fuel is enrichment. A reactor developer needs high-assay low-enriched uranium, the United States has little domestic commercial HALEU supply, Russia has been the historic commercial source, and every large public funding announcement tends to count kilograms, metric tons, centrifuges, deconversion awards, or reactor schedules. That explanation is directionally right. It is also incomplete.

Between enriched UF6 and a loaded advanced reactor sits a smaller, less liquid bottleneck: a package certificate, a criticality-safety validation trail, a physical fleet of packages and cylinders, a registered user base, shipment procedures, and enough payload per trip to make the logistics tolerable. The constraint is not simply "a cask." It is the conjunction of a certified package, licensed contents, benchmarked physics, qualified manufacturing, and repeatable operations under U.S. transportation rules.

The shape is validation-evidence

The shape is validation-evidence. The scarce asset is the document-and-data chain that lets a regulator and a package holder say, for this HALEU form, enrichment, geometry, moderator condition, absorber arrangement, mass, heel, package damage state, and transport configuration, the package remains subcritical and compliant. Steel is not the only scarce input. The harder constraint may be the evidence that lets the steel carry commercial quantities.

DOE's own transport language points there. The Department says the United States has "limited licensed options"to transport HALEU and that using highly enriched uranium casks for large HALEU quantities would be costly and time-consuming. In a 2024 explainer, DOE wrote that if existing HEU shipping containers were used for HALEU, it would take "thousands of shipments"to fuel one advanced reactor because those containers were not optimized for HALEU. DOE then put money behind the issue: up to $16 million for research, development, and NRC certification of new or modified HALEU transportation packages, followed by $11 million in awards to NAC International, Westinghouse, Container Technologies Industries, American Centrifuge Operating, and Paragon D&E, with up to three-year performance periods for new package designs and up to two years for modifications. Sources: DOE HALEU Transportation, https://www.energy.gov/ne/haleu-transportation; DOE transport explainer, https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/finding-better-way-transport-haleu.

The physical reason is simple enough to state and difficult to license away. Traditional fresh LWR fuel logistics were built around uranium enriched up to about 5% U-235. HALEU sits above 5% and below 20%. That extra fissile content changes the criticality-safety problem, especially when transport assumptions include water, flooding, moderator intrusion, damaged package geometry, arrays of packages, and nonstandard fuel forms such as TRISO particles, pebbles, compacts, molten-chloride salts, or metallic fuel precursors. A certificate of compliance is not a generic passport for "uranium." It is content-specific and condition-specific.

The first strong evidence point is Orano's DN30-X

The first strong evidence point is Orano's DN30-X. Orano's public HALEU transport paper describes the problem with standard 30B UF6 cylinders: for contents above 5% enrichment, full flooding has to be considered and the standard configuration is not enough. Orano's solution uses the existing DN30 protective structural package with a new 30B-X cylinder and an internal criticality control system using boron-bearing rods. The NRC certificate for DN30-X lists two content variants: DN30-10 up to 10% enrichment with 1,460 kg UF6 maximum, and DN30-20 up to 20% enrichment with 1,271 kg UF6 maximum. By comparison, a standard 30B cylinder is commonly associated with 2,277 kg UF6 capacity. The certified HALEU configuration therefore solves the licensing problem by giving up some payload and adding neutron poison and analysis. Sources: NRC/RAMPAC DN30-X certificate, https://rampac.energy.gov/docs/default-source/certificates/1019388-R1.pdf; Orano HALEU transport package paper, https://www.orano.group/usa/en/our-portfolio-expertise/orano-white-papers/haleu-supply-chain-transport-packages.

That certificate did not end the bottleneck. The clearest project-level clue is Urenco USA's 2026 exemption at the National Enrichment Facility in New Mexico. The Federal Register notice says that, at the time of the request, "only one approved UF6 transport package"existed for commercial quantities of HALEU: Orano's DN30-X under CoC No. 9388. It also says Urenco's higher-enriched UF6 contracts had a current need for transport and that the CoC 9388 package and 30B-10 cylinders were not ready for that need. NRC therefore granted a limited exemption allowing Urenco to use existing DN30 packaging under CoC 9362 for about 40 to 50 cylinders of UF6 enriched above 5% but below 10% in 2026-2027 to a single customer, with a CSI of 16.6 and no physical package-design changes. Source: Federal Register / NRC Urenco exemption, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/04/2026-11192/louisiana-energy-services-llc-dba-urenco-usa-national-enrichment-facility-exemption.

That case is the hinge. It does not prove that transport dominates every HALEU deployment. It proves something narrower and more useful: even when enriched material and customer demand exist, the certified optimized package fleet can still be out of phase with need. The workaround used the regulator's exemption authority, a constrained enrichment band, a fixed cylinder count, a single customer, and an existing package family. That is not a scalable logistics regime; it is a bridge.

The second strong evidence point is NAC's OPTIMUS-L

The second strong evidence point is NAC's OPTIMUS-L. NAC announced in January 2024 that the NRC had approved OPTIMUS-L for HALEU TRISO fuel compacts under CoC No. 9390 Rev. 2, calling it the first U.S.-licensed high-capacity package above 500 lb payload for HALEU TRISO. The same announcement emphasized the commercial issue: smaller drums could require excessive packagings and handling steps, while high-capacity packages reduce the number of packages needed. An NRC letter later acknowledges Rev. 3 of CoC 9390 for OPTIMUS-L. NAC then announced NRC certification for OPTIMUS-H in 2024 and disclosed a delivered fleet of 22 OPTIMUS-L and nine OPTIMUS-H systems since 2020. Sources: NAC OPTIMUS-L, https://www.nacintl.com/newsroom/nac-news/nac-international-receives-u-s-nrc-approval-for-the-optimus-l-to-transport-haleu-triso-contents; NRC OPTIMUS-L letter, https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2403/ML24032A321.pdf; NAC OPTIMUS-H, https://www.nacintl.com/newsroom/nac-news/nac-international-receives-u-s-nrc-certification-for-the-optimus-h-transport-packaging-system.

The OPTIMUS case cuts both ways. It shows the market can produce licensed high-capacity packages. It also shows that each fuel form and customer use case still needs the correct amendment, content basis, and operating envelope. A package certified for one HALEU TRISO compact campaign does not automatically clear UF6, molten salt fuel, metallic fuel, or a different TRISO geometry.

The third evidence point is DNCSH, the DOE/NRC Criticality Safety for Commercial-Scale HALEU initiative. ORNL says DNCSH is developing public benchmarks relevant to HALEU fuel forms, geometries, moderators, absorbers, and operating conditions, with goals that explicitly include higher-throughput fuel-cycle processes, higher-capacity transportation package designs, fewer NRC requests for additional information, and lower licensing uncertainty. DOE's criticality page says the results will be crucial for licensing facilities and certifying transport packages under 10 CFR Part 70 and Part 71. Sources: ORNL DNCSH, https://www.ornl.gov/dncsh; DOE Criticality Benchmarking, https://www.energy.gov/ne/criticality-benchmarking.

The raw benchmark numbers explain why the initiative exists

The raw benchmark numbers explain why the initiative exists. A DNCSH presentation states that the International Criticality Safety Benchmark Evaluation Project contains more than 5,000 benchmarks, with roughly 430 HALEU critical experiments, but only about 19 relevant for non-LWR HALEU cases, 11 of which predate 1980. The same deck describes existing non-LWR packages as not yet sufficient for commercial-scale needs in some configurations. Versa-Pac, for example, is discussed with low U-235 mass and no direct benchmark data in the deck's TRISO framing, while OPTIMUS-L is described with up to 68 kg of 20%-enriched TRISO compacts in a package then under review. Source: DNCSH slide deck, https://ncsp.llnl.gov/sites/ncsp/files/2024-03/76_2024-01-24_dncsh.pdf.

The named DNCSH projects are not generic science. They are logistics-enabling work. THETA targets TRISO-form HALEU fuel transport and water infiltration of pebble fuel containers. The Y-12/ORCEF work revisits historical UF6 cylinder critical experiments because no UF6 cylinder benchmarks were in ICSBEP for that case. A TerraPower-related project targets chlorine configurations for molten chloride salt fuel because current chlorine benchmarks are insufficient for criticality-safety needs in fabrication and transport. Other projects cover 19.75% UO2 fuels, stainless steel sensitivity, TRISO compacts, optimum moderation, and advanced moderator data. Source: DOE Criticality Benchmarking Project List, https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/Criticality%20Benchmarking%20Project%20List_0.pdf.

The visible fuel bottleneck still has force. Centrus reached 900 kg of HALEU production in June 2025 at Piketon, which DOE called the first such U.S. production milestone, and DOE extended the contract for another 900 kg over the next year. DOE also says the Piketon plant is the only U.S. facility licensed to enrich up to 19.75% and that there is currently no commercial U.S. HALEU source. DOE's allocation process says it must seek to make 21 metric tons of HALEU available by June 30, 2026, and expects domestic commercial HALEU enrichment capacity around 2030. Sources: Centrus/DOE 900 kg milestone, https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/centrus-reaches-900-kilogram-mark-haleu-production; DOE HALEU allocation process, https://www.energy.gov/media/331054.

Fabrication can also dominate

Fabrication can also dominate. NRC licensed TRISO-X's Oak Ridge HALEU fuel fabrication facility in February 2026 under Part 70; the NRC press release says it is the first U.S. approval of a Category II fuel fabrication facility, and the Federal Register says SNM-7007 authorizes TRISO-X to possess enriched uranium below 20% U-235 for 40 years. Before receiving special nuclear material and starting operations, X-energy says NRC must still inspect startup readiness, including equipment, safety systems, procedures, and trained operators. Sources: NRC TRISO-X press release, https://www.nrc.gov/sites/default/files/cdn/doc-collection-news/2026/26-019.pdf; Federal Register TRISO-X notice, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/16/2026-05086/triso-x-llc-special-nuclear-material-license-for-the-triso-x-fuel-fabrication-facility; X-energy TRISO-X announcement, https://x-energy.com/news/triso-x-receives-first-ever-part-70-haleu-fuel-fabrication-license/.

TerraPower's Natrium project is the cleanest counterargument

TerraPower's Natrium project is the cleanest counterargument. In 2022, TerraPower said HALEU fuel availability would delay Natrium by at least two years because domestic and allied HALEU manufacturing would not reach commercial capacity in time for the proposed 2028 in-service date. TerraPower's own FAQ says Natrium uses HALEU metallic fuel and that the first-of-a-kind cost includes fuel fabrication and fuel qualification. Sources: World Nuclear News on Natrium delay, https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/HALEU-fuel-availability-delays-Natrium-reactor-pro; TerraPower FAQ, https://www.terrapower.com/faq/.

In 2023-2026, enrichment, deconversion, and fabrication are often the loudest constraints because there

That counterargument does not invalidate the transport-package gate. It defines the handoff. In 2023-2026, enrichment, deconversion, and fabrication are often the loudest constraints because there is too little material and too little qualified fuel-making capacity. As enrichment projects move from demonstration cascades to contracted capacity, the bottleneck can migrate to the next certified interface. The question for capital allocation is not whether HALEU enrichment matters. It is whether each additional kilogram can move through a licensed chain without forcing exemptions, low-payload packages, serial one-off amendments, or a narrow set of package owners.

The control points are concrete

The control points are concrete. NRC controls Part 71 certificates and Part 70 fuel-facility licenses. DOE and ORNL coordinate the public criticality data that can shorten licensing loops. Orano controls the DN30-X/30B-X approach for UF6. NAC controls OPTIMUS package designs and delivered fleet operations. TN Americas and Framatome hold other package families that may be useful for particular contents. National labs control benchmark production, code validation, and experimental infrastructure. Cylinder and package fabricators control the physical build. Deconverters and fuel fabricators become demand aggregators because they know which packages can actually arrive and depart on schedule.

Markets can miss the node because the dollar amounts look small. An $11 million transport-package award is a rounding error beside a multibillion-dollar enrichment task order or reactor demonstration grant. But an industrial bottleneck does not need to be a large spend category to have leverage. It only needs to sit where all higher-value flows must pass. A package certificate that raises payload, reduces shipment count, or avoids an exemption can pull schedule risk out of a much larger reactor program.

The strongest failure modes are equally concrete. First, DN30-X and similar packages may enter normal fleet service fast enough that the transport constraint becomes a solved logistics detail. Second, enrichment may stay so tight through the late 2020s that package capacity remains underutilized; the cask is irrelevant if material never arrives. Third, project developers may redesign initial demonstrations around available package envelopes, smaller fuel lots, or DOE allocation windows, turning transport into a planning cost rather than a gating item. Fourth, NRC may continue to grant narrow exemptions where the safety case is strong, lowering the scarcity value of optimized packages but not eliminating the need for a commercial fleet.

The watch list is narrow: new NRC Part 71 certificate applications and amendments for HALEU contents

The watch list is narrow: new NRC Part 71 certificate applications and amendments for HALEU contents; RAMPAC updates for DN30-X, OPTIMUS, Versa-Pac, MAP-12-13, TN-B1, and new awardee designs; evidence that 30B-X cylinders are in production and available to users other than the design sponsor; Urenco-style exemption requests; NRC requests for additional information that cite criticality benchmarks or validation gaps; DNCSH project results entering package safety analysis reports; DOE HALEU allocation requests that require evidence of shipping and licensed storage; and actual shipments rather than press releases.

The restrained conclusion is that HALEU transport-package certification is not the whole bottleneck

The restrained conclusion is that HALEU transport-package certification is not the whole bottleneck. It is a relay point in a moving bottleneck chain. In a fuel system still short of enrichment and fabrication, it can look secondary. As material becomes available, the certificate-and-fleet layer can become the tollbooth that separates a paper fuel supply from a deliverable fuel supply. The useful lens is not "who owns uranium." It is "who can move each qualified form, in commercial quantities, through a regulator-approved package with enough evidence to make the route repeatable."
