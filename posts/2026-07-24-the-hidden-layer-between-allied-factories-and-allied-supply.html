The Hidden Layer Between Allied Factories and Allied Supply

Allied governments are spending heavily to localize missiles and ammunition. Factories, forging lines, live-fire tests, and annual production targets make that expansion easy to see. What remains harder to observe is the administrative and technical chain that determines whether another military can identify, accept, order, issue, and sustain the output.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-24 · 13 min read

In brief

  • 12 missiles | Australia proved local GMLRS assembly and live fire, not portable acceptance.
  • 15 countries | NATO is building shared indirect-fire certification without a named-item recognition result.
  • US$960.8M | NSM shows procurement can scale while OEMs retain configuration and sustainment control.
  • 2030 | The thesis turns on whether shared evidence eliminates retesting and produces repeat orders.

Australia’s Guided Multiple Launch Rocket System program captures the distinction. In January 2024, Australia awarded Lockheed Martin Australia A$37.4 million for work that included technical-data transfer and engineering certification. Port Wakefield opened in December 2025, production began in March 2026, and 12 Australian-made missiles were fired at Woomera on April 8, 2026. Australia plans capacity of as many as 4,000 missiles a year by 2029. (Australian GMLRS firing)

That sequence proves that Australia can assemble and test the missile to the intended technical baseline. It does not yet disclose a formal receiving-authority acceptance record, an approved-source action linked to Australian output, a third-country order, or operational issue of an Australian-made lot.

That missing step is the central industrial transition. Allied production becomes more valuable when evidence created at one site can support acceptance and procurement elsewhere without rebuilding the entire national qualification process.

Recognition is a sequential gate

Qualification evidence and codification are not the first obstacles confronting a new munitions source. Funded demand, export authority, technical-data rights, tooling, process capability, component availability, and configuration control come first. A site that cannot manufacture repeatably has no useful evidence package to transport.

The recognition layer becomes important after those earlier conditions are met. Test reports, raw measurements, material certificates, inspection records, deviations, approved configurations, manufacturer references, and national acceptance decisions must remain linked. A successful firing proves performance under defined conditions. It does not automatically establish that another buyer may order the article or substitute it for output from the original factory.

The NATO Codification System supplies part of this identity infrastructure, but it is a sovereign process rather than a commercial barcode service. DLA’s July 2026 guidance says a manufacturer cannot request an NSN directly. A government sponsor, valid technical data, and validated demand are required, and an existing stock identity may already contain a relevant part-number relationship. DLA also notes that local or one-time procurement may proceed without an NSN. (DLA NSN guidance)

The important transaction may therefore be a manufacturer-reference update, source approval, configuration decision, or procurement-system change rather than issuance of a new number. Accelerated NSN issuance is too narrow a description of the opportunity. The broader control point is the governed connection between evidence, approved sources, item identity, procurement eligibility, and sustainment.

The Canadian M795 case is the strongest evidence that an allied source can enter a repeatable U.S. procurement channel. IMT Defense received a US$391.015 million contract in November 2022 and opened a new automated facility at Ingersoll, Ontario, in October 2024. The U.S. Army reported capacity of 15,000 shell bodies a month. The reported capacity concerns metal bodies, not loaded complete rounds, and the exact first accepted production lot remains undisclosed.

The Australian M795 experience is the stronger warning. Thales performed trial and industrialisation work, but Australia ended that route in 2025 and moved to a A$72 million Rheinmetall NIOA forging pathway in 2026. Australian audit evidence found that no projectiles had been delivered under the preceding effort by December 2025, with production-line qualification, quality, certification, facility, and process issues still unresolved. (Australian munitions audit)

Common technical data did not overcome weak process maturity, ageing infrastructure, contract design, or a change in the government’s selected industrial route. Evidence portability can accelerate a viable pathway; it cannot make the pathway viable.

Mature systems show why elapsed time can remain long

The M72 family demonstrates how a stable identity, field history, variant development, and repeat procurement can compound over decades. The original weapon was qualified and offered to NATO countries in 1962. In December 2021, the U.S. Army awarded Nammo Defense Systems a five-year IDIQ production contract worth US$498.093 million for M72 variants and components. (M72 contract)

That is a mature product ecosystem, but it does not provide a clean modern example of a qualification package moving quickly from a Norwegian or Finnish authority into a U.S. catalog decision. Much U.S. production occurs through Nammo’s American subsidiary. The case proves the durability of an accepted product family more clearly than it proves rapid portability of foreign evidence.

Carl-Gustaf provides the opposite lesson: operational utility can precede broad institutional acceptance by decades. U.S. special operations selected the system in 1988 and began fielding it in 1991. Conventional Army requirements expanded much later. The lighter M3A1 received an urgent materiel release in 2019, completed type classification with the HE 441E round in January 2023, and reached final Army fielding in 2024.

The intervening work involved doctrine, training, safety, ammunition integration, maintenance, acquisition ownership, and logistics—not simply proof that the weapon worked. Once those pieces aligned, ammunition orders, spare-parts contracts, and repair demand followed.

The closest historical analogues therefore do not support a simple “test, number, order” clock. They support a layered transition in which technical acceptance, institutional ownership, procurement authority, and sustainment readiness must converge.

NATO is building the common layer, but has not proved it

NATO’s 2025 defence-production plan calls for wider contractual use of common standards, data sharing for ammunition interchangeability, cross-certification, and multinational testing. Its indirect-fire testing and certification initiative now lists 15 participating countries and is intended to harmonize national firing, testing, and certification procedures. (NATO production plan)

The initiative remains described as being established. No public record yet identifies a named ammunition class, one shared test package, the national authorities that accepted it, an eliminated retest, and the resulting procurement decision.

That distinction separates institution-building from operating proof. A common procedure would be progress. A report accepted by two national authorities would be stronger. A repeat order issued without duplicated national testing would establish the mechanism.

Until then, the multinational recognition layer is an early-deployment ecosystem assembled from existing government functions, OEM systems, test infrastructure, and catalog services. It is not yet a repeatable product with a demonstrated qualification-to-order service level.

Who controls the transition

Sovereign authorities retain final acceptance, export, procurement, and codification power. National codification bureaus decide what enters their records. Military technical authorities decide whether evidence satisfies national requirements. Procurement authorities decide whether validated demand exists.

Original OEMs control another critical layer. Lockheed Martin supplies the authoritative GMLRS baseline, engineering interpretation, training, and production support. Kongsberg is expanding NSM production, sustainment, and technical-refresh capacity in the United States while leading the planned Australian manufacturing and service facility. Saab and Nammo similarly remain central to configuration, ammunition, training, and sustainment for their product families.

This structure favors incumbent-led localization rather than displacement. Lockheed Martin, Kongsberg Gruppen, Saab, Moog, Northrop Grumman, and Rheinmetall have operating exposure to the transition through data, engineering, components, manufacturing, and support. That exposure is not evidence that a separate independent software or certification market will capture equivalent value.

The first spending wave is capital-intensive: facilities, tooling, training, component localization, and test campaigns. Recurring value appears later in configuration maintenance, change control, source surveillance, catalog updates, technical publications, spares, repair, software support, and technical refresh.

The UK has already unbundled one part of this workflow. Its Ministry of Defence awarded a £6.449868 million codification-services contract with two firm years and options for years three and four. (UK codification award) The award proves a contracted service category, not a four-year fully committed revenue stream or a large multinational software market.

Independent providers gain durable control only when governments separate these activities from prime contracts, require transferable data rights, adopt interoperable schemas, and permit alternate-source approval without continuous OEM interpretation. Otherwise, localization increases allied capacity while preserving the incumbent’s customer relationship and workflow authority.

Procurement can route around the common layer

The strongest counterargument is that allied procurement may not need a broad portable-evidence ecosystem at all.

NATO’s multinational munitions frameworks have already produced acquisition cycles, deliveries, shared inventories, and reported cost and schedule benefits through demand aggregation and common procurement. Those programs show that buyers can achieve interoperability and supply benefits by aligning requirements and placing coordinated orders, even when the underlying qualification and catalog transactions remain opaque.

NSM makes the same point at product level. A Norwegian-designed missile moved from a U.S. competition in 2018 to ship installation in 2022 and a US$960.8 million multiyear award in 2024. Kongsberg retained production, sustainment, technical-refresh, and configuration roles. The product scaled across borders without visible evidence that an independent catalog or certification platform controlled the transition.

DLA’s own rules reinforce the objection: government demand generally comes before NSN creation, and some procurement can proceed without a new number. Catalog records may document an accepted procurement decision rather than cause it.

If Australian GMLRS, Canadian M795, or Australian-produced NSM receives repeat cross-border orders through bilateral program controls while no shared evidence package, avoided retest, or meaningful multinational source-record action becomes visible, the narrower explanation wins. The durable control point would remain with sovereign buyers and OEM configuration authorities.

The next state transition is repeatable evidence reuse

The ecosystem should remain classified as early deployment. Its constituent parts already operate: factories produce, ranges test, authorities qualify, bureaus codify, primes manage configurations, and logistics agencies sustain fielded systems. What is missing is a closed modern case showing that those parts work together as a repeatable allied process.

The transition to repeatable procurement requires more than another factory opening. A named item must use one controlled evidence package across at least two national authorities, eliminate or materially reduce a national retest, receive a dated source or operational-record action, and then win a cross-border repeat order.

That could occur before 2030. Australian GMLRS is the nearest test because production and live firing are complete. NATO’s indirect-fire initiative could supply the common procedure. Newcastle will test whether a mature Norway-U.S. acceptance pathway can extend to Australian production and service from 2027. Rheinmetall NIOA’s M795 route will test whether evidence and technical work survive a supplier-path reset.

Failure would also be informative. If production quality, export authority, platform integration, demand, and contracting continue to explain most elapsed time, qualification portability will remain a useful complement rather than the organizing mechanism of allied munitions expansion.

What to watch

  • By December 2027, a primary record links Port Wakefield GMLRS output to approved-source or delivery-acceptance status.
  • By December 2028, NATO names an ammunition class using a shared multinational test or certification procedure.
  • By December 2028, Rheinmetall NIOA produces a buyer-accepted Australian M795 lot.
  • By December 2028, Newcastle produces or formally services an accepted NSM or JSM article.
  • By December 2030, one program documents an eliminated retest or at least 30% faster acceptance through foreign evidence reuse.
