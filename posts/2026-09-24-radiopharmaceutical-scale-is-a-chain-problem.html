Radiopharmaceutical Scale Is a Chain Problem: Decay, Qualification, Release, Transport and Licensed Administration

Radiopharmaceuticals are sometimes described as if radioactive decay creates a permanent, industry-wide shortage by itself. That framing is too simple.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-22 · 13 min read

In brief

  • Radioactive decay matters, but it is only one constraint in a multi-stage operating chain.
  • Pluvicto’s 2023 U.S. shortage was resolved; it should not be described as current.
  • Ac-225 remains limited, while qualification and commercial-scale adequacy remain unresolved questions.
  • Manufacturing announcements are not equivalent to qualified, released and reliably delivered commercial supply.
  • Durable control lies across isotope production, drug-product release, routing and licensed administration.

The constraint is a chain, not a universal shortage

The more useful model is a synchronized chain:

qualified isotope → drug-product manufacture → quality control and release → compliant transport → receipt before labelled expiry → administration at an appropriately licensed site.

Decay affects that chain because usable time is finite. But the commercial bottleneck can sit at several different points. A producer may have isotope but insufficient qualified manufacturing. A finished dose may exist but still require release before shipment. A released product may face transport constraints. A treatment centre may have product access but limited capacity to administer it. Reimbursement or clinical considerations may constrain utilization before isotope availability does.

That distinction matters because different radiopharmaceuticals can occupy very different operating states. A historical shortage in one product does not prove a permanent structural shortage across the field. Conversely, expansion of one established supply network does not demonstrate that a newer isotope has achieved comparable commercial-scale reliability.

The central industrial question is therefore narrower: who can repeatedly convert qualified radioactive material into released drug product, move it within its usable window and complete administration under applicable licensing conditions?

That is a control-point map, not an investment recommendation.

Pluvicto shows both the constraint and the recovery

Pluvicto offers a useful case because it demonstrates that radiopharmaceutical supply can become constrained—and later recover.

The U.S. Pluvicto shortage in 2023 is historical. FDA declared the shortage resolved on October 26, 2023, and Novartis subsequently described U.S. supply as unconstrained. It would therefore be inaccurate to use that episode as evidence that Pluvicto remains in shortage today. (Novartis supply update)

The recovery is important analytically. It shows that a radiopharmaceutical constraint can be addressed through manufacturing and network expansion rather than being permanently dictated by radioactive decay.

At the same time, decay still shapes the operating architecture.

The 2026 FDA label gives Pluvicto a shelf life of 120 hours from calibration. The labelled dose is 7.4 GBq, or 200 mCi, with ±10% tolerance. The label also specifies radiation shielding, use of a sealed container, Type A shipment requirements and restrictions tied to labelled expiry. (FDA Pluvicto label)

Those requirements impose scheduling discipline. Manufacturing, release, shipping, receipt and treatment cannot be organized independently.

But one qualification is important: the FDA label does not require direct delivery from the manufacturer to the administration site. The operational requirement is compliance within the labelled and regulatory framework, not one mandatory distribution topology.

That leaves room for different logistics structures as long as the product remains compliant and usable.

Treatment-site numbers require denominator discipline

Another source of confusion is the number of radiopharmaceutical treatment sites.

Published figures do not use one consistent denominator.

Earlier 2023 material referred to more than 200 facilities or ordering locations. A current Pluvicto treatment-centre locator says more than 700 centres, while noting that participation in the locator is optional. An HCP FAQ cites more than 800 Pluvicto sites. RLT Now says there are more than 850 U.S. RLT sites.

Those numbers should not be collapsed into a single definitive count.

“Sites,” “centres,” participating locator entries, ordering facilities and broader radioligand-therapy locations are not necessarily interchangeable categories. The correct conclusion is simply that the network has expanded substantially while published counts reflect different definitions.

The same caution applies to manufacturing.

Novartis identifies Millburn, Indianapolis and Carlsbad as operating U.S. locations. It lists Denton for 2028 and Winter Park for 2029. The latter two are planned facilities, not current operating capacity. (Novartis RLT FAQ)

A credible capacity map must therefore distinguish at least three states: operating assets, announced projects and potential future output.

Treating all three as current capacity overstates the system that exists today.

Decay changes logistics, but release remains a separate gate

The physical clock begins before the commercial process is complete.

For Pluvicto, the 120-hour labelled shelf life means usable inventory cannot simply be manufactured and warehoused like a conventional long-lived product. Scheduling errors consume part of the available window.

Yet “decay-sensitive” should not become shorthand for “delivery is the only bottleneck.”

Before administration, a product still has to satisfy manufacturing and release requirements. Release is especially important because nominal isotope production does not automatically become administrable drug product.

This distinction becomes even more important when evaluating emerging isotopes.

A large theoretical isotope output does not answer whether commercial batches can repeatedly meet required specifications. Nor does construction of a production facility establish release performance, stability, uptime or fulfilment reliability.

Physical production is necessary. Commercial adequacy is broader.

A useful way to think about the chain is:

Control pointWhat must happenWhat capacity claims do not prove
Isotope productionQualified isotope becomes availableReleased drug-product volume
Drug-product manufacturingIsotope is converted into the intended productBatch consistency or commercial adequacy
Quality and releaseProduct satisfies applicable release requirementsReliable fulfilment at scale
TransportProduct moves compliantly inside the usable windowOn-time administration
Treatment siteProduct is received and administered under applicable licensing conditionsPatient throughput or reimbursement
BillingAdministration is reported through the revenue cycleAny extension of the physical shelf-life clock

The last row is frequently misplaced in operational diagrams.

CMS identifies A9607 as the HCPCS code for lutetium Lu-177 vipivotide tetraxetan, billed per 1 mCi. That is downstream revenue-cycle reporting after administration; it is not part of the physical decay window governing production, release and transport. (CMS HCPCS summary)

Licensed administration does not imply one mandatory pharmacy model

Regulation adds another constraint, but it should also be described precisely.

Under 10 CFR 35.300, qualifying radioactive material can come through a manufacturer or preparer authorized under §32.72 or an equivalent pathway. Preparation can also occur through an authorized nuclear pharmacist, authorized physician or appropriately supervised individual under the applicable framework. (10 CFR 35.300)

That means it is incorrect to state that every treatment site must obtain radiopharmaceuticals through an external nuclear pharmacy.

The important point is broader: administration sits inside a regulated material-handling and licensing structure.

This makes treatment capacity different from ordinary physician-office capacity. Expanding drug-product supply does not by itself guarantee proportional growth in administrable throughput. The downstream network has its own operating requirements.

Those requirements can become binding even when manufacturing is adequate.

Ac-225 is a different problem from the resolved Pluvicto shortage

Actinium-225 should not be analysed by simply extending the 2023 Pluvicto story.

The Department of Energy describes Ac-225 as high-demand and limited, while multiple production routes are being pursued. (DOE Ac-225)

But the important issue is not merely gross isotope quantity. Supply is qualification-specific.

For a commercial therapeutic chain, the relevant question is not whether Ac-225 can be produced somewhere. It is whether a given production route and manufacturing system can repeatedly supply material that supports the required pharmaceutical quality, release and regulatory package at commercial scale.

That is why development milestones require careful interpretation.

NorthStar has reported FDA acceptance of a Type II Drug Master File for its no-carrier-added Ac-225. That can be a meaningful regulatory-process milestone, but a DMF is not an FDA approval. It also does not independently establish that supply is commercially adequate.

Evidence needed to evaluate scale would extend further: batch-release performance, purity, stability, yield, production uptime, fulfilment reliability and relevant CMC and inspection evidence. (NorthStar DMF announcement)

The distinction is fundamental.

Production capability is not the same thing as qualified commercial supply.

Announced facilities show intent, not realised output

Large facilities are another area where language can outrun evidence.

TerraPower has announced a $450 million, 250,000-square-foot Ac-225 facility in Philadelphia and has described the project as supporting a claimed 20-fold capacity expansion. Those are company-announced project and capacity claims. They should not be converted into realised operating output before that output exists and is demonstrated. (TerraPower project announcement)

Similarly, ITM has described a 10-fold Lu-177 capacity expansion associated with a new production facility. That figure is also a company claim about its own Lu-177 expansion. (ITM facility announcement)

The two figures should not be treated as directly comparable isotope-output metrics. They involve different isotopes and company-specific capacity baselines.

The relevant analytical sequence is therefore:

announced project → commissioned facility → qualified process → reproducible batches → released product → reliable fulfilment.

Skipping the middle stages creates the illusion of supply before commercial supply has actually been established.

Vertical integration shortens handoffs, but does not eliminate them

Manufacturing architecture can still matter.

RayzeBio/Bristol Myers Squibb describes its Indianapolis model as integrating isotope production with drug-product manufacturing. That potentially consolidates important upstream steps within one operating system. (RayzeBio manufacturing)

But integration does not remove the entire chain.

Final release still matters. Delivery still matters. Receipt still matters. Licensed administration still matters.

RYZ101 also remains Phase 3 and investigational, with no located marketing authorization in the supplied evidence. It should therefore not be treated as an already commercialized Ac-225 therapy when discussing current market supply. (BMS pipeline)

This is a useful example of why manufacturing readiness and commercial-product status need separate columns on any industry map.

A company can invest in production architecture ahead of regulatory approval. That may be strategically important, but it is not equivalent to current commercial demand or marketed-product output.

The strongest counterargument: isotope supply may not be the binding constraint

Any serious analysis of radiopharmaceutical bottlenecks needs to state the counterargument plainly.

Pluvicto’s shortage was resolved. Manufacturing and treatment networks expanded. That experience weakens any claim that radiopharmaceuticals are structurally incapable of scaling.

Moreover, isotope availability is not automatically the first constraint encountered by a therapy.

Clinical efficacy and toxicity can bind. Reimbursement can bind. Treatment-centre capacity can bind.

Even when isotope production is sufficient, utilization can be limited elsewhere in the system.

This is why the sector should not be reduced to an isotope-scarcity thesis.

For an established product such as Pluvicto, the historical evidence shows that manufacturing constraints can be relieved and distribution networks can expand.

For Ac-225, by contrast, the evidence still supports caution around qualification and scale. DOE continues to describe the isotope as limited, and announced production initiatives do not by themselves establish dependable commercial-scale supply for future drug products.

Both statements can be true simultaneously.

There is no contradiction between saying Pluvicto’s 2023 shortage is resolved and saying Ac-225 commercial-scale qualification remains an open industrial question.

The durable control point is end-to-end execution

The most informative industry map does not begin and end with the reactor, accelerator or isotope-production line.

It follows material all the way to administration.

At the upstream end, the question is whether the isotope is available through a qualified route.

Next comes drug-product manufacturing: can the isotope be incorporated into a reproducible pharmaceutical process?

Then release: can batches consistently meet the necessary specifications?

Then transport: can released material be routed compliantly within its labelled usable period?

Finally, the treatment network must be able to receive and administer it under applicable licensing conditions.

Only after administration does revenue-cycle reporting such as HCPCS coding enter the chain.

This sequence is more demanding than simply measuring nominal isotope output. It also explains why different participants may control different bottlenecks.

One organization may own isotope production. Another may specialize in drug-product manufacturing. Another may organize distribution. Treatment centres control the final clinical handoff. Integrated systems may internalize several stages, but they still face release, transport and administration requirements.

The strategic issue is therefore not radioactive scarcity in the abstract. It is repeatable coordination across the control points that convert radioactive material into an administered therapy.

What to watch

  • Qualified output: Evidence that expanded isotope production repeatedly converts into specification-compliant, released material.
  • Release performance: Batch consistency, purity, stability, yield and reliable release at increasing production volumes.
  • Operational uptime: Whether new facilities sustain production rather than merely reaching commissioning or announced capacity.
  • Fulfilment: Demonstrated ability to route released doses through transport and treatment scheduling inside labelled expiry.
  • Administration capacity: Whether licensed treatment networks expand fast enough to absorb additional drug-product supply.
  • Ac-225 evidence: CMC, inspection and commercial-scale operating data that distinguish qualified supply from nominal isotope production.
