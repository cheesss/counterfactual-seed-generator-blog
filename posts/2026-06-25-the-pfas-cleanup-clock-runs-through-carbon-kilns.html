The PFAS Cleanup Clock Starts After the Filter Works

The public PFAS drinking-water story usually ends when a treatment plant starts producing compliant water. A utility detects PFOA or PFOS, selects granular activated carbon, ion exchange, or membranes, and funds the installation through rates, grants, state revolving funds, or legal settlements.

The visible buildout is only the first operating cycle

That description captures the first capital cycle. It misses the operating cycle that begins after the treatment system works.

Adsorption does not eliminate PFAS. It moves the contaminant from water into carbon or resin. Membranes move it into concentrate. Once a bed reaches breakthrough, the utility must secure replacement media, remove the spent material, transport it, obtain acceptance from a permitted outlet, document the transaction, and keep the water system operating throughout the exchange.

The defensible thesis is narrower than a claim that the United States already faces a national carbon-kiln shortage. Through 2028, engineering, financing, permitting, construction, testing, and workforce constraints are more likely to pace implementation. From roughly 2028 through 2031, residual management becomes a plausible sequential gate as the installed base begins accumulating first and second replacement cycles. It may bind selectively by technology, geography, permit, and contract—not uniformly across the country.

EPA’s 2024 rule set enforceable 4.0-parts-per-trillion limits for PFOA and PFOS, with initial monitoring due in April 2027 and compliance originally due in April 2029. EPA’s May 2026 proposal would retain those limits while allowing qualifying systems to obtain two additional years and would rescind four other federal PFAS requirements.The possible 2031 date would apply to systems receiving an approved exemption, not to every public water system. (EPA drinking-water proposal)

The policy clock may stretch. The physical sequence does not disappear.

Treatment changes the residual rather than removing the obligation

The treatment choice determines which residual problem a utility inherits.

Granular activated carbon adsorbs PFAS until the carbon approaches breakthrough. The operator then replaces it with virgin or reactivated carbon. Spent GAC may be reactivated, incinerated, or landfilled, depending on its condition, the receiving facility, applicable permits, and customer requirements.

PFAS-selective drinking-water ion-exchange resins are generally designed for single-use replacement. Their compact footprint makes them attractive at constrained well sites, but exhausted resin still requires packaging, transport, and an accepted incineration or landfill route. This conclusion should not be generalized to every ion-exchange system; EPA’s current guidance also recognizes regenerative systems and regeneration brines.

Reverse osmosis and nanofiltration can achieve high removal rates, but they create a concentrate stream. Coastal or industrial systems with a permitted discharge option may find this manageable. Inland systems may face a much harder brine question.

EPA’s revised 2026 destruction-and-disposal guidance is more favorable to incumbent thermal routes than its 2024 version. EPA reports greater than 99.99% destruction and removal efficiency for selected PFAS in a full-scale carbon-reactivation furnace and states that certain hazardous-waste combustors and GAC reactivation units can effectively destroy PFAS under specified conditions. The agency still identifies gaps around products of incomplete combustion and comprehensive emissions characterization. Its performance-testing recommendations remain guidance rather than a universal federal acceptance rule. (EPA 2026 residual guidance)

This changes the bottleneck question. The issue is not whether every PFAS residual must pass through a scarce kiln. It is whether each operating plant can repeatedly secure a regulator-, customer-, and contract-acceptable residual pathway at predictable cost and turnaround.

The contra-cases matter as much as the expansion projects. SCV’s IX operations and Bemidji’s GAC changeouts show that residual routes already function. Neither incineration nor landfill universally prevents plant operation. Any national-shortage thesis must explain why these operating pathways will stop scaling—or become materially more expensive, slower, or legally fragile—as treatment volumes grow.

The commercial transition is from equipment to service loops

Municipal treatment has reached repeatable procurement. Utilities are issuing solicitations for complete plants with vessels, instrumentation, controls, warranties, commissioning, training, and acceptance testing.

Residual services are less standardized, but the American Water–Calgon contract shows what a more mature commercial structure can look like. The nine-year agreement covers GAC, equipment, and reactivation services at more than 50 sites across ten states. A large operator chose a portfolio arrangement rather than purchasing every carbon load and reactivation cycle as a separate spot transaction. (American Water agreement)

That agreement proves commercial possibility, not market-wide economics. Its value, annual volume, minimum purchase commitments, reserved capacity, price index, service-level penalties, title-transfer terms, and supplier margin have not been publicly disclosed.

The value shift is therefore mechanical before it is strategic. As plants accumulate operating cycles, more spending moves from construction toward replacement carbon or resin, field exchange, transport, reactivation or disposal, laboratory testing, chain-of-custody records, and contract administration.

Margin and control do not necessarily follow the spending. Freight surcharges may simply pass fuel costs through to the customer. Waste fees may compensate operators for liability rather than produce exceptional returns. Utilities continue to own the compliance obligation and customer relationship. Regulators still decide which residual routes and evidence packages are acceptable.

EPA’s 2024 economic analysis estimated approximately $1.55 billion in annualized rule costs, mostly from treatment. That number is a regulatory cost estimate under the 2024 rule, not a current market-size figure. It predates the proposed 2026 narrowing and cannot be assigned wholesale to carbon, resin, engineering, or waste companies.

Control sits where capacity, qualification, and logistics meet

The strongest commercial position would combine four capabilities: qualified replacement media, an accepted residual outlet, field-service logistics, and contractually reliable turnaround.

Calgon and its parent Kuraray are moving toward that integrated model. Calgon supplies carbon and treatment equipment, operates reactivation assets, transports spent material, performs field exchanges, and markets technical and emergency services. Its Columbus expansion would add approximately 27 million pounds per year of drinking-water reactivation capacity and two potable-water kilns, with startup targeted for the first quarter of 2028. (Calgon Columbus expansion)

The company also launched Filtrasorb Rx in June 2026 as a specification-oriented reactivated carbon product. That is an attempt to turn reactivation from a disposal service into a differentiated potable-water product supported by quality controls and performance documentation. The lifecycle-cost claims remain vendor-sponsored until utilities or independent engineers publish comparative pricing and repeated-cycle performance. (Filtrasorb Rx launch)

Other treatment routes distribute control differently. Xylem’s Evoqua operations, DuPont’s Purolite business, LANXESS, and other resin suppliers influence IX selection and replacement-media supply. Combustors and landfills control acceptance for spent resin and GAC that cannot be reactivated. Laboratories control low-level water testing, waste characterization, and any permit- or customer-driven emissions verification.

The missing commercial documents are the most revealing ones: residual-specific insurance wording, indemnity schedules, waste-acceptance agreements, capacity reservations, turnaround guarantees, rejected-load provisions, and data-rights clauses. Without them, it is premature to claim that operational outsourcing also transfers long-tail environmental risk.

Relief may arrive before a national shortage forms

The Columbus project is both confirmation and contradiction.

A nearly $100 million investment confirms that an incumbent expects enough potable-water reactivation demand to justify specialized capacity. But the additional capacity may prevent the shortage that appears to justify the investment. Announced capacity is not booked demand, and the public record does not disclose utilization, contracted volume, queue length, or expected return.

Federal timing also reduces the probability of one synchronized demand spike. The proposed exemption process could move approved systems toward 2031. State rules and already-funded projects will keep treatment activity moving, but plant startups and first changeouts may spread over several years.

Technology substitution adds another relief valve. Utilities with constrained sites can choose IX and move the residual burden away from carbon reactivation. Systems with discharge options can select membranes. Others may blend sources, replace contaminated wells, or reduce treatment volume through source control.

The base case is therefore not a nationwide kiln crisis. It is a larger recurring service market in which some regions or technologies may encounter tight accepted capacity, additional qualification requirements, or difficult liability allocation. The constraint becomes materially stronger only when utilities begin delaying required media changes or curtailing treated-water production because they cannot obtain an accepted outlet.

The strongest counterargument is that the real gate remains upstream

EPA’s own implementation diagnosis focuses on water-quality data, technology selection, construction, testing, financing, affordability, and workforce availability. It does not cite residual-outlet scarcity as the demonstrated reason systems need more time.

That may remain true for years. A utility cannot experience a spent-media bottleneck until it has financed, permitted, built, commissioned, and operated the treatment plant long enough to exhaust the media.

The closest historical comparison is the U.S. arsenic rule. EPA adopted a 10-parts-per-billion standard in January 2001, and systems had to comply by January 2006. The rule produced familiar disputes over science, cost, and small-system affordability before moving into a multiyear implementation program. (EPA arsenic-rule history)

PFAS differs in important ways. Detection occurs at parts-per-trillion levels. Treatment creates persistent residuals with unsettled destruction, emissions, and long-tail liability questions. Litigation and manufacturer settlements also shift part of the funding burden. The arsenic precedent supports a prolonged implementation cycle; it does not establish that residual infrastructure will become the binding national constraint.

The thesis fails in its stronger form if the 2028–2031 installed base completes repeated media cycles with stable pricing, ordinary turnaround, no production curtailments, and no broad tightening of permit, testing, or insurance requirements.

What to watch

  • December 31, 2027: a second utility discloses a multi-site, multi-year GAC-and-reactivation contract.
  • June 30, 2028: Calgon’s added Columbus potable-water reactivation capacity enters commercial operation.
  • December 31, 2029: a municipal contract specifies media, transport, residual route, and turnaround in one scope.
  • Quarterly through 2029: count outlet-caused media-changeout delays or treated-water production curtailments.
  • December 31, 2031: a permit or contract requires PFAS-specific full-scale thermal-performance evidence.

The PFAS cleanup clock does not stop when a filter vessel starts. It changes from a construction schedule into an operating schedule.

The next question is no longer whether utilities can remove PFAS from water. It is whether they can repeatedly move the captured mass through an accepted service chain without losing time, flexibility, or control.
