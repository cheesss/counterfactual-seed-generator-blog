Federal AI Procurement Is Starting to Buy the Exit Path

Federal agencies can now buy access to leading commercial AI models at prices designed to make the software entitlement look incidental. GSA’s OneGov page has advertised introductory offers ranging from $0.25 to $1, although the products, eligibility rules, durations, and pricing bases differ. USAi gives agencies a shared environment for testing models, comparing them, tracking usage, and accessing multiple providers.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-24 · 13 min read

In brief

  • $0.25–$1 OneGov offers make model access look cheaper than operating control.
  • GSA’s 2026 draft clause turns portability and model changes into contract duties.
  • HHS is testing whether agencies will pay for evidence, integration, and transition.
  • The thesis fails if agencies scale without these duties and lose no control.

That is real progress, but it is not the decisive commercialization transition. The relevant sequence is capability, qualification, customer acceptance, and then repeatable procurement and operation.

A federal agency must still authorize the service, define what happens to government data, connect identity and records systems, evaluate performance against agency tasks, preserve evidence for oversight, manage model changes, and leave the provider without losing control of the workflow. These functions turn a model subscription into an operating service.

The sharper thesis is therefore narrower than “portability will drive AI adoption.” Exit, observability, and model-change obligations are a sequential enterprise-operability gate. Agencies encounter it after they have obtained model access and begun clearing more immediate problems involving security authorization, data readiness, integration, staffing, and user demand.

The current state is qualification, not repeatable procurement. Policy exists. Shared access exists. A proposed standard clause exists. A detailed agency solicitation exists. Public evidence of repeated awarded terms, accepted monitoring artifacts, priced migrations, exercised remedies, and completed provider transitions does not.

The contract is becoming part of the technical architecture

OMB’s April 2025 memorandum, M-25-22, placed portability, intellectual-property rights, recurring testing, vendor performance, model rollback, feature-change notification, contract administration, and usable closeout data inside the federal AI acquisition lifecycle.

That matters because it changes the unit the government is trying to buy. A model endpoint can be demonstrated in days. An administrable production service requires defined rights, operating records, test criteria, escalation paths, and approval authority.

GSA’s June 17, 2026 proposed large-language-model clause translates much of that policy into draft contract language. It calls for government-data export in open or standard machine-readable formats, associated schemas and metadata, documented APIs, and migration tooling. It would restrict proprietary formats, added charges, and licensing conditions that materially obstruct retrieval or migration.

The draft also addresses model change as an operating event. Contractors would provide 30 days’ notice of planned material changes, notify the government of identified performance degradation within seven days, and report specified incidents within 72 hours. Major and minor version overlap periods are proposed at 30 and 15 days, respectively, but only under a reasonable efforts standard. The GSA language was proposed, not operative, at the July 24, 2026 research cutoff.

The division of responsibility is equally important. Model developers, system operators, integrators, service providers, and prime contractors do not control the same parts of an AI service. GSA’s proposal attempts to make duties follow that chain rather than disappear between subcontractors.

This is where legal drafting begins to resemble systems engineering. The contract defines which artifacts must exist, who must produce them, when the agency can inspect them, and what happens when the model changes underneath the application.

The chronology draws on records from OMB, GSA, SAM.gov, GAO, and the federal court docket.

HHS provides the closest case to a buyer converting these ideas into a procurement package. Solicitation 7571TE26R00004 contemplated firm-fixed-price pilot awards for up to 1,000 initial users per award, with options to expand to 10,000. The package reportedly covered commercial-release parity, integration, security pathways, telemetry, usage attribution, governance reporting, and transition planning.

The primary attachment set was not directly retrievable during the audit, so its detailed deliverable descriptions should remain attributed to the solicitation package rather than treated as independently verified contract text. More importantly, no public award or resultant contract was found in exact-identifier searches as of July 24, 2026.

HHS therefore proves buyer intent and requirement formation. It does not yet prove that suppliers accepted those duties at an executable price, that HHS accepted the work, or that the agency exercised an expansion option.

Other cases stop earlier. NIST CAISI’s model-hosting and inference notice remained market research and explicitly did not constitute a solicitation. A VA agentic-AI request for information was reported as discontinued after internal review, although the primary cancellation record was not accessible during verification. These cases matter because they show how often federal AI demand can stall before lifecycle clauses become the decisive issue.

An exit path has four layers

A contractually compliant file export is not the same thing as a working provider transition.

The first layer is legal control: ownership or usable rights over inputs, outputs, uploaded files, logs, prompts, custom code, and evaluation records. The second is syntactic portability: export in documented formats with schemas, metadata, and APIs.

The third layer is semantic portability. A replacement model must use the same context, policies, tools, and evaluation criteria with acceptable results. Model behavior, agent frameworks, embeddings, retrieval implementations, safety filters, and proprietary tools may not transfer even when the underlying data does.

The fourth is organizational recovery. The agency needs configuration inventories, test suites, identity mappings, runbooks, security artifacts, replacement environments, and staff capable of executing the move.

This distinction explains why migration can become a services engagement rather than a download button. A new provider may need to rebuild retrieval systems, agents, connectors, approval workflows, and monitoring baselines. The incumbent may need to support dual running, export validation, and knowledge transfer.

GSA’s existing cloud guidance already recommends an actionable exit strategy, non-proprietary data return, and a predefined data-exit price. Yet the cloud record also shows that formal portability rights do not eliminate provider dependence.

Who controls the transition

Model vendors still control the core capability and release cadence. They decide when commercial versions change, which features remain proprietary, and how their APIs, agent tools, safety behavior, and administrative interfaces evolve.

Agencies control a different set of assets: authorization, mission acceptance, records obligations, government data, appropriations, and the authority to exercise options or terminate contracts. Contracting officers determine whether lifecycle duties are enforceable or aspirational.

Between those poles sit the potential control points.

Systems integrators connect models to agency identity, data, cybersecurity, records, and mission workflows. Evaluation platforms maintain test sets, regression histories, drift measures, and release evidence. Data-governance systems preserve telemetry, provenance, retention, and cost attribution. Contract-management teams track notices, deviations, remedies, and closeout. Security providers maintain the authorization boundary as services change.

Control can therefore move away from the model endpoint without necessarily leaving the incumbent’s ecosystem. A provider can support standard data export while retaining leverage through proprietary workflow components. An agency can legally own its information while depending operationally on the integrator that understands how the system was assembled.

Public-company exposure follows this structure but does not establish mechanism-specific revenue. Alphabet supplies Gemini through OneGov. Snowflake and Broadcom have broader OneGov agreements involving data and infrastructure software. Microsoft occupies cloud, identity, productivity, and model-distribution layers. Accenture has federal data and AI integration exposure. None of those relationships proves that federal exit or observability duties are already producing material incremental revenue.

Value may move beyond the model license

If this commercialization path advances, the spend sequence is likely to look less like a conventional software subscription and more like a managed operating stack:

model entitlement → integration and authorization → recurring evidence operations → event-driven transition work.

The first migration is from headline model access into identity, security, data, and workflow integration. A $1 entitlement does not configure roles, connect records systems, establish an authorization boundary, or produce an agency-specific evaluation baseline.

The second is from provider self-assessment into government-owned or third-party evaluation. OMB requires recurring testing, and GSA and NIST announced a partnership to develop shared evaluation methods, benchmarks, guidance, and tools. If agencies use those resources in source selection or model-release approval, accepted test evidence could become a durable control point.

The third is into telemetry and contract administration. Multiple providers, model versions, incidents, users, and cost centers create a continuing need for version registries, usage attribution, approval records, and notification workflows.

The final category is migration and closeout. That work may command high event value but should not be mistaken for recurring revenue until actual transition frequency is visible.

Several cautions follow. Integration, evaluation, governance, and security may all be delivered by the same contractor, so their revenue cannot simply be added together. Transition work may be bundled into subscriptions or general labor. Award ceilings are not booked revenue. Shared government services such as USAi may internalize functions that otherwise would become commercial software sales.

The cloud precedent cuts against the strongest version

The closest historical analogue is federal cloud procurement. Portability and exit planning have been policy objectives since the Cloud First period, but restrictive licensing and provider-specific dependence persisted.

GAO’s 2024 review found that five of six selected civilian agencies reported restrictive practices, including repurchase requirements, conversion charges, provider or architecture restrictions, and one instance in which an agency had to pay to regain ownership of migrated data.

A separate DOD review found that data-egress fees represented less than 1% of known cloud expenditure and were not viewed as the principal source of lock-in. Workforce skills and reliance on provider-specific services could matter more.

The same pattern could repeat in AI. Agencies may obtain machine-readable exports while remaining unable to reproduce agent behavior, retrieval quality, safety performance, or operational knowledge elsewhere. Contract language could improve legal readiness without materially changing incumbent leverage.

There is an even stronger counterargument: exit clauses may not be the pacing gate at all. Authorization, usable data, integration capacity, budget ownership, and workflow demand may explain most delays. GAO’s April 2026 review of 13 AI acquisitions found difficulty accessing technical expertise, understanding AI-related costs, and systematically sharing vetted clauses and lessons.

The thesis therefore holds only in a narrower form. Lifecycle qualification is a real sequential gate for enterprise operation. The evidence does not show that it dominates initial adoption or that it has already created a repeatable market for independent evaluation, observability, or migration suppliers.

The next transition is an administered contract

The maturity state changes when agencies begin using lifecycle duties rather than merely writing them.

The first step is an awarded contract that preserves measurable monitoring, change-control, portability, and transition obligations. The second is accepted operating evidence: quantitative reports, version approvals, regression tests, or export artifacts. The third is a decision made from that evidence—an option exercise, release delay, remediation, rollback, closeout, or provider replacement.

A final GSA clause retaining export, documented APIs, and material-change notification would strengthen the standardization case. A public HHS disposition would show whether a detailed qualification package can convert into an award. Ten or more FY2027 records containing both recurring quantitative monitoring and transition assistance would provide the first evidence of diffusion.

The strongest proof would be a completed provider transition with a documented reason, elapsed time, accepted export, validation result, and cost. Until then, federal AI procurement is beginning to buy the exit path, but it has not shown that the exit works.

What to watch

  • By April 24, 2027, does GSA retain export, APIs, and change notification in formal text?
  • By July 24, 2027, does HHS publish an award, cancellation, withdrawal, or replacement procurement?
  • On September 30, 2027, are there at least 10 qualifying FY2027 federal AI records?
  • By January 24, 2028, does one federal AI award separately price transition or closeout work?
  • By January 24, 2029, is a completed production rollback or provider transition publicly documented?
