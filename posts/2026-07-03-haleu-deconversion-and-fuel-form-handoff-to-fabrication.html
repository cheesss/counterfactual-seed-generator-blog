HALEU’s next problem is turning enriched uranium into accepted fuel

The most visible constraint in advanced nuclear fuel is the supply of high-assay low-enriched uranium, or HALEU. The United States has responded with large enrichment contracts, new cascades and federal ownership of early production.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-03 · 14 min read

Enrichment is only the first conversion

But enriched uranium hexafluoride is not reactor fuel.

A sodium fast reactor may require uranium metal. A high-temperature gas reactor needs coated fuel particles assembled into graphite pebbles. Other designs may require oxide powders or, eventually, salt-compatible chemistry. Between an enrichment cascade and a reactor core sits a second industrial sequence:

HALEU UF6 → metal or oxide → approved package → fabricator acceptance → qualified fuel → reactor delivery

The important word is accepted. A chemically correct product can still fail because its form, impurity profile, packaging, material-accounting record or process history does not match a fabricator’s licensed safety basis and proprietary specifications.

That makes HALEU deconversion a possible late-2020s validation gate. It does not make it the dominant bottleneck today. The gate becomes binding only when three things converge: multi-tonne enriched supply, a selected fuel form and a reactor project with a firm delivery schedule.

DOE has built a bidder pool, not a factory

DOE selected six companies to compete for HALEU deconversion work: Nuclear Fuel Services, American Centrifuge Operating, Framatome, Global Nuclear Fuel-Americas, Orano Federal Services and Westinghouse Government Services.

The contracts can run for as long as ten years and carry an aggregate ceiling of up to $800 million. Each award has a $2 million minimum. Those figures establish procurement eligibility and programme scale. They do not identify which company will receive material, where processing will occur or when a plant will operate. (DOE deconversion programme)

DOE’s November 2023 solicitation shows what a real task order could require. Its sample scope contemplated receipt of six metric tonnes of uranium per year as HALEU UF6, with three tonnes converted into uranium metal and three into sinterable uranium dioxide powder.

The contractor would also need to package and store the material, meet applicable ASTM specifications, maintain DOE ownership and operate a facility licensed to receive the UF6 and load the final product for transport.

The sample schedule assumed about 18 months for environmental and licensing preparation, 30 months for facility modification after approvals and a further 12-month operating phase. That is a planning template, not a current construction programme. (DOE sample task order)

The distinction matters because contract language often sounds more complete than the physical system. “Selected,” “awarded” and “licensed” each describe a real milestone. In this supply chain, each milestone usually opens another gate.

The upstream buildout is real—and still slow

Centrus provides the clearest example of what DOE-backed fuel-cycle development can accomplish.

DOE awarded the company its original HALEU demonstration contract in 2022. Centrus began enrichment in 2023 and reached 900 kilograms by June 2025 at the American Centrifuge Plant in Piketon, Ohio. That sequence demonstrates domestic production at enrichment levels approaching 19.75% uranium-235.

It does not demonstrate commercial fuel supply. The material remains HALEU UF6 owned by DOE.

The next Centrus phase is much larger. A June 30, 2026 agreement provides $900 million in milestone-based payments to establish additional enrichment capacity and deliver one metric tonne of HALEU UF6 by March 2032. DOE can later exercise options for two additional five-tonne production periods.

The contract is strategically significant, but its delivery date also limits the near-term bottleneck claim. A one-tonne obligation nearly six years after contract award means that enrichment can remain the upstream pacing item while deconversion facilities are being designed or licensed.

General Matter received a separate $900 million HALEU-enrichment award. Orano Federal Services received another $900 million task order, but that award is for low-enriched uranium rather than HALEU. The three awards should not be treated as one $2.7 billion HALEU programme. (DOE enrichment awards)

The downstream plants are further along than the deconversion routes

TRISO-X illustrates the mirror image of Centrus.

In February 2026, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission issued a 40-year Part 70 special-nuclear-material licence for the TX-1 and TX-2 facilities in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. That is the most important completed fabrication-side milestone in the current HALEU chain.

TX-1 is designed to produce about 700,000 fuel pebbles per year, according to DOE. Sargent & Lundy describes an initial design capacity of five metric tonnes uranium per year and possible expansion to 16 tonnes in the early 2030s. Those MTU figures are contractor-stated design values, not demonstrated output.

TRISO-X still must complete construction, pass a final NRC inspection, receive special nuclear material, qualify its process and deliver accepted fuel. DOE has said initial fabrication is expected in early 2028. (DOE TRISO-X update)

That creates a clear sequencing question: will accepted oxide or kernel feed be available when TX-1 is ready to operate, or will fabrication readiness arrive before a deconversion route is assigned?

The metallic-fuel chain is less mature. Framatome and TerraPower produced uranium metal pucks at Framatome’s Richland, Washington, facility in 2025. The demonstration used depleted uranium. The companies said the process could be applied at higher enrichment, but the public record does not yet show licensed commercial production of near-19.75% HALEU metal for TerraPower’s Natrium reactor. (Framatome–TerraPower metallisation)

TerraPower’s Natrium project received its construction permit on March 9, 2026. The reactor is designed for 345 megawatts electric output, with thermal storage supporting a temporary 500-megawatt boost. Construction authorization advances the customer side of the chain, but it does not establish the supplier of the first HALEU metal, the qualified fabrication route or the operating-licence schedule.

Dow and X-energy’s Long Mott project in Seadrift, Texas, would use four Xe-100 modules with a combined output of 320 megawatts electric. NRC completed its environmental assessment and finding of no significant impact on May 18, 2026. The final construction-permit decision remains pending.

These projects create credible future demand, but neither has publicly closed the full path from DOE-owned UF6 to accepted first-core fuel. Fuel only becomes the binding constraint if the reactor programmes maintain enough schedule pressure to need it.

Transportation can become the next gate

Moving HALEU is not equivalent to moving ordinary commercial reactor fuel.

The existing U.S. transport system was built mainly around uranium enriched to five percent or less. DOE says licensed HALEU options remain limited and that some existing packages could move only small quantities at higher cost and with more containers.

In 2025, DOE awarded $11 million to five companies to develop new or modified transportation packages: NAC International, Westinghouse, Container Technologies Industries, American Centrifuge Operating and Paragon D&E. The projects have two- to three-year performance periods. (DOE package awards)

This investment weakens the claim that transport is an ignored future problem. Relief is already underway.

It does not prove that the problem is solved. Public records do not yet disclose certified payloads, fleet counts, package availability by material form, lease rates or shipment turnaround times. A certificate also does not create a fleet automatically.

As deconversion comes online, value could move into package ownership, certification, maintenance, secure storage and shipment coordination. But the market may remain small and project-specific if only a few first-wave reactors proceed.

The value may stay with integrated fuel vendors

The deconverter will not necessarily control the most valuable part of the chain.

DOE owns much of the early material and controls allocation. NRC controls licences, inspections and package certificates. ASTM standards provide baseline material requirements. Reactor developers and fuel fabricators control proprietary acceptance specifications and qualification data.

That division of authority creates several potential control points.

Centrus controls domestic enrichment know-how and is eligible to compete for deconversion work. Nuclear Fuel Services, Framatome, Global Nuclear Fuel-Americas, Orano and Westinghouse already operate inside regulated nuclear-fuel ecosystems. Their existing security systems, quality programmes and customer relationships can be more important than the underlying chemical reaction.

TRISO-X is vertically integrated with X-energy’s reactor platform. If it controls feedstock acceptance, particle coating, pebble manufacture and delivery to Xe-100 customers, it can retain the workflow even if enriched uranium becomes widely available.

Framatome occupies a similar position in the metallic-fuel chain through its existing Richland facility and work with TerraPower.

Public-company exposure therefore exists at several nodes—Centrus, BWX Technologies through Nuclear Fuel Services, GE Vernova through Global Nuclear Fuel-Americas, and Dow as the Long Mott industrial customer. That is exposure to programme activity, licensing and customer demand, not evidence that any one company captures the eventual margin.

DOE can also compress scarcity rents. Competitive task orders, government ownership and multi-supplier development reduce the chance that the first deconverter gains permanent pricing power.

The strongest counterargument is that no bottleneck appears

The central thesis can fail without any technical breakthrough.

Enrichment, deconversion, transport and fabrication may mature in parallel while reactor projects move more slowly. Under that scenario, every part of the chain remains necessary, but no single node becomes scarce enough to delay deployment.

That possibility is credible.

Centrus and General Matter are building enrichment capacity. Six established firms can compete for deconversion work. TRISO-X already holds its fabrication licence. Five package projects are funded. Natrium and Long Mott still face downstream licensing, construction and procurement milestones.

The feared gate may therefore remain downstream of the actual constraint.

There is also a substitution route. Framatome has pursued a licence amendment for TRISO fuel fabrication below ten percent uranium-235 at Richland. A lower-enrichment design could reduce some Category II security and supply-chain burdens, although it may require different reactor physics, larger fuel inventories or other economic trade-offs.

The closest historical base rate is mixed. Centrus moved from a DOE contract in 2022 to initial enrichment in 2023 and 900 kilograms by June 2025, showing that government-backed nuclear-fuel infrastructure can progress within several years. TRISO development took longer: pilot work began around 2016, the commercial Part 70 licence arrived in 2026 and initial production is expected in 2028.

What is similar now is the dependence on federal support, licensing and staged qualification. What is different is the absence of a broad operating reactor fleet. HALEU demand remains tied to a small number of first-wave projects, so reactor delay can leave fuel-cycle capacity underused.

What to watch

  • 2027-12-31: DOE names a deconversion awardee, site and metal-or-oxide task order.
  • 2028-12-31: TRISO-X completes its final NRC inspection before HALEU receipt.
  • 2029-12-31: An NRC-certified DOE-funded HALEU package enters service.
  • 2029-12-31: A named fabricator accepts an assay-backed HALEU metal or oxide lot.
  • 2030-12-31: A public record links deconverter, package, fabricator and reactor customer.
  • 2031-12-31: A reactor customer discloses binding quantity, fuel form and delivery timing.

The conclusion is narrower than a shortage call

The United States has moved beyond policy discussion. It has demonstration-scale enriched material, billion-dollar enrichment awards, a licensed TRISO facility under construction, funded package development and named reactor projects.

What it does not yet have is a public, repeatable chain connecting those elements.

The most defensible claim is therefore sequential. As enrichment and reactor licensing advance, developers may encounter a fuel-form validation gate: the need to convert UF6 into the exact material, package and evidence record that a fabricator can accept.

That gate may become important between 2027 and 2030. It may also disappear into ordinary commissioning if suppliers, regulators and reactor developers progress in parallel.

The settlement event is straightforward. The thesis strengthens when a multi-tonne HALEU batch moves through deconversion, approved transport and fabricator acceptance for a named reactor—and one of those steps delays the project. It weakens when several suppliers complete the same handoff at ordinary lead times before customers need the fuel.

Until then, the $800 million deconversion programme represents strategic optionality, not proven capacity; the licensed fabricator represents readiness, not output; and enriched UF6 represents inventory, not a reactor core.
