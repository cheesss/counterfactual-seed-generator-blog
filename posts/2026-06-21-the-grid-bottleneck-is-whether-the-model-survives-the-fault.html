The grid's next bottleneck may be whether the model survives the fault

Hero image: ASCOM Prefeitura de Votuporanga / Wikimedia Commons_(22963311836).jpg) (CC BY 2.0_(22963311836).jpg)).

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-21 · Updated 2026-06-28 · 10 min read

In brief

  • 2,060 GW sat in U.S. interconnection queues at end-2025.
  • Blue Cut cut nearly 1,200 MW after a cleared transmission fault.
  • Four Western bulk-power IBR disturbances were logged in March-June 2024.
  • FERC approved new IBR modeling standards in February 2026.
  • IEEE 2800 supplies a baseline, not plant-specific proof.

A solar plant can clear land, order equipment, and survive the interconnection queue, then still face a different question when the planner studies the fault. Not whether the site exists, but whether the submitted model reproduces what the plant will actually do when voltage collapses, frequency swings, or protection clears a nearby line.

Physical bottlenecks still dominate much of the U.S. buildout. Public records also point to a second gate forming around proof: disturbance data, plant-specific model packages, and validation work that convince a planner the simulated plant and the real plant behave the same way. That is not yet a settled national bottleneck. It is an emerging one, and the evidence is strongest where inverter-based resources already dominate the queue.

The approval object has changed

Transmission capacity, transformers, queue reform, and siting remain the visible choke points. Order No. 901 did not replace them. It changed what regulators expected once an inverter-based project reached study and operations: NERC had to address data sharing, model validation, planning and operational studies, and performance requirements for inverter-based resources. (FERC Order No. 901)

The approval step came later. In February 2026, FERC approved standards including MOD-032-2, MOD-033-3, and MOD-026-2, moving IBR data collection, dynamic models, disturbance monitoring, verification, and validation deeper into the formal reliability rulebook. (FERC 2026 IBR modeling approval)

IEEE 2800 supplies a common technical baseline for ride-through, voltage and frequency response, dynamic support, and power quality. It does not erase the local modeling burden. It gives planners firmer grounds for asking whether a plant-specific package is credible. (IEEE 2800 standard summary)

The scarce layer is the evidence package

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory counted more than 2,060 GW of generation and storage actively seeking connection at the end of 2025. Solar and storage dominate that pipeline. The queue composition chart uses the last full technology breakout, end-2024, while the 2,060 GW headline refers to the end-2025 total; it is there to show mix, not restate the same-year total. (LBNL interconnection queues)

Queue volume overstates real buildout because many requests withdraw before operation, so the live burden still has to be tested against late-stage projects and operating plants.

EIA expected about 63 GW of new U.S. utility-scale capacity in 2025, mostly solar and battery storage. That matters because the marginal project joining the bulk power system increasingly connects through power electronics rather than a spinning machine. (EIA 2025 utility-scale additions)

The hidden constraint sits between equipment delivery and permission to rely on the equipment. An inverter OEM controls part of the behavioral truth. A plant controller adds site-specific settings. A consultant or internal engineering team turns those inputs into RMS and EMT models. A lab may then test control behavior under fault conditions, and a planner decides whether the resulting evidence package is credible enough for interconnection and operations.

The labor stack already looks specialized. DOE's i2X Transmission Interconnection Roadmap says EMT modeling requires new skill sets, training, and engineering capacity, while NERC's 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment says inverter-based resources account for most new bulk-power generation in development. Vestas has publicly hired for grid and interconnection roles covering renewable integration, grid-code compliance, PSCAD, PSS/E, PowerFactory, EMT simulation, ride-through testing, and EMT-to-RMS validation. (DOE i2X Transmission Interconnection Roadmap) (NERC 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment) (Vestas grid and interconnection role)

The commercial tool layer is also visible. DNV offers renewable-power equipment model development and validation for interconnection. Siemens, RTDS, and PSCAD market the software and simulation layers used to build or test that package. (DNV renewable model development) (Siemens PSS software) (RTDS PSCAD-RTDS co-simulation) (PSCAD inverter model knowledge base)

Not every project will need the same depth of proof, and public evidence still does not show how large or durable the spend pool will be. That uncertainty matters. The evidence is strong on the existence of a validation layer, weaker on whether it becomes a uniform national chokepoint or a heavier burden concentrated in high-IBR regions.

Disturbance reports turned software into a reliability problem

The named events matter because they keep this from becoming an abstract software story. Over several years, public disturbance reports kept returning to the same theme: what looked like an electrical event on the grid often turned into a problem of control behavior, model fidelity, and pre-interconnection study quality.

The public case record runs through NERC's Blue Cut disturbance report, Canyon 2 disturbance report, 2018 Southern California disturbance report, Odessa disturbance report, and 2024 Western Interconnection disturbance report.

That sequence does not prove model validation dominates every other bottleneck. It does show why operators no longer ask only whether a project can inject power under normal conditions. They also ask whether the project's models, settings, and disturbance evidence are good enough to trust through the next fault.

Who controls the constraint

The decisive feature of this gate is that the truth about plant behavior is split across parties who do not answer to one another. The inverter OEM holds proprietary control logic, often as a black-box model. A plant controller vendor adds site-specific settings. Consultants, internal engineering teams, and labs translate those inputs into study-ready models and validation reports. Disturbance recorders and PMUs supply the measured events that those reports have to match.

CAISO's public New Resource Implementation and Interconnection Request Study pages are a simple example of where the evidence package lands. Technical data, study revisions, and implementation steps sit between a finished facility and permission to operate. The same basic handoff exists elsewhere even if the exact tariff language and regional rigor differ. (CAISO new resource implementation) (CAISO interconnection request study)

That is why the control point is easy to miss. The physical asset sits in the field, but the approval object is distributed across OEM model support, study software, validation labor, disturbance data, and planner acceptance. Public materials are strong on the existence of those handoffs and weaker on who captures durable margin from them. That is a reason to narrow the claim, not to ignore the layer.

The counterargument is that this may stay regional or routine

The counterevidence is serious. Interconnection delays may continue to come mostly from network upgrades, deposits, siting, tariffs, or equipment lead times. Generic or common EMT models may become acceptable in more studies. Utilities, RTOs, and OEMs may internalize enough skill that outside consultants and labs never gain much pricing power.

There is also a timing question. Standards approval does not automatically produce a national commercial chokepoint. Regional manuals, audit practice, exemptions, and planner behavior determine whether model validation becomes a hard gate or remains one more compliance layer inside a much larger buildout process.

The strongest public version of the thesis is therefore conditional. In high-IBR regions, repeated disturbances and newer standards are pushing the system toward a thicker evidence burden. What remains unresolved is how binding that burden becomes, how quickly it spreads, and whether the economics sit with OEM support, software licenses, outside engineering firms, or internal utility teams.

What to watch

The signals that would separate an emerging gate from routine paperwork are observable.

  • CAISO and peer manuals adding PSCAD or EMT artifacts, RMS-to-EMT reconciliation, ride-through plots, or HIL evidence. (CAISO new resource implementation) (CAISO interconnection request study)
  • NERC audit or enforcement activity tied to MOD-026-2, MOD-033-3, MOD-032-2, or related model-validation obligations after FERC's February 2026 approval. (FERC 2026 IBR modeling approval) (NERC standards under development)
  • Specialist hiring for PSCAD, EMT, RTDS, and grid-code work, the sort of role Vestas already advertises. (Vestas grid and interconnection role)
  • More public evidence that operating plants must update settings, models, and validation packages after disturbances rather than only at initial interconnection. (2024 Western Interconnection disturbance report)
  • Clear disclosures that common model libraries are either becoming acceptable across planners or, instead, failing to close the model-versus-reality gap. (NERC EMT modeling guideline)

The most defensible public claim is not that validated models have already displaced towers and transformers as the grid's main constraint. It is that the approval object has changed. As solar, wind, and storage dominate new additions, the system increasingly asks for proof of behavior, not just delivery of hardware.

If that proof package becomes standardized, this story collapses back into routine compliance. If disturbances keep revealing gaps between submitted models and real plant behavior, the quiet infrastructure of validation could become one of the next gates in grid buildout.
