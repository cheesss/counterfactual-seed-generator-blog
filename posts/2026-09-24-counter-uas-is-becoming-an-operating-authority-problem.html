Counter-UAS is becoming an operating-authority problem

Counter-drone hardware can be bought, tested and fielded quickly. Domestic deployment is slower because an active system must be connected to a lawful operator, a site plan, aviation and spectrum coordination, trained personnel, evidence handling and a continuing audit trail. The 2025-26 U.S. reforms widened authority, but they also made the operating stack visible.

A deployed system needs more than a sensor and an effector

The old counter-UAS debate treated legal authority as a binary condition: either a site could disrupt or disable a drone, or it could not. That is no longer the whole story. The SAFER SKIES provisions enacted in the FY2026 NDAA extended conditional authority to qualified state, local, Tribal and territorial law-enforcement and correctional agencies. Yet the July 2026 DOJ/DHS rule turned that authority into a regulated workflow rather than a broadly available permission to operate.

An agency must establish who holds authority, certify personnel, select an authorized system, build an operations plan, arrange site access, coordinate in advance, complete FAA and FCC steps where relevant, notify air-traffic coordination points during an activation, preserve the right records and report afterwards. Contractors can train and maintain systems, but the rule does not permit them to exercise mitigation authority or to make the threat decision.

That distinction matters because a critical-infrastructure owner can purchase hardware without acquiring the legal and institutional capacity to use it. The constraint has moved from an absence of authority toward the throughput of a permissioned operating system.

The airspace and spectrum layers are part of the product

For a normal SLTT mitigation operation, the new framework anticipates federal notification 30 calendar days in advance. A submission routes to the FBI and DHS for deconfliction, the FAA for aviation safety and the FCC for spectrum issues. RF-emitting systems require spectrum authority; the FAA receives coordinates, duration and airspace information; activation normally triggers real-time FAA notification within five minutes.

This is not paperwork after deployment. It determines what can be offered as a repeatable service, at which sites and on what timetable. Airport operators face a similarly integrated process: FAA guidance links a proposed detection or mitigation system to aeronautical studies, airport certification materials, ATC, TSA, airport operations and law enforcement.

Training and records are becoming capacity constraints

The FBI's National Counter-UAS Training Center in Huntsville is the sole national certifying authority for SLTT mitigation under the 2026 framework. In April, FBI testimony put the certified officer count at 45, while the rule's planning case contemplated roughly 1,500 detection-tier and 150 mitigation-tier agencies in its first two years. Whether those numbers converge is a practical measure of deployable capacity.

The operating burden continues after an incident. Mitigation-related activity generally requires a report within 48 hours; control communications ordinarily face a 180-day retention ceiling unless a specified exception applies; and agencies file semiannual summaries. DOJ and DHS put their central annual information-burden estimate at roughly 72,000 hours. That creates demand for operating procedures, data controls, training, documentation and command structures alongside the physical system.

Hardware supply does not settle the domestic deployment question

There is evidence that production and procurement can move much faster than permissioning. The Army's January 2024 award to RTX covered 600 Coyote 2C interceptors for $75 million and was completed through rapid acquisition. The Marine Corps' 2025 Anduril installation-C-UAS contract carried a $642.21 million ceiling. GAO has reported six formal Army C-sUAS acquisition programs in addition to prototypes and fielded systems.

The contrast is instructive: inventory and contract vehicles can grow without making an airport, prison, port or private critical-infrastructure site an immediately lawful active-mitigation location.

The World Cup was a positive control, not proof of frictionlessness

The 2026 FIFA World Cup showed that the stack can operate at scale when it is deliberately assembled. FBI-led task forces, NCUTC-trained officers, FAA restrictions and interagency command arrangements supported more than 700 unauthorized-drone seizures reported nationwide after the tournament. That was a major operating success, but it was also an exceptional mobilization with a defined event, lead agencies and advance preparation.

The question for ordinary infrastructure is whether that model becomes repeatable. Persistent protection of fixed critical-infrastructure or correctional sites can receive windows of up to 365 days, rather than indefinite authorization. The framework also requires reporting of protection requests that certified public agencies could not support, leaving a useful signal about the gap between demand and qualified operating capacity.

What could make this interpretation wrong

Permissioning is not the only bottleneck. Difficult targets can still defeat present systems: low-observable, autonomous, fiber-controlled and swarm drones continue to require layered technical defenses. Passive detection can often proceed without the full mitigation stack. And the SAFER SKIES legislation itself is evidence that the authority gap is not fixed; federal technology lists and FAA categorical determinations could reduce site-by-site friction.

The narrower claim is that governance now shapes where existing hardware can become durable domestic defensive capacity. Watch NCUTC certification throughput, the Authorized Systems List, FAA categorical determinations, FCC authorization speed, unsupported infrastructure requests and safety or privacy incidents. Those measures will show whether the new authority converts into a repeatable operating capability before the 2031 statutory sunsets.
