The grid buildout is turning into a factory-calendar problem

Europe and the UK have announced enough offshore links and interconnectors to make the next decade of grid construction look like a civil-engineering boom. But the decisive scheduling problem is increasingly industrial: a project needs a compatible sequence of 525 kV cable, converter valves, transformer bays, test capacity, specialist engineers, vessels and commissioning windows. A permit creates the right to build. It does not reserve the machinery needed to do it.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-09-24 · 4 min read

In brief

The unit that matters is no longer the announced gigawatt

Transmission plans are often expressed in gigawatts. That is an intuitive unit for policy, but a weak one for delivery. A two-gigawatt HVDC link is not a generic order for wire. It is a tightly coupled system: converter stations need compatible valves and custom transformers; long-distance cable needs qualified insulation, joints and installation capacity; and both must arrive at the same moment as platforms, civil works, AC substations and outage windows.

The IEA's 2025 supply-chain review puts useful boundaries around that problem. Ordinary high-voltage cable was taking roughly two to three years to procure, large transformers up to four years, and specialised DC cable more than five. Those ranges are not a forecast that every project will fail. They do explain why a large programme cannot be converted directly from an approval calendar into an energisation calendar.

The buyers are already behaving as if capacity is infrastructure

The clearest evidence is in how transmission owners are buying. TenneT's North Sea 2 GW programme bundled many standardised projects into long framework agreements: Hitachi Energy and Petrofac for six systems, GE-led consortia for five, and Siemens Energy and Dragados for three. The point of a framework is not just price. It gives suppliers enough visibility to assign factory lines, engineering teams and fabrication capacity years ahead.

Britain has moved in the same direction. Ofgem introduced an Advanced Procurement Mechanism in 2025 so owners can reserve critical supply-chain capacity before the usual investment-approval sequence is complete. National Grid then put in place an HVDC framework spanning roughly GBP21.3 billion of cable and GBP24.6 billion of converter systems. SSEN had already signed capacity-reservation agreements with NKT and Hitachi Energy for Scottish links planned for 2030.

That reverses the older logic of transmission delivery. Under abundant supply, a developer could secure approval and then shop for equipment. Under tight supply, procurement and permission must overlap. The factory calendar becomes part of the project definition.

One programme can change another project's timetable

This is not an abstract concern. In its review of Eastern Green Link 1, Ofgem concluded that exceptional contemporaneous HVDC procurement pressure, including TenneT's programme, materially contributed to the project's tender delay. The regulator did not blame the entire delay on suppliers; it also found weaknesses in the original schedule. That distinction matters.

The useful conclusion is narrower. When many owners pursue similar 525 kV systems at once, they are competing for a finite industrial calendar. A project can be well designed, financed and politically supported yet still face a delayed equipment sequence because another buyer reserved the same scarce capability earlier.

New factories help, but only after the next bottleneck

Suppliers are responding at scale. NKT committed about EUR1 billion to expand Karlskrona and add a cable-laying vessel, with new assets expected from 2027. Nexans expanded its Halden facility with another extrusion tower for 525 kV HVDC work. GE Vernova is expanding Stafford to target a doubling of HVDC valve manufacturing capacity. Hitachi Energy has a USD1.5 billion transformer-capacity programme through 2027, while Siemens Energy is expanding Nuremberg with a stated target of roughly 50% more capacity by 2028.

Those investments are encouraging, but timing is the point. Much of the relief arrives in 2027 and 2028, while today's projects are already trying to book production, testing and installation. Capacity additions also do not instantly make supply interchangeable. A new line must make an approved product, complete qualification and fit the technical requirements of the specific network operator.

Qualification is what turns a factory into usable supply

The nominal number of cable factories can overstate the practical supplier pool. TenneT's 525 kV prequalification programme involved long-duration electrical, mechanical, joint and accessory testing across eight suppliers. ENTSO-E has since consulted on harmonising qualification requirements, an acknowledgement that differing technical standards can repeatedly consume approval work.

This is why standardisation matters. TenneT's move toward repeatable 2 GW connections reduces the number of unique engineering decisions and makes it easier for suppliers to plan. It does not abolish scarcity, but it makes capacity more usable across a programme.

What could prove this story wrong

Manufacturing is not the master explanation for every delayed line. The IEA still identifies permitting as the primary cause of transmission delay in many advanced economies. Land rights, offshore consents, onshore substations, civil works and commissioning can each become the long pole in a project that already holds its equipment slot.

The practical question is therefore not whether HVDC has a single bottleneck. It is whether the pieces of its industrial sequence are becoming more credible at the same rate as policy ambition. The signs to watch are quoted DC-cable lead times, the commissioning of new cable and transformer capacity, common 525 kV qualification rules, vessel availability and whether framework reservations turn into completed, energised links. Until then, the relevant unit of grid expansion is not GW approved. It is GW with a qualified factory slot and a credible path to commissioning.
