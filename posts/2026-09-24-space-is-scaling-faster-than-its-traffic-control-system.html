Orbital Traffic Coordination: The Control Plane Catching Up to Launch Scale

A satellite needs more than a place in orbit. It needs a reliable account of where other objects will be, whether they can move, and what their operators intend to do. Launch supplies the asset; coordination helps make continued operation possible.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-09-24 · 13 min read

Orbital Traffic Needs Compatible Decisions

Open source visual from https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/photo-gallery/

That distinction matters as deployment expands. ESA's 2026 environment report announcement, covering data through end-2025, records more than 300 launches and over 4,000 new payloads in that year. Those totals explain the pressure on orbital operations. They do not measure whether coordination capacity is falling behind launch capacity.

The constraint to examine is the ability to turn uncertain predictions into compatible actions. More satellites increase the work of identification, prediction and maneuver assessment, but automation can also increase the capacity to do that work. Calling coordination the dominant new bottleneck requires evidence of delays or failures attributable to it, not deployment growth alone.

The recurring pattern is standards and interoperability: independent fleets can share an operating environment only if their data and decisions remain intelligible to one another. That is already creating purchases of software, observations and integration services, even without proof of an aggregate traffic ceiling.

A Warning Is Not A Maneuver

A conjunction message identifies a predicted close approach. Operators must still assess the uncertainty, decide whether action is justified and screen the proposed trajectory for newly introduced risks. NASA's operating requirements explicitly distinguish those steps and require predictions incorporating planned maneuvers and their execution uncertainty. The requirements govern NASA's activities; they are not international operating law.

This distinction separates detection from useful knowledge. An observation does not necessarily identify the correct object. A nominal trajectory does not establish realistic uncertainty. A warning does not tell two operators which one should act.

The consequences can extend beyond the spacecraft involved. On 10 February 2009, active Iridium 33 and derelict Cosmos 2251 collided at approximately 790 kilometres altitude and 11.6 kilometres per second. NASA's analysis documents the resulting debris clouds and their longer-term implications. The event establishes a destructive externality, not a numerical limit on today's orbital traffic.

Aeolus provides a different lesson. On 2 September 2019, ESA maneuvered the spacecraft after the estimated collision probability with Starlink 44 reached about one in 1,000. ESA's account describes email contact establishing that Starlink would hold its trajectory, allowing conflict-free avoidance. ESA did not assign fault. Its description of manual coordination belongs to that historical episode, not automatically to current operations.

Automation Has Already Provided Relief

Starlink's live collision-avoidance description reports an autonomous system typically executing more than 1,000 avoidance maneuvers daily. It lists approximately hourly updates for its preferred trajectory feeds, compared with eight-hour updates for legacy feeds. Daily station-keeping and roughly hourly orbit-transfer burns help explain why predictions need updating.

These are operator disclosures, not independent certification. Precautionary maneuvers are not a count of otherwise-certain collisions. Nevertheless, the system is substantial counterevidence to a simple human-dispatch ceiling: routine avoidance need not require a separate negotiation for every event.

Responsibility can also be allocated before an encounter. Under the NASA-SpaceX agreement announced in March 2021, Starlink takes avoidance action while NASA normally holds its trajectory unless otherwise informed. That bilateral arrangement reduces the danger of incompatible responses. It does not establish universal right of way.

Nor is the agreement a customer sale. Its financial provisions specify that each party funds its participation and no money changes hands under the agreement. Operational cooperation and commercial revenue are different milestones.

The Public Service Is Growing, But Remains A Pilot

NOAA's Traffic Coordination System for Space, or TraCSS, has delivered capabilities rather than merely announced them. Program Increment 1.2, implemented on 20 May 2025, enabled beta users to submit thousands of ephemerides together and receive on-demand results in a reported two to five minutes. That is a provider-reported capability, not an audited latency guarantee under every operating condition.

The overview available on 10 October 2026, still labelled August 2026, lists 82 pilot organizations representing more than 11,370 satellites and ten national-government accounts. Participation is substantial, but it is neither a paying-customer count nor a measure of uninterrupted coverage.

The production distinction remains material. NOAA's FAQ says known production risks are being retired before an updated integrated schedule is published. It also describes legacy military services initially running in parallel. The service site's notice identifies a pilot with evaluation-only products and schedules a roughly 24-hour maintenance interruption for 13-14 October. As of 10 October, that interruption is prospective.

Service continuity therefore deserves scrutiny. But a maintenance plan and unfinished transition do not establish that satellite numbers have overwhelmed screening calculations. Availability, input quality and acceptance are separate questions from processing throughput.

More Observations Do Not Always Improve A Decision

The 2024 Consolidated Pathfinder paper offers a useful mixed result. Additional tracking produced little uncertainty improvement for selected conjunction objects. Many were already adequately tracked; some difficult objects remained difficult for the participating sensors. Certain underserved objects that the sensors could acquire benefited more.

The result is not that commercial tracking fails. It is that the value of another observation depends on the existing information and the object being observed.

Service outputs can differ for similar reasons. NOAA's 7 October 2026 account of the EU SST-TraCSS study reports demonstrated operational information exchange, yet approximately 45 times as many published conjunction events from TraCSS. Publication thresholds and trajectory inputs explain substantial differences; outputs converged more closely when the systems used similar operator data.

Neither message volume nor event overlap is a safety ranking. The practical question is whether an operator receives a consistent, actionable assessment.

Standards help at different layers. The CCSDS Conjunction Data Message standard, first published in 2013, specifies message content and format, not a common prediction algorithm or guaranteed accuracy. ISO 23705:2026, published in June, covers collision assessment, data and avoidance workflows. Publication alone does not establish incorporation into binding licences.

Existing Products, Identifiable Buyers

Commercial collision avoidance predates the current public buildout. LeoLabs introduced its service in May 2020, combining radar-backed information with screening and analytics.

In February 2022, the company announced a multi-year agreement with OneWeb. The announcement names OneWeb as a customer and describes integration through APIs and web interfaces. It establishes a commercial relationship for an existing product family, not a newly proposed 2026 branch. It does not disclose the price, payment settlement or subsequent renewal.

Public procurement buys several other components. In March 2024, NOAA awarded Parsons a USD15.5 million one-year base contract for integration and cloud management. Parsons' responsibilities included the software backbone, third-party component integration, cybersecurity and administration.

NOAA's November 2024 Slingshot award bought a presentation layer for USD5.3 million over 12 months, with extension options. Its existing Beacon product supplied the starting framework; Slingshot would work with the system integrator. Reusing a commercial platform is different from having accepted a complete production service.

The June 2025 COLA Gap awards totalled USD10.1 million for work on uncertain identification and trajectories immediately after launch. The package separated data providers, GMV's orbit determination and independent quality monitors. These were contracts to evaluate capabilities for future integration, not proof that the gap had closed.

Award values establish commitments and purchased objects. They are not interchangeable with recognized supplier revenue, cash received or repeat operational orders.

Downstream demand is tangible too. Eutelsat reported EUR297 million in LEO revenue for the year ended 30 June 2026. That is connectivity revenue, not traffic-management revenue. It demonstrates an operating business that safety services support without establishing those suppliers' profitability.

Who Controls The Constraint?

Responsibility does not follow the money automatically. Sensor vendors supply observations; integrators connect systems; public providers distribute assessments; spacecraft operators retain flight decisions. EU SST's service explanation explicitly leaves mitigation decisions with operators and describes services supplied free of charge.

The division creates a commercial tension. Governments can purchase supplier capabilities while offering basic information without direct user fees. NOAA describes TraCSS on that basis too. Another undifferentiated warning may consequently be harder to sell; independent coverage, validated uncertainty, dependable delivery and integration could be more defensible products. That is an economic inference, not a measured industry-wide margin trend.

Starlink's Stargaze documentation describes optical observations from star trackers already aboard its fleet. It acknowledges incomplete coverage of public radar catalogues and says advance operator trajectories remain preferable. A subsequent web-interface announcement says Stargaze had begun releasing conjunction messages. That is operator-reported delivery, not independent production acceptance or complete coverage.

A fleet can thus be both a consumer and a source of safety information. Open formats could expand suppliers' reach while lowering switching costs; dependable performance histories could still sustain customer relationships.

Aviation offers an analogy for standardized responsibilities, not a single global dispatcher. ICAO assigns air traffic service responsibilities to states. Space's demonstrated bilateral and inter-centre arrangements show that compatibility can improve without first creating universal positive control.

Inspection Is Delivered; Routine Removal Is Not

A failed satellite cannot share intent or execute an avoidance command. Coordination helps active spacecraft respond to that object, but does not remove it.

JAXA's July 2024 ADRAS-J release documents fly-around imaging of an H-IIA upper stage. Astroscale Japan developed, owned and operated the inspection spacecraft; JAXA specified the required service and supplied technical support. That is realized activity concerning a noncooperative object. It was inspection, not capture or removal.

ClearSpace-1 illustrates the distance between a service purchase and routine capability. ESA's December 2020 announcement described an EUR86 million contract and a 2025 Vespa-adapter removal mission. Its current mission page instead identifies PROBA-1, an OHB-led industrial team and a planned 2029 launch as an in-orbit demonstration.

The change is substantial, but it does not establish cancellation. Nor do these projects establish recurring fleet-scale cleanup throughput.

Removal also has a different payer problem. Tracking can protect a specific customer's asset; removing legacy debris benefits multiple parties. The government-supported cases demonstrate qualification efforts, not a self-sustaining mechanism through which all beneficiaries pay.

Launch, Spectrum And Liability Remain Separate Gates

Amazon Leo supplies a concrete objection to treating launch as a solved input. The FCC's 5 June 2026 order records the operator's case that launch-provider delays and shortages created deployment backlogs. The regulator granted a conditional milestone waiver, with temporary spectrum-priority consequences for satellites not deployed and operational by the July deadline. It was not the blanket two-year extension requested.

That record does not rank constraints across every constellation. It does show why an aggregate launch total cannot settle a particular network's deployment problem.

Spectrum has its own clock. ITU's milestone framework covers specified NGSO bands and services: 10%, 50% and full deployment at two, five and seven years after the relevant bringing-into-use regulatory period. These are frequency-assignment conditions, not a universal countdown from first launch.

Implementation also matters. Some FCC spectrum-sharing changes took effect in July 2026, but the 11 September Bureau notice said compatibility-showing reviews had not begun pending approval. Adoption should not be credited as completed operating relief.

Disposal rules address residence time rather than screening speed. The FCC's implementing text requires disposal within five years for covered spacecraft ending in or passing through LEO below 2,000 kilometres and using uncontrolled reentry. U.S. authorization or market-access scope, transition provisions and waivers matter.

Insurance is jurisdiction-specific too. FAA financial responsibility concerns permitted or licensed launch and reentry. The UK's September policy describes the current EUR60 million standard-mission liability limit and new GBP50 million, GBP20 million and zero tiers for applications submitted on or after 1 December 2026. Liability limits are not insurance premiums. Neither regime establishes insurance as the principal global deployment constraint.

What Would Change The Conclusion?

Three counterfactuals clarify the test.

If screening becomes faster while operators still cannot reconcile trajectories or maneuver responsibility, additional computing capacity will not solve that coordination problem. The valuable improvement would be compatible decisions, not more messages.

If reliable public services and interoperable automation absorb fleet growth without worsening decision delays, coordination remains essential infrastructure without becoming the marginal deployment constraint. Satellite population alone would overstate the private market for generic warnings.

If operators handle active-fleet encounters successfully while uncontrollable objects accumulate, disposal and removal become more important than refining active-to-active negotiation. Better coordination cannot substitute for every sustainability measure.

The useful indicators are therefore production acceptance, delivery reliability, recovery after interruptions, sharing of planned trajectories, resolution of conflicting assessments and procurement progressing from trials to repeat services. Coordination-caused deployment deferrals or worsening decision latency under comparable workloads would support a stronger bottleneck claim.

No defensible universal deadline follows from the present operating records. The industrial change is visible without one: launches add assets, while a distributed network of data, software, standards and agreements enables their continued use. Progress should be judged by compatible action, not the number of warnings produced.
