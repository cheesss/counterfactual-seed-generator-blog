The supervisor bottleneck thesis shrinks to a narrower Oregon question

The intuition was sound. If psychedelic training has to pass through live supervised sessions, classroom seats will not tell you how fast the workforce can grow. The strong version of that thesis predicted a visible queue: didactic completers piling up ahead of practicum, supervisors pulled off client work, and centers losing throughput to training.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-14 · 8 min read

The original thesis predicted a queue the public record does not show

The public record still does not show that queue. Oregon and Colorado publish training-hour requirements, program lists, fee schedules, and broad activity counts. They do not publish active supervisor hours, practicum lead times, center-level teaching shifts, trainee concurrency, or the cycle time from coursework completion to licensure. Without those measures, a statewide shortage claim runs ahead of the evidence. (Oregon year in review) (Colorado Natural Medicine program page)

Oregon did create a real second gate

Oregon nevertheless created a real second gate on January 1, 2026, when it required all practicum to occur at licensed service centers. State guidance updated on May 1, 2026 says the practicum supervisor must remain on-site and may not simultaneously provide psilocybin services. That rule does not prove a shortage. It does prove that training now competes with service delivery for a non-concurrent labor input. (Oregon rules) (Oregon guidance)

That matters because an Oregon practicum-bearing session is a bundle, not a seat. A center needs compliant room-hours, a consenting client, support coverage, product and safety compliance, and an administration window that can run from 15 minutes to six hours, depending on the session conditions. Once a center has enough paid demand to fill those slots, protected supervisor time becomes the least substitutable part of the bundle. The surviving claim is narrow: the scarce unit is the practicum-bearing session-hour, not the statewide facilitator count. (Oregon rules)

Colorado already built the relief valves the strong thesis ignored

Colorado shows why the original two-state bottleneck story does not survive intact. The state requires 150 hours of coursework, 40 hours of practicum, and 50 hours of consultation, with at least 30 practicum hours tied to administration sessions. But Colorado also permits recorded-session review, peer-support apprenticeships, role play, and specified non-drug altered-state experiences when in-person supervised experience is unavailable or inaccessible. That is an existing relief mechanism, not a future reform. (Colorado rules) (Colorado Natural Medicine FAQ)

The approved-provider list weakens the shortage story further. On February 2, 2026, Colorado listed 15 approved programs. The evidence in this report supports a more mixed market than the original thesis allowed: 13 of 15 programs listed practicum and 13 listed consultation as available currently or during 2025. The five cases below still matter because they show how differently the pipeline can be assembled, but they do not describe a universally fragmented market. (Colorado Natural Medicine program page)

The named cases do not show the queue yet

The Oregon operating record proves that a market exists. Oregon reported 345 facilitators, 31 service centers, 22 approved curricula or programs, and 724 worker permits at December 31, 2024. A peer-reviewed analysis of the state's 2025 data then reported 5,935 clients and 5,375 administration sessions. Client activity peaked at 1,758 in Q2, then fell to 1,310 in Q3 and 1,358 in Q4. That pattern can fit affordability, seasonality, center economics, uneven demand, or uneven utilization. It does not read like a clean statewide system pushing harder and harder against a supervisor ceiling. (Oregon year in review) (Frontiers analysis)

The named Oregon cases stay narrower than the thesis wanted. EPIC Healing Eugene, the first licensed Oregon service center in 2023, launched with an attributed waitlist of about 80 people. Satya Therapeutics later reported more than 1,550 guided sessions and company-reported all-inclusive pricing of $850 to $1,650. Those cases show early interest and a functioning integrated operator. They do not disclose practicum wait times, supervisor utilization, displacement of paying sessions, or supervision premiums. (KLCC on EPIC Healing Eugene) (Satya Therapeutics site) (Satya pricing)

The key missing series is cycle time. Oregon publishes activity files and historical snapshots, but the evidence set does not show the lag from didactic completion to practicum start, from practicum start to completion, or from training completion to active paid practice. That is the cycle-time evidence the original thesis needs most. Without it, the strongest conclusion remains operational rather than diagnostic. (Oregon data archive)

The closest base rates say regulated bottlenecks migrate

The best historical base rate is not a boom industry. It is other regulated service systems that turned supervision or monitored delivery into a hard operating unit. CACREP counselor training requires a 100-hour practicum, a 600-hour internship, and weekly supervision. Mature training systems often discover that field placement, not classroom instruction, determines throughput. (CACREP professional practice standards)

Later analogs show why that still does not rescue the stronger thesis. SPRAVATO turns the scarce unit into a certified observation chair plus at least two hours of monitored clinician time. TMS turns it into a device-and-operator schedule. ECT turns it into a coordinated physician, anesthesia, and room slot. The 2024 Part 8 revision for opioid treatment programs shows that regulators can also relax personnel coupling when access stalls. Regulated systems surface a composite bottleneck, then reprice it, code it, or route around it. (SPRAVATO label) (CMS mental health coverage) (SAMHSA OTP rule)

Research demand could still make the labor unit heavier

The research pathway shows why the labor question can intensify even if the state-authorized market stays mixed. FDA's final psychedelic-drug guidance expects a lead and assistant monitor through administration, and it expects physician reachability within 15 minutes when the lead monitor is not a physician. Washington University's mechanistic protocol uses lead and assistant facilitators across preparation, dosing, and integration. (FDA guidance) (Washington University protocol)

Named programs reinforce the point. COMPASS Pathways said on February 17, 2026 that COMP006 had 581 dosed participants. Usona is also running a Phase 3 major depressive disorder study. Those studies do not prove that Oregon or Colorado supervisors are already leaving state-authorized services for sponsor-funded sites. They do show that any federal or trial-led expansion is likely to demand paired, monitored labor before it delivers the relief that scale narratives usually expect. (COMPASS filing) (Usona Phase 3)

The surviving control points are narrow and mostly local

What survives of the original idea is an operating map, not a winner's list. Oregon Health Authority controls the rules, the licensed-site requirement, and the fee structure. Centers control room-hours, support staffing, and whether they host teaching shifts. Eligible supervisors control the teaching hours that cannot overlap with paid facilitation. Colorado regulators control how much substitution remains available. Those are real control points, but most sit with regulators, individuals, or private operators rather than a named public owner. (Oregon rules) (Colorado rules)

Oregon Health Authority's draft 2026 rulemaking also proposed raising annual center fees from $10,000 to $20,000 and facilitator fees from $2,000 to $4,000 Proposed, not final, as of July 14, 2026. (Oregon draft rules). The same proposal would recognize Colorado-approved training, which could widen the eligible pipeline into Oregon and delay any statewide supervisor queue. That matters because the evidence supports treating supervisor time as a local and episodic second gate through 2027, not as the statewide pacing constraint today. If center economics fail first, the supervisor bottleneck never gets the chance to bind. (Oregon rulemaking)

The stronger thesis fails because the report did not find statewide backlog data, durable pricing power, named public-company ownership of practicum coordination, or proof that teaching shifts already squeeze client throughput. The surviving claim is smaller: if paid demand and center viability strengthen first, Oregon's protected supervisor requirement may become the pacing component inside a practicum-bearing session. That is a live operating question. It is not yet the market's settled bottleneck.

What would overturn even this narrower view

The remaining claim fails if centers report open practicum slots, idle compliant rooms, and underemployed supervisors after the January 1, 2026 rule change. It also fails if closures and reduced operating days track fees, weak paid conversion, and fixed costs better than supervision, or if Colorado-style substitutes deliver completions at scale without rising practicum queues. A narrower claim has to accept narrow falsifiers.

What to watch

  • Oregon publishes post-January 2026 series for didactic completions, practicum starts, practicum completions, or supervisor allocation.
  • Oregon finalizes or drops draft 2027 changes: higher fees tighten center economics, while recognizing Colorado-approved training widens the eligible pipeline.
  • Centers disclose teaching shifts that leave client-session volume intact through dedicated supervisors or legitimate multi-trainee concurrency.
  • Colorado trainees use recorded review, role play, or other permitted substitutes at material scale without delayed licensure.
  • Trial sponsors or service centers disclose monitor compensation, staffing gaps, or clinician movement between research sites and state-authorized services.
