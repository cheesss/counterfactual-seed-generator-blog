Fluorine’s Industrial Constraint Is the Qualified Pathway

Fluorine supply usually appears as a mineral-security problem. The United States remains fully reliant on net fluorspar imports when stockpiled material is excluded, and Mexico supplied 64% of U.S. imports during 2021–2024. China produced an estimated 6 million tonnes of fluorspar in 2025, roughly 60% of global mine output. Acid-grade fluorspar feeds the anhydrous hydrofluoric acid plants behind uranium conversion, semiconductor cleaning, refrigerants, aluminum fluoride and battery electrolyte salts.

Those facts make mining the obvious control point. They do not make it the decisive one.

Imports, restarted mines, phosphate-derived fluorine and recovered HF can relieve the bulk-material constraint. The harder question begins after fluorine reaches the factory gate: can an operator transform it into the correct molecule, at the required purity, inside a licensed and insured process, then deliver it through an approved logistics route with documentation that a nuclear regulator, semiconductor fab or battery customer will accept?

The evidence supports a sequential-constraint model. Once industry routes around raw-material scarcity, the pacing gate moves downstream into conversion assets, impurity control, process know-how, customer qualification, hazardous-material systems and residual disposition. This mechanism is strongest in uranium conversion and semiconductor HF. It remains plausible, but commercially unproven, for North American LiPF6 and domestic acidspar.

Uranium conversion shows where the constraint can migrate

Metropolis Works in Illinois provides the clearest example. It is the only operating U.S. facility converting uranium concentrates into uranium hexafluoride, or UF6, the material sent to enrichment plants. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission license extends to March 2060, and Solstice Advanced Materials expects the plant to produce more than 10 kilotonnes of UF6 during 2026.

Solstice also reports more than $2 billion of backlog, active debottlenecking and preliminary engineering for further expansion. These are company disclosures rather than audited production outcomes, but they point toward an asset-capacity problem: licensed equipment, operating reliability, maintenance, experienced labor and long-term utility contracts control output.

The fluorspar requirement is comparatively modest in the available engineering model. Idaho National Laboratory estimated that a 15,000-tonne-uranium-per-year conversion plant would consume about 14,700 tonnes of fluorspar annually—approximately 3.2% of U.S. consumption in the model’s 2011 reference year. At $600 per tonne of delivered fluorspar, the modeled contribution was $0.59 per kilogram of uranium in UF6.

That historical model cannot prove current procurement conditions. It does show why higher fluorspar prices alone are unlikely to explain the nuclear-fuel constraint. At Metropolis, the controlling asset is the licensed conversion system. Solstice’s in-house HF capability further reduces the relevance of an external spot-HF shortage.

The next settlement point is operating evidence. Solstice’s reported calendar-2026 production must reach at least 10 kilotonnes of UF6 to validate its current expansion claims. A miss caused by equipment, maintenance or labor would still support the asset-capacity thesis. A miss explicitly attributed to an inability to procure HF or fluorspar would overturn it.

DUF6 reverses the direction of the HF problem

The Department of Energy’s depleted-uranium hexafluoride program presents the same chemistry with the material flow reversed.

Enrichment leaves large inventories of depleted UF6. At Paducah, Kentucky, and Portsmouth, Ohio, DOE facilities convert that material into uranium oxide and aqueous HF. Here, HF is an output that must find a commercial outlet, not an input constraining production. Revenue from HF sales returns to the program and partially offsets operating costs.

The current operator is Mission Conversion Services Alliance, a venture involving Atkins, Westinghouse and Jacobs. DOE awarded the alliance a contract with a maximum value of approximately $2.3 billion over as many as ten years and four months, although the scope includes broader site and mission-support work and cannot be assigned entirely to DUF6 conversion.

The operating constraint has historically included cylinder management, plant reliability, oxide shipping, disposal capacity and legal authority governing material transfers. GAO estimated in 2022 that conversion and disposal would cost at least $7.2 billion. Its finding that only one of three prospective oxide-disposal sites was licensed was accurate as of March 2022, but it no longer describes the entire operating picture. DOE now reports active oxide shipments to authorized disposal facilities and more than 1,000 cylinders converted in each of 2024 and 2025.

DOE is installing dedicated oxide-shipping infrastructure, improving cylinder evacuation and adding backup power for HF-transfer equipment. The department expects the evacuation upgrade to improve throughput by roughly 15%. Applied to the recent operating baseline, a useful test is whether annual conversion exceeds 1,150 cylinders during 2026 or 2027.

This is a residual-management business supported by federal operating and infrastructure spending. HF brokerage, purification and transport may create additional commercial activity, but the economics remain opaque because DOE has not publicly identified current buyers, annual volumes, realized prices or net cost offsets.

Semiconductor HF turns purity into the product

A semiconductor fab does not purchase generic fluorine. It purchases repeatable impurity performance, validated process behavior, lot-level records and continuity from an approved supplier.

Stella Chemifa advertises HF refined beyond 12N purity and more than 100,000 tonnes of annual production capacity across its sites. The capacity figure does not establish current scarcity, but the purity specification shows the nature of the product. A supplier must control trace contaminants at levels that ordinary industrial HF producers do not need to achieve.

The 2019 Japan–South Korea semiconductor-chemical dispute demonstrated how this qualification layer can become a policy choke point. Japan imposed shipment-specific export licensing on hydrogen fluoride, photoresists and fluorinated polyimides. Contemporaneous industry participants warned that approvals could take as long as three months, although some shipments were authorized within weeks.

The episode eventually eased through licensing, supplier diversification, Korean localization efforts and broader diplomatic resolution. That historical base rate matters. Qualified chemical concentration can create a sharp disruption, but the likely response is substitution and multi-sourcing rather than permanent physical scarcity.

TSMC provides the clearest modern counterexample to a rigid fluorspar pathway. Working with suppliers, it qualified HF derived from phosphate-fertilizer waste and introduced the material at Fab 12A and Fab 15A by February 2023 without reporting process-quality degradation. Other 12-inch fabs remained in trial. TSMC reported an initial reduction of 317 tonnes of carbon dioxide and expected much larger reductions from broader rollout.

The experiment proves that mined-fluorspar-derived HF is not indispensable. It does not prove that new sources can be qualified quickly across the industry. The relevant question is replication: how many fabs approve the route, how long approval takes, whether impurity performance remains stable, and whether recurring volumes follow the technical trial.

LiPF6 is still a project-execution test

Battery electrolyte creates a less mature version of the same pathway.

Koura, Orbia’s fluorine-materials business, plans a lithium hexafluorophosphate facility at St. Gabriel, Louisiana. The proposed plant would produce at least 10,000 tonnes of LiPF6 annually. DOE awarded $100 million of financial assistance, and Koura licensed commercial production technology from Japan’s Kanto Denka Kogyo.

DOE completed the project’s final environmental assessment and issued a finding of no significant impact on June 29, 2026. That decision clears an identifiable federal review gate. It does not establish final investment approval, complete state and local permitting, committed financing, an EPC contract, construction, successful commissioning or customer qualification.

Louisiana previously described approximately $400 million for the proposed LiPF6 facility and about $800 million for a two-project package at St. Gabriel. These were proposed investment estimates contingent on final investment decisions, not committed capital.

The assessed project would require new production equipment, warehouses, rail-loading capacity, cooling systems, wastewater treatment and stormwater infrastructure within an existing fluorochemical site. The brownfield location should reduce some interface risk, but it also shows why technology licensing alone cannot create production.

Koura therefore remains a project-finance and execution case before it becomes a clean qualification case. Physical construction by June 30, 2027 would show that federal review, technology access and sponsor support have converted into capital deployment. Commercial shipments to two named independent customers by the end of 2030 would provide much stronger evidence that North American LiPF6 has reached repeatable qualified procurement.

Global supply conditions complicate the scarcity argument. Fitch warned in 2023 that planned Chinese electrolyte capacity could substantially exceed projected 2025 demand. That was a forecast, not verification of realized capacity or utilization. It nevertheless means the Louisiana project should be understood as localization, supply-security and customer-acceptance infrastructure—not proof that the world lacks LiPF6 molecules.

Domestic acidspar still has to become repeatable procurement

Ares Strategic Mining’s Lost Sheep project in Utah illustrates the difference between policy support and delivered material.

Ares has a permitted domestic fluorspar project and access to a five-year Defense Logistics Agency indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contract. The vehicle has a $250 million ceiling, but the government guarantees only $2 million. Additional procurement requires separately issued firm-fixed-price delivery orders.

The previously cited $168.938 million figure represents an estimated or proposed total contract value, not an initially obligated award. A contract ceiling measures purchasing authority; obligations and recognized revenue appear only when delivery orders are issued and performed.

The commercial proof points are therefore concrete: completed production equipment, independent assays, acid-grade certificates of analysis, federal obligations above the guaranteed minimum, accepted shipments and repeat orders. Until those records appear, the contract demonstrates strategic procurement access rather than scaled demand.

Canada Fluorspar offers a nearer-term upstream relief case. Its St. Lawrence operation in Newfoundland and Labrador made its first acid-grade shipment after restarting in August 2025. Historical processing capacity was approximately 200,000 tonnes per year, although the current ramp rate, financing and customer destinations remain unverified.

Together, Canada’s restart, Ares’s procurement vehicle and phosphate-derived fluorine weaken a thesis based on unavoidable geological scarcity. They strengthen the narrower argument that new feed sources still need conversion capacity, specification evidence and customer acceptance before they alter the industrial balance.

Control sits with the actor who can release the material

The industrial value chain divides control differently in each market.

At Metropolis, the operator controls the licensed conversion asset, proprietary process, maintenance history and in-house HF system. Utilities create demand through long-term conversion contracts, while ConverDyn controls the exclusive marketing interface. The potential economic rent sits with scarce operating capacity, although backlog is not revenue and public evidence does not disclose contract prices, cancellation rights or margins.

In semiconductors, the fab controls release authority. A supplier’s plant and purity claims have little value until the customer approves the material in its process. Historical quality records, lot genealogy, audit access and change-control data may create switching friction, but no reviewed source quantifies the premium.

At Koura, risk remains with the sponsor and government until financing, construction and customer commitments appear. Kanto Denka may receive licensing or technical-service revenue, but the commercial terms are undisclosed. EPC companies, analytical laboratories, rail contractors and hazardous-material specialists may capture project spending even if product margins later prove weak.

At the DUF6 sites, DOE remains the payer and retains the long-duration environmental obligation. Contractors earn operating, maintenance and infrastructure revenue, while HF sales reduce the program’s net cost. Gross federal spending and coproduct sales must not be added as separate economic value without accounting for purification, loading, transport and brokerage costs.

The general pattern is clear: ownership of ore is only one form of leverage. The more durable control points may be the operating license, approved process, qualification file, logistics route and customer interface. The evidence does not yet show that these control points consistently produce superior margins.

The strongest counterargument is that qualification may be routine

The thesis can fail even while every named project advances.

Koura could begin construction because subsidies and industrial policy absorb development risk, then discover that financing, global prices or weak offtake—not customer qualification—determine utilization. Ares could make accepted DLA deliveries without unusual testing delays or premium economics. Semiconductor fabs could qualify additional HF sources within ordinary procurement cycles. Insurers and regulators could treat experienced HF operators through standardized, readily transferable arrangements.

In that scenario, qualification remains necessary but does not remain scarce. It becomes a normal cost of doing business rather than a persistent control point.

The 2019 Japan–South Korea episode and TSMC’s alternative-HF deployment support this challenge. Both show that concentrated supply can be disrupted and then diversified. Neither demonstrates enduring pricing power for a small group of qualified suppliers.

The value-migration claim is therefore less established than the operational mechanism. Current evidence shows where spending could move—to conversion equipment, purification, laboratories, logistics, safety systems and qualification work. It does not show comparative margins, return on capital, switching-cost monetization or durable supplier bargaining power.

Public-company exposure follows different nodes

Solstice Advanced Materials provides exposure to the operating uranium-conversion asset and its expansion cycle. Fluor was part of the previous DUF6 operating venture, while current work is performed by Mission Conversion Services Alliance; public-company exposure to that contract also runs through AtkinsRéalis and Jacobs, though the DUF6-specific revenue allocation is undisclosed.

Orbia controls Koura and the proposed Louisiana LiPF6 pathway. Kanto Denka supplies licensed production technology. Stella Chemifa and Solvay participate in high-purity fluorochemical markets, although product-level economics and customer concentration are not visible in the reviewed material. TSMC represents downstream qualification power rather than upstream fluorine scarcity.

Ares is a direct domestic-fluorspar project exposure, but its economic relevance depends on obligations, production and accepted deliveries—not the IDIQ ceiling. These names identify where industrial risk and spending may surface; they do not constitute recommendations.

The constraint is sequential, not universal

Fluorspar remains strategically concentrated, and HF remains hazardous. Neither fact alone identifies the pacing industrial constraint.

Uranium conversion is governed primarily by a licensed operating asset and its throughput. DUF6 cleanup is governed by conversion, logistics and disposition, with HF acting as a coproduct. Semiconductor HF is governed by impurity control and fab approval. North American LiPF6 and domestic acidspar still have to progress through finance, construction, production and procurement before qualification can be shown to bind.

The durable thesis is narrower: when industry successfully routes around a visible raw-material problem, it often encounters a less visible pathway problem downstream. That pathway matters only when commercial-scale output, specification compliance, customer acceptance and repeat purchasing appear together.

The next several years should reveal whether qualified fluorine becomes a persistent control point through 2026–2028, or merely a temporary transition cost on the way to broader supply, substitution and multi-sourcing.
