The visible shortage story around fluorine chemistry usually starts with mining: the United States does

The visible shortage story around fluorine chemistry usually starts with mining: the United States does not mine meaningful commercial fluorspar, China dominates global production, Mexico supplies most U.S. imports, and hydrofluoric acid sits behind uranium conversion, semiconductor etching, refrigerants, aluminum fluoride, and lithium-ion electrolyte salts. That version is true enough to be useful, but incomplete enough to mislead.

The tighter question is not whether the world can find another tonne of calcium fluoride. It is whether a tonne of fluorine can pass through the right qualified, permitted, hazardous-material chain and arrive as the right molecule, at the right purity, inside the right customer qualification file. In uranium conversion, bulk fluorspar looks less like the controlling constraint than the conversion asset itself. In semiconductors, the gate is ultra-high-purity wet chemistry and export-license resilience. In batteries, the gate is localized LiPF6 know-how, feed integration, and customer acceptance, not merely electrolyte demand. In depleted uranium hexafluoride cleanup, the awkward fact is reversed: the process makes hydrofluoric acid as a coproduct and then needs a safe outlet.

Fluorine is a commodity input until it is not

That distinction changes how the industrial map should be read. Fluorine is a commodity input until it is not. Once it becomes anhydrous HF, electronic-grade HF, LiPF6, or a byproduct stream from DUF6 deconversion, the bottleneck migrates from geology into standards, permits, process safety, customer audits, and residual handling.

USGS’s 2026 fluorspar summary gives the blunt upstream picture. Excluding stockpiled material, the United States was 100% net import reliant for fluorspar. The same document reports that acid-grade fluorspar is used to produce anhydrous HF at facilities in Louisiana and Texas; that HF is the primary feedstock for most fluorine chemicals; and that HF is used in uranium processing, semiconductor etching and cleaning, and other fluorochemical systems. The import base is concentrated: for 2021-2024, Mexico supplied 64% of U.S. fluorspar imports, with Vietnam, China, and South Africa making up most of the remainder. The U.S. also imported hydrofluoric acid, with Mexico supplying most HF imports in the third quarter of 2025.

That looks like a classic mineral-security bottleneck. Yet the nuclear fuel evidence cuts against the simplest version. Idaho National Laboratory’s uranium-conversion cost basis report estimated that a domestic conversion plant operating at 15,000 metric tons uranium per year would require about 14,700 metric tons of fluorspar, equal to roughly 3.2% of U.S. 2011 fluorspar consumption. At a delivered fluorspar cost of $600 per metric ton, the report estimated only $0.59 per kilogram uranium in UF6 from fluorspar. INL also described conversion as less than 4% of nuclear fuel cost. If those order-of-magnitude relationships still hold, fluorspar price alone does not explain the nuclear-fuel bottleneck.

The more relevant nuclear control point is Metropolis Works in Illinois

The more relevant nuclear control point is Metropolis Works in Illinois. ConverDyn describes Metropolis as the only U.S. uranium hexafluoride conversion facility. NRC’s environmental assessment for license renewal describes a 40-year renewal basis and an authorized capacity of 15,000 metric tons of uranium. Solstice Advanced Materials, which now operates the asset, said in February 2026 that expected 2026 production would exceed 10 kilotonnes of UF6, about 20% above the planned 2024 capacity, and that the facility carried more than $2 billion of backlog. That is not a fluorspar mine story. It is an asset-rights, licensed-capacity, refurbishment, customer-contract, and operating-know-how story.

The depleted-uranium cleanup case points in the same direction but with the sign flipped. DOE’s Paducah and Portsmouth DUF6 conversion facilities convert depleted uranium hexafluoride into uranium oxide and hydrofluoric acid. DOE says the HF can be collected and sold, with revenue returned to the project to partially offset operating costs. Fluor’s Mid-America Conversion Services page describes the joint venture among Atkins, Fluor, and Westinghouse operating the DUF6 conversion facilities and brokering the sale of aqueous HF. GAO estimated in 2022 that DOE faced at least $7.2 billion of cost to convert and dispose of its DUF6 inventory. GAO’s bottleneck facts were not “DOE cannot buy HF.” They were cylinder disposition, legal authority for transfers, restart modifications, and oxide disposal. GAO noted that only one of three potential depleted-uranium oxide disposal sites was licensed as of March 2022. In this branch of the system, HF is a coproduct outlet and cost-offset path, not the scarce reagent.

Semiconductor chemistry is where the “right molecule in the right document trail” problem becomes clearer. Stella Chemifa says its ultra-high-purity hydrofluoric acid reaches 12N purity and that its products are used in semiconductor etching and cleaning. Solvay’s electronic wet chemicals page describes high-purity HF sourced from plants in China, Thailand, Germany, and the United States, and references photovoltaic HF with impurity below 10 ppb. These specifications change the bottleneck shape. A semiconductor fab does not buy generic fluorine; it buys repeatable purity, impurity-control documentation, logistics reliability, waste-handling practices, and supplier qualification.

The 2019 Japan-South Korea semiconductor chemicals dispute is the historical warning

The 2019 Japan-South Korea semiconductor chemicals dispute is the historical warning. USITC’s review of that dispute notes that Japan imposed export licensing restrictions on hydrogen fluoride, fluorinated polyimides, and photoresists shipped to South Korea. Industry speculation at the time pointed to individual shipment licenses that could take up to three months. That episode did not prove a permanent physical shortage of HF. It proved that qualified chemical supply can become a policy choke point even when the underlying chemistry is well known.

The workaround evidence is equally important. TSMC says it collaborated with suppliers to replace fluorspar-derived inputs with hydrofluoric acid made from phosphate fertilizer waste, while maintaining process quality. Its Fab 12A and Fab 15A had adopted the alternative by February 2023, with other 12-inch fabs in trial. TSMC reported 317 metric tons of carbon dioxide reduction from early adoption, expected more than 20,000 metric tons after rollout across 12-inch fabs, and targeted more than 40,000 metric tons after a second production line. USGS separately notes that fluorosilicic acid from phosphate operations can substitute for fluorspar-derived fluorine in some routes and that U.S. phosphate rock reserves contain large fluorine-equivalent resources. That does not eliminate the qualification gate. It relocates it into a different industrial residue stream.

Battery electrolyte adds a third version of the same mechanism. Koura, part of Orbia, received a $100 million DOE award for a planned LiPF6 facility at St. Gabriel, Louisiana. Orbia and Louisiana Economic Development described a 10,000 metric ton per year LiPF6 project, enough for more than one million electric vehicles annually, with the LiPF6 facility estimated around $400 million and a two-project St. Gabriel proposal around $800 million. Orbia also signed a technology licensing agreement with Kanto Denka Kogyo for LiPF6 production know-how. That licensing detail matters. The scarce item is not just fluorine feedstock; it is proven commercial LiPF6 technology embedded in a vertically integrated fluorine chain and then accepted by battery customers.

The disconfirming evidence keeps the battery case from becoming a simple scarcity narrative

The disconfirming evidence keeps the battery case from becoming a simple scarcity narrative. Fitch reported in 2023 that Chinese electrolyte capacity was likely to exceed demand by a wide margin by 2025. Benchmark Minerals later reported sharp LiPF6 price moves in late 2025, but price spikes in a volatile battery material do not automatically imply structural shortage. They can reflect lithium carbonate swings, policy timing, inventory cycles, or localized qualification. The better phrasing is that North American LiPF6 may be a localization and qualification bottleneck even if the global electrolyte chain is oversupplied.

Ares Strategic Mining’s Lost Sheep project in Utah is the cleanest domestic fluorspar asset to watch, but it also illustrates why the deepest constraint is not geology alone. Ares describes Lost Sheep as the only permitted and operational U.S. fluorspar mine, with a mine near Spor Mountain and processing in Delta. Its annual information form points to BLM environmental review, reclamation plans, bonding, and the requirement that plans, permits, and financial assurance be in place before mining. Ares later announced a Defense Logistics Agency contract for acid-grade fluorspar with a $168.9 million initial value and a ceiling up to $250 million over five years. If that contract translates into delivery orders, quality certificates, and repeatable acidspar output, the domestic fluorspar story becomes more concrete. If the plant slips, product fails specification, or official task-order evidence remains thin, the asset remains a lead rather than a solved constraint.

The regulatory layer makes all of these chains harder to scale in ordinary industrial sites. EPA’s Risk Management Program list sets a 1,000-pound threshold for anhydrous hydrogen fluoride and hydrofluoric acid at 50% or greater concentration. PHMSA hazardous-material rules place hydrofluoric acid and anhydrous hydrogen fluoride in the hazardous-materials system, with DOT shipping names, hazard classes, packing groups, and reportable quantities. Emergency response guidance for HF and AHF emphasizes trained responders, personal protective equipment, isolation, and community protection. The effect is not that HF cannot move. It moves every day. The effect is that new capacity prefers existing chemical corridors, incumbent operators, trained labor, insurance acceptance, community tolerance, and environmental paperwork already built around fluorine.

A miner looks like leverage to raw material scarcity

That is where capital markets can miss the control point. A miner looks like leverage to raw material scarcity. A chemical plant looks like capacity. A uranium converter looks like nuclear-fuel exposure. A semiconductor wet-chemical supplier looks like a process consumables business. A DUF6 contractor looks like environmental cleanup. The same fluorine chain threads through all of them, but value capture may sit at different nodes depending on which gate binds: mine-to-acidspar qualification, AHF handling, electronic-grade purification, LiPF6 process know-how, customer qualification, waste recovery, or byproduct monetization.

Three outcomes deserve close monitoring. First, the uranium conversion bottleneck could remain mostly a Metropolis Works and conversion-service issue, with fluorspar prices visible but not controlling. Evidence would include Solstice hitting production expansion targets, utilities continuing to contract for conversion, and no disclosed HF procurement disruption. Second, battery and semiconductor customers could turn qualified fluorochemicals into a localization premium even if global bulk supply looks ample. Evidence would include long-term offtake, customer qualification announcements, domestic LiPF6 startup milestones, and new electronic-grade HF capacity inside trusted jurisdictions. Third, phosphate-derived fluorine and DUF6 HF outlets could become relief valves. Evidence would include more fabs adopting waste-derived HF, more fluorosilicic-acid-to-HF investment, and public contracts identifying where DOE’s DUF6 HF actually goes.

One fact would weaken the whole thesis: if project records show that qualified fluorochemical suppliers

One fact would weaken the whole thesis: if project records show that qualified fluorochemical suppliers, AHF handlers, electronic-grade HF producers, and LiPF6 plants can add capacity quickly without permitting friction, customer qualification delay, transport constraints, or material-spec problems, then fluorine collapses back into a conventional commodity story. The current record points elsewhere. The bulk mineral matters, but the durable control point is qualified fluorine through hazardous, regulated, customer-approved channels.
