Cold Storage’s Refrigerant Shift Is Building a Service Economy Around the Machine Room

The visible story in cold-storage decarbonization is refrigerant substitution: warehouses move away from high-global-warming-potential HFCs and toward ammonia, carbon dioxide, or newer synthetic refrigerants. The more important industrial story is what has to form around those choices.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-08-29 · 17 min read

In brief

  • GWP 700 governs new U.S. cold-storage systems during the federal transition through 2031.
  • 2032 brings stricter 150/300 GWP limits and another round of equipment qualification.
  • 606,375 square feet makes Lineage Hutchins a major live test of post-rule procurement.
  • 90% lower reported GHG emissions at Valley accompanied a real compressor-related service event.

A refrigeration architecture brings compressors, valves, heat exchangers, piping, sensors, pressure limits, controls, leak or gas detection, safety procedures, listed components, commissioning routines and technician requirements. Those pieces must work together well enough for an engineer to specify them, a contractor to install them, a code official to accept them and an operator to maintain them for decades.

That is an ecosystem-expansion mechanism: the regulatory and installed-base transition creates architecture-specific requirements that equipment alone cannot satisfy; standards, qualified components, commissioning, software, training and lifecycle service form around the installed system.

EPA’s May 2026 final rule gave that ecosystem a long runway. New cold-storage warehouse systems moved to an interim GWP ceiling of 700 on July 27, 2026. Beginning January 1, 2032, the ceiling falls to 150 for systems carrying at least 200 pounds of refrigerant and to 300 for smaller systems and the high-temperature side of cascades. EPA explicitly extended the schedule after considering safety, building-code readiness, commercial availability, mechanical practicality, efficiency and cost. (EPA final rule)

The result is not a six-year march toward one winner. The interim rule permits a nonflammable synthetic path such as R-513A while ammonia and CO₂ remain available and A2L configurations develop further. That matters because each route creates a different complement set—and therefore a different map of suppliers, skills and recurring service.

Natural refrigerants already have a head start

EPA’s rulemaking record says more than 90% of U.S. cold-storage warehouse refrigeration systems use ammonia or CO₂. It also cites a 2023 GCCA survey in which 91% of respondents reported ammonia, 10% synthetic F-gas and 5% CO₂; respondents could report more than one refrigerant.

That makes an A2L-only interpretation of the transition too narrow. Much of the near-term expansion may happen inside natural-refrigerant systems that are already commercial.

CO₂ plants require high-pressure-rated equipment, gas coolers, controls that manage transcritical operation, pressure testing and technicians comfortable with operating conditions that differ from conventional HFC installations. Ammonia brings a mature but demanding system of IIAR standards, inspection, testing, maintenance and process-safety practice. A2Ls add another branch of component compatibility, ignition protection, refrigerant-release analysis and flammability-related training.

UL Solutions already sells many of those complements: component and system certification, A2L component work, ignition-protected component testing, ISO 14903 leak testing, pressure and fatigue testing, full-scale refrigerant-release testing, computational-fluid-dynamics analysis and training. Intertek operates a parallel commercial-refrigeration testing and ETL certification route.

This is where standards interoperability starts to matter economically. ASHRAE Standards 15 and 34, ICC model-code provisions, IIAR’s ammonia standards, equipment listings and local enforcement all have to meet inside a working plant. California has gone further by creating a dedicated acceptance-test form, NRCA-PRC-17-F, for transcritical CO₂ refrigeration under its 2025 nonresidential compliance regime.

The valuable activity is therefore not simply selling a lower-GWP molecule. It is making refrigerant, hardware, software, standards and operating practice interoperable.

Valley Fine Foods offers the richest operating evidence. The Yuba City food-processing plant replaced R-22 spiral-freezer refrigeration with a transcritical CO₂ rack. The state project record lists compressors, condensers, evaporators, chillers, piping, fittings, insulation and controls, while the final report identifies ACCO Engineered Systems as installation contractor.

The reported results were strong: electricity consumption fell about 50%, refrigeration energy intensity 57%, natural-gas use 31%, water consumption 12% and annual greenhouse-gas emissions about 90%. Yet the same project recorded a compressor failure that contaminated the refrigerant charge and forced replacement of the CO₂. The final report explicitly recommends confirming that qualified service technicians are available for troubleshooting and safe repairs. (Valley final report)

That is the ecosystem thesis in miniature. Successful equipment adoption did not eliminate specialized service demand; it created an installed asset whose performance depended on it.

Alpine Food Distributing in Vernon shows the supplier stack more clearly. ARCO describes the 116,000-square-foot facility as using transcritical CO₂, while Classic Refrigeration names EVAPCO LMP equipment, Güntner heat-rejection equipment and Pride components. Classic also advertises continuing 24/7 service around the installation. The rack sale pulls a chain of equipment, integration and field support behind it.

Arcadia’s roughly 295,000-square-foot Joliet facility provides another large CO₂ deployment. LJC’s project record connects design and permit coordination to the warehouse build, although public material located in the research does not expose the refrigeration OEM, commissioning contract or service economics. That absence is important: the physical system is visible before the value split inside it is.

The approval clock is not necessarily the hard part

RLS Complete’s Sturbridge project helps separate land-use review from refrigeration complexity. The roughly 83,000-square-foot frozen-food warehouse submitted its site-plan application in late January 2021. The Planning Board approved it on February 23 and issued its decision on February 24—about a month from application to decision. The facility opened in October 2022.

The municipal decision records review by fire, building and engineering officials, but it does not identify refrigerant qualification as the cause of the planning cycle. Later operating and industry records associate the facility with transcritical CO₂.

That distinction matters. Low-GWP adoption does not automatically turn every warehouse into a prolonged entitlement problem. Much of the monetizable complexity can sit deeper in the project: mechanical design, component selection, pressure testing, control tuning, commissioning, documentation and continuing service.

Shoreline Freezers in Bridgeton reinforces the point. Municipal records document redevelopment agreements and expansion phases beginning in 2021, while the operator advertises transcritical CO₂ refrigeration. A New Jersey environmental permit surfaced in the research, but it was a site-level general permit rather than a refrigeration-system approval.

For researchers trying to track the transition, the obvious planning docket can therefore be the wrong place to look. Mechanical schedules, equipment submittals, fire-code reviews, commissioning records, warranties and service contracts may contain the economically relevant evidence.

Design changes can redistribute spend during construction

RL Cold’s Ridgeville project shows that refrigerant architecture need not be fixed when construction starts. Engineering News-Record reported that the approximately 295,000-square-foot warehouse changed from a Freon design to an ammonia/CO₂ cascade during delivery and absorbed more than 100 owner-directed changes overall. The refrigerant-design change comes from trade-press reporting; the underlying owner change order was not located in the research.

A change like that does not alter the warehouse’s basic purpose, but it can reroute spending across engineering, compressors, vessels, piping, controls, safety procedures and commissioning. It is a useful reminder that value migration can happen inside a project even when the visible asset—a refrigerated warehouse—looks unchanged.

The largest unresolved live case is Lineage’s Hutchins, Texas freezer development. The Texas state record lists 606,375 square feet, an estimated construction cost of $162 million, a September 2025 start and a June 2027 listed completion. TGW announced in June 2026 that it will supply automation and expects go-live around the end of 2027.

The refrigeration architecture was not found in the project, contractor or state records searched. That missing specification is itself useful. A project of this scale can reveal whether a major owner chooses ammonia, CO₂, an interim synthetic architecture or another route under the new federal schedule—and which vendors control the associated commissioning and service stack.

Who controls the transition

The control points sit at the interfaces.

EPA controls the federal refrigerant schedule. Standards organizations and code bodies define acceptable design and operating frameworks. UL Solutions and Intertek translate portions of those rules into product-testing and certification evidence. Refrigeration integrators such as ACCO and Classic turn equipment packages into operating plants. Component suppliers such as Danfoss, EVAPCO and Güntner participate where architecture-specific pressure, heat-rejection and controls requirements matter.

The most durable control point may emerge after startup. Dover’s 2025 filing says Dover Food Retail, which it describes as a market leader in CO₂ refrigeration solutions, introduced cloud software spanning deployment, diagnostics, warranty management and maintenance. Dover specifically says the workflow can shorten startup cycles and reduce errors such as incorrect high-pressure control set points. (Dover 2025 10-K)

That is a different business from simply selling a rack. The platform with commissioning data, alarm history, warranty records and diagnostic access can remain embedded in operations after construction ends.

Training works similarly. IIAR currently charges nonmembers $1,590 for its IIAR 2 course, $1,350 for PSM/RMP and $1,390 for IIAR 6 inspection, testing and maintenance training. RETA’s 2025 conference included CO₂ transcritical systems, natural-refrigerant codes and industrial-refrigeration skills. Hussmann offers dedicated CO₂ training and an eight-hour A2L and flammable-refrigerant certification course.

These fees are small beside a warehouse project, but the economic signal is clear: architecture-specific competence has become a formal commercial product.

The installed base makes service durable

The opportunity does not depend on greenfield construction continuing at the same pace. Refrigeration systems already sit inside large warehouse portfolios and require recurring repair and replacement.

Lineage reported $173 million of companywide recurring maintenance capital expenditure in 2025 and $64 million in the first half of 2026. Its filings explicitly include repair or replacement of refrigeration systems in that category, although the company does not disclose the refrigeration share. Americold similarly identifies refrigeration-equipment replacement as maintenance capital across its warehouse network.

That installed base changes the economics of the transition. Even if new construction slows, refrigeration plants keep generating preventive maintenance, controls upgrades, technician work, refrigerant handling and eventual component replacement.

The public-company exposures therefore span different parts of the mechanism rather than one uniform “low-GWP” trade: Lineage and Americold own refrigerated assets; Dover sells refrigeration equipment and lifecycle software; UL Solutions and Intertek provide testing and certification; Johnson Controls participates in industrial refrigeration equipment and service; Chemours supplies both newer Opteon refrigerants and legacy Freon products.

Chemours also demonstrates why policy should not be translated into a straight-line revenue forecast. Opteon sales rose from $710 million in 2023 to $1.264 billion in 2025 across multiple end markets, but second-quarter 2026 Opteon sales fell year over year while Freon sales rose. Those figures are not cold-storage-specific. They are useful precisely because they show how uneven a long substitution cycle can be.

The closest precedent argues for coexistence, not a cliff

The HCFC-22 phaseout is the closest historical base rate in the report. New R-22 supply was phased down over years, while existing systems continued to operate using previously produced, recovered or reclaimed refrigerant. The result was prolonged coexistence between old assets, replacement equipment and a continuing service economy.

The same feature is present now: federal restrictions change new-system choices without forcing the entire installed base to disappear.

What is different is the number of viable architectures. The current transition includes mature ammonia, increasingly deployed CO₂, interim A1 synthetics and several A2L substitutes rather than a single obvious replacement pathway. That diversity raises the number of interfaces that engineers, suppliers and technicians must manage even if the overall transition proceeds gradually.

The early U.S. experience with transcritical CO₂ in supermarkets points in the same direction. Knowledge accumulated over years as components, controls and service practices diffused from early projects. Industrial cold storage operates at different scale and alongside a much stronger ammonia incumbent, but the precedent argues against expecting an instantaneous transition.

The counterargument is that incumbents may absorb almost everything

EPA’s delay is itself the strongest argument against an expansive reading. Regulators extended the stricter limits because codes, safety practices, commercial availability, mechanical practicality and economics were not uniformly ready.

Meanwhile, ammonia and CO₂ already have substantial industrial-refrigeration ecosystems. Established contractors can retrain employees rather than create entirely new service companies. OEMs can internalize diagnostics and training. Owners can continue operating legacy equipment. R-513A can postpone A2L adoption for some new systems during the interim period.

Three plausible paths follow.

First, natural refrigerants could absorb most new procurement. If at least four of the next six comparable projects with public refrigerant disclosures choose ammonia or CO₂, the expansion thesis survives—but its beneficiaries remain concentrated around mature natural-refrigerant equipment, controls and service rather than a new A2L economy.

Second, the GWP-700 period could postpone A2L-specific formation. Repeated selection of R-513A, ammonia or CO₂ through 2028 would imply that A2L qualification, testing and training grows more slowly than the federal end-state might suggest.

Third, lifecycle software could expand before refrigerant choice converges. Dover already provides one example. If other major OEMs or integrators add commissioning, diagnostics, warranty and maintenance platforms by 2029, the most persistent value may migrate toward operating data and service workflow rather than any single refrigerant.

There is also a clear falsifier. If a meaningful sample of post-2026 new builds and major retrofits shows no separately identifiable increase in specialized components, qualification, commissioning, training, diagnostics or lifecycle service compared with conventional prior projects, this is mostly an equipment substitution being absorbed by the existing refrigeration industry.

The next transition runs from procurement to repeatability

The timeline now has three distinct phases.

From 2026 through roughly 2028, the main evidence will come from actual project specifications under the interim GWP-700 rule: which refrigerants owners choose, which vendors appear in equipment schedules and whether commissioning or diagnostics packages become explicit procurement items.

From 2028 through 2030, ecosystem expansion should become measurable if it is real. Training calendars should persist, listed component families should broaden, multiple vendors should offer lifecycle diagnostics, and service contracts should begin to show architecture-specific requirements rather than generic refrigeration maintenance.

From 2030 into 2032, long project lead times should force the next qualification cycle. Projects expected to operate after the stricter thresholds take effect will increasingly need to specify configurations compatible with the 150/300 limits before the legal deadline arrives.

After 2032, the decisive question changes. Compliance-driven installation work can fade; maintenance, inspection, diagnostics, warranty, technician training and refrigerant-management activity must persist for the ecosystem to prove durable.

What to watch

  • Lineage Hutchins refrigeration specification: a disclosed refrigerant and Division 23 equipment schedule would settle a major 2026-27 procurement case.
  • Six post-July-2026 project disclosures: four or more ammonia/CO₂ selections would support natural-refrigerant dominance through 2028.
  • Three major lifecycle-software platforms by end-2029: deployment, diagnostics, warranty or maintenance functions would confirm workflow expansion.
  • 2030-31 project specifications: repeated use of configurations already compliant with the 2032 GWP 150/300 limits would mark the next qualification wave.
  • UL/ETL certification volumes or lead times: measurable growth would show qualification becoming a distinct commercial activity rather than routine product refresh.

The refrigeration plant is becoming a useful place to watch industrial commercialization because the transition is visible at several layers at once. Federal policy changes the architecture menu; standards and component ratings determine what can be built; integrators make the system work; software and technicians keep it working.

The strongest version of the thesis is not that a new standalone “low-GWP industry” appears beside cold storage. It is that a more diverse refrigeration installed base increases the number and value of interfaces that must be managed repeatedly. Whether those interfaces become new independent businesses or remain inside established OEMs and refrigeration contractors is the question the next six years of procurement will answer.
