Green Hydrogen Feedwater Is Emerging As A Water-Loop And Discharge Control Point

The visible fight in green hydrogen is usually framed around power prices, electrolyzer factories, subsidy rules, and offtake contracts. Those are real gates. They also make it easy to miss a smaller control point inside the plant boundary: the water loop that can keep the stack within spec, prove compliance to the regulator, and dispose of concentrate, purge, spent resins, and wastewater.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 12 min read

In brief

  • 15 MWe Northfleet had to revise its water balance before the permit was granted.
  • 120 MWe Humber still carries a discharge condition tied to the Humber Estuary.
  • 50 MW Orica shows water can be solved while funding and offtake stay binding.
  • 18 Mohm-cm PEM specs push projects toward packaged polishing and monitoring systems.

The first tell is that this looks like a chemistry problem

Public operating evidence remains sparse. The strongest material in this quarry is permit-stage and pre-operation documentation, not demonstrated fleetwide downtime or a long record of disclosed feedwater-linked outages.

That makes the cleaner thesis narrower. The scarce item is not water in the abstract. It is a qualified, monitored, permitted ultrapure-water loop that converts whatever local water is available into stack-grade feedwater and then proves where the residual stream goes. At this stage, that reads as a site-level permitting and commissioning control point that can extend into warranty or O&M risk, not as a settled fleetwide bottleneck.

The chemistry alone can mislead. Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen requires about 9 kilograms of water for 1 kilogram of hydrogen, but a real plant still has to pretreat the incoming water, polish it to stack grade, monitor contaminants, and document the purge and waste stream. The Environment Agency's emerging-techniques guidance treats water as a managed system with monitoring, wastewater, and solid residuals, not as a cheap commodity input. (Environment Agency emerging-techniques guidance)

Named cases show where the constraint moves

Northfleet Hydrogen Production Facility in Gravesend is the cleanest small case. The application was duly made in August 2024, additional information followed in October 2024, and the Environment Agency granted the permit in February 2025 for a 15 MWe PEM plant supplying hydrogen by pipeline to the adjacent Northfleet Paper Mill. The sequence matters because the water loop became part of the permit clock, not just an engineering note. (Environment Agency decision document)

The applicant initially described 4.2 cubic metres per hour of demineralized water to produce 280 kilograms per hour of hydrogen, or roughly 15 kilograms of water per kilogram of hydrogen. The regulator challenged that against the 9:1 chemical minimum.

Manufacturer data then split the loop into 3,456 litres per hour of input water, 864 litres per hour removed by purification, and 2,592 litres per hour of pure water consumed, bringing the feedwater ratio back to about 9.1 kilograms per kilogram. The same decision document records a feedwater conductivity requirement below 1 microsiemens per centimetre, reverse-osmosis effluent sent to foul sewer under Southern Water trade-effluent consent, and pre-operational conditions tied to the final purification design. (Environment Agency decision document)

The plant is also a permit stack

The Humber H2ub Green project makes the same pattern larger and more visible. Planning material for Uniper's proposed 120 MWe plant at Killingholme describes six PEM electrolyzer units, about 21,000 normal cubic metres per hour of hydrogen at roughly 31.5 bar, and a future expansion path beyond 200 MWe. It also describes several water-source options, a water-treatment plant, raw-water and demineralized-water storage, and treatment wastewater plus site drainage discharged through the existing KPS-B outfall to the Humber Estuary. (North Lincolnshire planning report)

The sharper clue is what the planning report says about cooling and permits. The project selected air coolers partly because water supply on the South Humber bank is limited. More important, operation cannot begin until the local planning authority receives either an Environmental Permit setting water-borne pollutant limits agreed with the Environment Agency and Natural England, or a Natural England statement that the conservation objectives for the estuary's protected sites will not be compromised. Water becomes estuary risk, effluent chemistry, and agency concurrence. (North Lincolnshire planning report)

ITM Power's May 2025 supplier release adds the capital-markets angle. The company said it had been selected to supply six 20 MW POSEIDON modules, with final investment decision expected in 2026 and operation targeted for 2029. That is exactly the kind of project where a water package is small in capex terms but still capable of slowing startup or complicating warranty compliance if the permit stack is not closed. (ITM Power project release)

The counterweight: water solved, commercial risk binding

Orica's Hunter Valley Hydrogen Hub is the strongest counterexample in the file. Orica said on 1 July 2026 that it had taken final investment decision on a 50 MW first phase expected to produce 4,700 tonnes per year of renewable hydrogen and 26,600 tonnes per year of low-carbon ammonia, begin construction in 2026, and start production in early 2029. The project uses recycled water and renewable electricity at Orica's existing Kooragang Island ammonia site. Its support stack includes AUD 432 million from ARENA's Hydrogen Headstart program, AUD 45 million from New South Wales, and AUD 70 million from the federal Regional Hydrogen Hubs program. (Orica FID release)

That matters because the observed delay was not primarily a water delay. Reuters reported Origin Energy's October 2024 exit from the project as a response to uncertainty around hydrogen-market development and capital intensity, not feedwater. Orica's later decision to proceed after revenue support says the binding gate was commercial: power cost, offtake, funding, and risk allocation. Water still mattered, but as a solved prerequisite rather than the dominant constraint. (Reuters on Origin's exit)

Vendor packaging, and the edge case that breaks scale

Shell's Holland Hydrogen I points to the vendor-control version of the story. Shell describes a 200 MW Rotterdam plant under construction, powered by Hollandse Kust Noord offshore wind, expected to produce around 60 tonnes per day of hydrogen, with commissioning in late 2026 and ramp-up in 2027. Pure Water Group says thyssenkrupp nucera selected it to provide the ultrapure-water package for the same project. If that modular pattern becomes standard, feedwater becomes less like a bespoke local emergency and more like a recurring subsystem inside the electrolyzer supply chain. (Shell Holland Hydrogen I) (Pure Water Group project release)

NEOM is the edge case that keeps the thesis narrow. Air Products, ACWA Power, and thyssenkrupp nucera describe a project producing up to 600 tonnes per day of hydrogen in the form of green ammonia, with more than 2 GW of alkaline electrolysis. In the source set captured here, the public evidence does not show ultrapure water as the named gating item. At that scale, the more visible public gates are power buildout, electrolyzer delivery, ammonia synthesis, export logistics, financing, and offtake. (thyssenkrupp nucera on NEOM)

Who controls the constraint

The vendor map is already visible in the quarry. DuPont launched AmberLite P2X110 as an ion-exchange resin for green hydrogen and describes a treatment stack that runs from pretreatment through reverse osmosis, EDI, and loop polishing. Ecolab's Purolite line markets UltraClean resins for PEM loops and says PEM systems need ultrapure water above 18 Mohm-cm, or below about 0.056 microsiemens per centimetre. (DuPont hydrogen resin release) (Purolite green hydrogen page)

The rest of the chain looks like a service and monitoring stack, not yet a proven pool of disclosed economic rent. Xylem's Evoqua materials emphasize portable ion-exchange tanks, off-site regeneration, local technicians, and no on-site chemical handling. Veolia markets packaged systems from small plants to sites above 500 MW and stresses wastewater management and residual handling. LANXESS markets a three-stage PEM polishing train with UV to manage total-organic-carbon release. (Xylem service deionization FAQ) (Veolia hydrogen production page) (LANXESS green hydrogen page)

Those pages do not prove a large public-equity opportunity, disclosed margin capture, or public risk-transfer terms. They do show why these suppliers matter to the control-point map. The project owner cares about uptime and levelized hydrogen cost. The OEM cares about warranty and degradation. The regulator cares about the water balance and the discharge route. That makes water-treatment vendors, service contractors, and labs relevant exposure points if the site-level loop proves hard to qualify or maintain, not demonstrated leverage winners.

The counterargument: water isn't scarce at the national level

The strongest counterargument is that water is usually not scarce in aggregate. RMI estimates green-hydrogen water consumption at roughly 20 to 30 litres per kilogram of hydrogen after purification and process cooling, broadly comparable to or lower than many fossil-hydrogen pathways. A 2025 DESNZ-commissioned study found that even a 17 GW UK hydrogen scenario with evaporative cooling would require 78 million tonnes per year of water, including 58.5 million tonnes from freshwater, equal to about 0.4% of nominally available groundwater and 0.1% of nominally usable surface water on the British mainland. (RMI water-consumption analysis) (DESNZ / Ricardo water-demand study)

That is why a national "hydrogen uses too much water" thesis is too blunt. The same DESNZ study stresses local and seasonal constraints, and a vendor white paper in the quarry puts water treatment at around 1% of electrolyzer-system capex. In many locations the larger gates remain electricity cost, renewable procurement, grid connection, demand formation, certification, subsidy qualification, and final investment decision. (EUROWATER white paper)

What would prove this wrong, and what to watch

The practical reading is narrower than a blanket "water is the bottleneck" thesis. The public record here is still concentrated in permit files, planning conditions, and supplier releases, not operating downtime disclosures. Even so, the loop can become a binding node at the margin, especially when projects move from pilot scale to 50-200 MW industrial sites, when cooling choices interact with local scarcity, when wastewater leaves through a sewer or protected estuary, and when OEMs require conductivity, TOC, silica, metals, fluoride, and resin data to preserve warranty.

The best monitoring signals are operational rather than rhetorical.

  • Permit records that force applicants to revise water-consumption ratios or discharge assumptions.
  • Planning conditions that make startup contingent on water-borne pollutant limits or estuary protection.
  • Procurement notices for RO-CEDI skids, service-DI tanks, resin replacement, and online analyzers.
  • Job postings for water-chemistry engineers, lab contractors, and wastewater operators at hydrogen sites.
  • Disclosed downtime, warranty exclusions, or stack degradation tied to feedwater impurities.

If those signals stay rare even as large projects reach operation, the thesis should weaken. If they become normal features of commissioning packs, warranty discussions, O&M contracts, and permit negotiations, then the relevant bottleneck may sit not in water in the abstract but in the certified loop that keeps the stack clean, the regulator satisfied, and the residual stream legal.
