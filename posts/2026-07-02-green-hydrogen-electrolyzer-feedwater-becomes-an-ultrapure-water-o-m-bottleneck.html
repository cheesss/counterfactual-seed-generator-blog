Green Hydrogen Electrolyzer Feedwater Becomes An Ultrapure-Water O&M Bottleneck

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 12 min read

The visible fight in green hydrogen is usually fought over power prices, electrolyzer factories, subsidy rules, and offtake contracts. Those are real gates. They also make it easy to miss a smaller control point that sits inside the plant boundary: the water loop that keeps the stack alive, proves compliance to the regulator, and disposes of the concentrate, purge, spent resins, and wastewater left behind.

The bottleneck shape is residual-handling. The scarce item is not water in the abstract. It is a qualified, monitored, permitted ultrapure-water loop that converts whatever local water is available into stack-grade feedwater, then proves where the residual stream goes. The adjacent shape is validation-evidence: OEM feedwater specifications, conductivity limits, water-balance calculations, discharge consents, and commissioning records decide whether the plant can run without damaging the electrolyzer or breaching a permit.

The chemistry is simple enough to invite bad analysis. Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen requires about 9 kilograms of water for 1 kilogram of hydrogen. That number is useful, but it understates the industrial system. A real plant must take potable water, surface water, groundwater, refinery effluent, recycled municipal water, or seawater and pass it through pretreatment, reverse osmosis, electrodeionization, mixed-bed ion exchange, polishing, storage, and a recirculating electrolyzer loop. The UK Environment Agency’s 2024 emerging-techniques guidance tells operators to track water consumption per kilogram of hydrogen, identify contaminants in purge and wastewater streams, segregate and treat emissions, and account for spent membranes, catalysts, seals, adsorbents, and ion-exchange resins. In other words, regulators are not just asking whether the project has water. They are asking whether the project can document a water system.

The Northfleet Hydrogen Production Facility in Gravesend is the cleanest small case. Green Hydrogen 3 Limited received an Environment Agency permit in February 2025 for a 15 MWe PEM plant supplying hydrogen by pipeline to the adjacent Northfleet Paper Mill. The permit decision shows the water loop becoming a live issue. The application initially described 4.2 cubic metres per hour of demineralized water to produce 280 kilograms per hour of hydrogen, a ratio of roughly 15 kilograms of water per kilogram of hydrogen. The Environment Agency challenged that number against the 9:1 chemical minimum. The manufacturer data then separated the loop: 3,456 litres per hour of input water, 864 litres per hour removed by purification, and 2,592 litres per hour of pure water consumed, bringing the feedwater ratio back to roughly 9.1 kilograms per kilogram of hydrogen. The decision document also records a feedwater conductivity requirement of less than 1 microsiemens per centimetre, discharge of reverse-osmosis effluent to foul sewer under Southern Water trade-effluent consent, and pre-operational conditions tied to final design and purification equipment.

That is not a headline-scale water shortage. It is more specific: a regulator forcing a project to reconcile water arithmetic, OEM specification, and waste-route documentation before operation. For capital markets, that is the kind of bottleneck that can sit below the materiality threshold in a model and still delay commissioning, shift EPC risk, or move margin to a specialist vendor.

The Humber H2ub Green project makes the same pattern larger and more visible. Uniper’s proposed 120 MWe plant at Killingholme would supply hydrogen to Phillips 66’s Humber Refinery. Planning documents describe six PEM electrolyzer units, about 21,000 normal cubic metres per hour of hydrogen at roughly 31.5 bar, and a future expansion path beyond 200 MWe. The project considered several water-source options: refinery effluent, other industrial effluent, or non-potable supply. It chose air coolers partly because water supply on the South Humber bank is limited. The application also describes a water-treatment plant, raw-water and demineralized-water storage tanks, and discharge of treatment wastewater and site drainage to the Humber Estuary via an existing outfall.

The important condition is not the electrolyzer order. ITM Power was selected in May 2025 to supply six 20 MW POSEIDON modules, with final investment decision expected in 2026 and operation targeted for 2029. The sharper water clue is the planning condition: operation cannot begin until the local planning authority receives either an Environmental Permit setting discharge limits for water-borne pollutants agreed with the Environment Agency and Natural England, or a Natural England statement that conservation objectives for the Humber Estuary special-area and special-protection sites will not be compromised. Here the water loop becomes estuary risk, effluent chemistry, and agency concurrence.

Orica’s Hunter Valley Hydrogen Hub is the strongest counterweight. It has recycled water at the center of the design, yet the observed delay was not primarily a water delay. The facility is integrated with Orica’s existing Kooragang Island ammonia operations in the Newcastle industrial port precinct. Orica announced on 1 July 2026 that it had taken final investment decision on a 50 MW first phase expected to produce 4,700 tonnes per year of renewable hydrogen and 26,600 tonnes per year of low-carbon ammonia, reduce natural-gas feedstock demand by about 7.5%, begin construction in 2026, and start production in early 2029. The project uses recycled water and renewable electricity. Its support stack includes AUD 432 million of ARENA Hydrogen Headstart funding, AUD 45 million from New South Wales, and AUD 70 million from the federal Regional Hydrogen Hubs program.

Origin Energy’s October 2024 exit from the same project was attributed to uncertainty around hydrogen-market development and capital-intensive project risk, not feedwater. Orica’s later decision to proceed after government revenue support says the largest live gate was commercial: power cost, offtake, funding, and risk allocation. The water system still matters, but as a solved prerequisite rather than the dominant constraint. That distinction matters. A useful bottleneck thesis has to survive cases where the constraint is present but not binding.

Shell’s Holland Hydrogen I points in the same direction. Shell describes a 200 MW Rotterdam plant under construction, powered by Hollandse Kust Noord offshore wind, expected to produce around 60 tonnes per day of hydrogen, with commissioning in late 2026 and ramp-up in 2027. Pure Water Group says thyssenkrupp nucera selected it to provide ultrapure-water generation for the same Nucera 200 MW electrolyzer package. This is the vendor-control version of the story: a water-treatment package becomes part of the electrolyzer supply chain, not a bespoke local crisis. If this modular pattern becomes standard, feedwater becomes a small recurring profit pool and risk-management service rather than a system-wide buildout choke point.

NEOM is the edge case that disciplines the argument. The Saudi project is orders of magnitude larger: Air Products, ACWA Power, and NEOM have described a facility producing up to 600 tonnes per day of hydrogen in the form of green ammonia, while thyssenkrupp nucera’s release describes a more-than-2 GW alkaline electrolysis plant based on 20 MW modules. Publicly available source material located in this pass did not show project-level evidence that ultrapure water is the gating item. For a coastal desert megaproject, desalination, brine management, marine permitting, and water legitimacy can matter. But the bigger public gates appear to be power buildout, electrolyzer delivery, ammonia synthesis, export logistics, financing, and offtake. If the water thesis has force, it will show up through ESIAs, desalination supply contracts, brine outfall permits, or operational water-quality records—not through generic statements that deserts are dry.

The vendor map is already forming. DuPont launched AmberLite P2X110 in 2023 as an ion-exchange resin designed for electrolyzer loops and describes a hydrogen water-treatment stack that includes ultrafiltration, reverse osmosis, electrodeionization, and ion-exchange polishing. Ecolab’s Purolite line markets UltraClean resins for PEM loops, with an explicit claim that PEM systems need ultrapure water quality above 18 Mohm-cm, equivalent to less than about 0.056 microsiemens per centimetre, and removal of calcium, magnesium, sodium, chloride, sulfate, silica, trace metals, fluoride, and organics. Xylem’s Evoqua service-deionization materials show the service model: portable activated-carbon and ion-exchange resin tanks, off-site regeneration, local service technicians, and no on-site chemical handling. Veolia’s hydrogen water-treatment pages offer packaged systems from small units to more than 500 MW and emphasize wastewater management, residual accumulation, monitoring, and maintenance. LANXESS markets Lewatit UltraPure resins and a three-stage PEM polisher with UV to control total-organic-carbon release.

Those nodes do not prove a large public-equity opportunity. They do show how value can shift away from the electrolyzer brand. A project owner cares about levelized hydrogen cost and uptime. The stack OEM cares about warranty and degradation. The regulator cares about water balance, pollutant limits, and the receiving sewer or estuary. The industrial water vendor controls the skid, resin, polishing design, service interval, and analytical evidence. The local water utility or outfall owner controls the residual route. The accredited lab or monitoring contractor controls the document trail. In a plant that needs to run at high utilization to amortize power and stack capex, a small impurity-control failure can become expensive even if the water package is a small fraction of capex.

The serious counterargument is strong. RMI estimates cumulative green-hydrogen water consumption at roughly 20 to 30 litres per kilogram of hydrogen after purification and process cooling, broadly comparable to or lower than many fossil-hydrogen pathways. A 2025 UK DESNZ-commissioned study found that even a 17 GW 2035 hydrogen scenario with evaporative cooling would require 78 million tonnes per year of water, including 58.5 million tonnes from freshwater, equal to about 0.4% of nominally available groundwater and 0.1% of nominally usable surface water on the British mainland. The same study stresses local and seasonal constraints, but nationally it does not support a simple “hydrogen uses too much water” claim. Another vendor white paper puts water treatment at around 1% of electrolyzer-system capex. In many locations the larger constraints remain electricity cost, renewable procurement, grid connection, demand formation, certification, subsidy qualification, and final investment decision.

The practical reading is narrower. Ultrapure feedwater is unlikely to stop the whole hydrogen economy on volume grounds. It can still become a binding node at the margin, especially where projects move from pilot scale to 50-200 MW industrial sites, where cooling choices interact with local scarcity, where wastewater leaves through a sewer or protected estuary, and where OEMs require conductivity, TOC, silica, metals, fluoride, and resin data to preserve warranty. It is a small industrial system with many owners. Small systems with many owners can be slow.

The signals worth monitoring are concrete. Permits that force applicants to revise water-consumption ratios. Planning conditions that make operation contingent on discharge limits. Procurement notices for RO-CEDI skids, service-deionization tanks, resin replacement, online conductivity analyzers, TOC analyzers, silica monitoring, and laboratory services. Job postings for water-chemistry engineers at hydrogen projects. Disclosed downtime tied to feedwater impurities or stack recirculation. OEM warranty language linking water quality to stack degradation. Shifts from evaporative cooling to air cooling in water-stressed clusters. Recycled-water tie-ins and trade-effluent consents. Vendor disclosures that hydrogen has moved from demonstration sales to installed-base service revenue.

The restrained conclusion is that feedwater should be treated neither as a media myth nor as the central global limit. The raw input is cheap. The qualified loop is not trivial. The more hydrogen plants resemble ordinary chemical plants, the more the decisive documents will look like ordinary chemical-plant documents: discharge consents, water balances, lab certificates, service contracts, and pre-operational conditions. The hidden question is not whether the industry can find water. It is whether each plant can keep a certified water loop clean enough, long enough, and documented enough to protect the stack, satisfy the regulator, and avoid turning a low-cost consumable into a commissioning gate.
