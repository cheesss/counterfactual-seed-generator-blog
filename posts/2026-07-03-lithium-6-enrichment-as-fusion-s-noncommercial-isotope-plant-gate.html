Fusion’s next bottleneck may be the blanket, not the plasma

Fusion’s visible milestones are plasma temperature, magnetic confinement, laser gain, grid interconnection and power-purchase agreements. Those milestones determine whether a reactor concept can become a credible power project. But they do not close the deuterium-tritium fuel cycle.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-03 · 16 min read

A self-sufficient D-T power plant must replace the tritium it consumes. It must direct fusion neutrons into a lithium-bearing blanket, breed enough new tritium after accounting for losses, extract it continuously, prevent unacceptable leakage, measure the inventory and return the fuel to the reactor.

That is a different industrial problem from producing a high-performance plasma. It combines controlled isotope feedstock, blanket manufacturing, fusion-relevant neutron testing, tritium processing, instrumentation, material accountancy, safety engineering and regulatory evidence.

The resulting thesis is narrower than a claim that lithium-6 will delay every fusion machine. Experimental devices can use external tritium, and other constraints may fail first. The gate emerges when a developer moves from an externally fuelled demonstration toward repeatable, self-sufficient D-T electricity production. At that point, the relevant constraint is the complete blanket fuel-cycle qualification chain; enriched lithium-6 is one potentially scarce input to that chain.

The blanket turns lithium into a fuel-cycle system

Natural lithium contains approximately 7.5 atom percent lithium-6. Many prominent blanket concepts raise that concentration because lithium-6 has a favourable tritium-producing reaction with neutrons. Enrichment above natural abundance also moves the material into nuclear export-control regimes in the United States and China. (U.S. export controls)

This means the relevant product is not ordinary lithium carbonate from the battery chain. It is isotopically specified material with controlled handling, assay documentation, chemical-form requirements, licensing constraints and a qualification history tied to a particular blanket design.

The quantities may also resemble reactor-core inventory rather than conventional annual fuel consumption. A 2024 TU Eindhoven study estimated 46.4 tonnes of 60%-enriched lithium for an HCPB solid breeder, 118.6 tonnes of 90%-enriched lithium for an ARC-type FLiBe concept, 152.8 tonnes for a WCLL design and 155.8 tonnes for a HELIAS DCLL design. These are study outputs, not disclosed procurement requirements from the reactor developers themselves. The four estimates use different blanket architectures and enrichment assumptions; they establish scale, not a universal specification.

The historical U.S. solution was COLEX, a chemical-exchange process operated at the Y-12 complex in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. Between 1950 and 1963, the programme used approximately 11 million kilograms of mercury. ORNL reports that about 3% was lost into buildings and the surrounding environment, creating a remediation burden that continued long after production ended. (ORNL mercury history)

COLEX therefore demonstrates that industrial-scale lithium isotope separation is physically possible. It does not provide an easy template for civilian fusion. The process belonged to a Cold War system with strategic urgency, state financing, secrecy, large material inventories and environmental tolerances that would be difficult to reproduce today.

Mercury-free alternatives are advancing, but the public evidence remains at the research or pilot stage. Hexium and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory are adapting atomic-vapour laser isotope separation to lithium. Marathon Fusion’s partial-ionisation centrifuge programme remains small-scale R&D and model validation. Molten Salt Solutions received an NSF Phase I award for a liquid-liquid separation route using high-speed counter-current chromatography. None has publicly reported independently assayed lithium-6 production at one tonne per year.

Figures are drawn from ITER, UKAEA, DOE and company disclosures, together with awarded UK procurement records.

ITER’s current programme covers four blanket concepts intended for two equatorial ports. Its purpose is to test breeder materials together with cooling, tritium extraction and processing systems. ITER itself states that large-scale tritium production and recycling still require demonstration. (ITER tritium breeding)

The United Kingdom’s LIBRTI programme is more directly aligned with private commercial timelines. UKAEA plans a dedicated high-output neutron source, shielded experimental infrastructure, multitonne test articles, tritium diagnostics, digital modelling and facilities for hazardous-material handling. Its strategy targets building completion and the start of neutron-source installation in March 2028, integrated breeder rigs and initial data in 2029, and full international-user operation by 2030.

The programme’s total stated investment is £220 million, including £180 million in the current five-year allocation. Those figures describe overlapping programme scopes rather than additive budgets. Earlier public references used approximately £200 million; the current framing is £220 million total investment.

UKAEA has already translated the programme into discrete industrial packages. SHINE received a £34.7 million contract for a 14.1 MeV neutron source, including commissioning, training, future upgrade work and maintenance obligations. A distributed-control-system award was approximately £518,000 excluding VAT. A liquid-scintillation-counter award for tritium accountancy was £708,620. A detritiation-system procurement carried an estimated value of £2 million.

These contracts matter because they show the blanket problem becoming modular and procurable. The programme is no longer described only through scientific objectives. It is buying neutron availability, control architecture, analytical instrumentation, cleanup systems, continuous monitoring and operating support.

ARC exposes the gap between demand and qualification

Commonwealth Fusion Systems provides the clearest commercial case. Its proposed ARC plant in Chesterfield County, Virginia, targets approximately 400 MW. Google signed an agreement for 200 MW from the first plant, with an option associated with later projects. Eni announced an offtake agreement valued at more than $1 billion.

Those agreements demonstrate customer demand and strategic capital. They do not disclose the lithium chemical form, lithium-6 enrichment level, initial blanket inventory, start-up tritium allocation, isotope supplier, export-control pathway or accepted blanket-qualification test.

CFS states that ARC will use a liquid blanket and breed tritium. That remains a design claim rather than demonstrated plant performance. SPARC does not include a breeding system, and CFS is developing the blanket work separately for ARC.

Its LIBRTI partnership makes the sequencing explicit. CFS will manufacture representative test articles, help develop experimental protocols and use the facility to pursue evidence of net tritium production. (CFS joins LIBRTI)

The arrangement is a selective make-buy strategy. CFS retains blanket design and test-article fabrication while relying on UKAEA for the scarce neutron environment and qualification infrastructure. It avoids duplicating a large national test facility, but its commercial schedule becomes partially dependent on the facility’s construction, commissioning, experimental queue and acceptance standards.

The clock is tight rather than necessarily impossible. LIBRTI targets initial integrated data in 2029 and full operation in 2030. ARC targets the early 2030s. That leaves limited schedule margin between generating first engineering-scale evidence and using that evidence in licensing, procurement, financing and plant construction.

The historical base rate points to sovereign infrastructure

The closest direct precedent is Y-12. The United States solved lithium-6 enrichment through a vertically controlled state programme, not through a competitive commercial isotope market. The government accepted large capital requirements, controlled distribution and substantial environmental liabilities because the material served a strategic defence objective.

The tritium precedent is similar. Heavy-water fission reactors accumulated tritium that could support ITER’s experimental campaign. That arrangement can supply a limited flagship programme without establishing a scalable fuel market for many commercial fusion plants.

The lesson is not that fusion will repeat COLEX. It is that strategic isotope constraints have historically been resolved through sovereign capacity, inventories and allocation. If lithium-6 becomes urgent before private mercury-free processes mature, governments may bridge the gap through national laboratories or tightly controlled procurement.

DOE’s July 2026 announcement that ORNL had restored scaled domestic stable-isotope enrichment is therefore relevant. ORNL now has electromagnetic and gas-centrifuge capabilities, with further infrastructure scheduled for phased operation beginning in 2028. DOE did not announce lithium-6 production, assay, annual capacity or fusion allocations. The development creates an enabling platform, not evidence that reactor-scale supply already exists. (DOE isotope expansion)

This base rate also limits the simple commercial scarcity thesis. A genuine bottleneck may create public spending and strategic control without producing broad private pricing power. Governments may absorb the fixed cost, restrict the customer base and allocate material according to policy rather than an open market.

Spend is moving before the fuel is

The most visible revenue today is not lithium-6 sales. It is publicly funded infrastructure, engineering and equipment revenue tied to qualification.

The SHINE contract combines source manufacture, commissioning, training, upgrade development and maintenance. That structure points toward a large initial equipment award followed by availability and service obligations. Tritium diagnostics create potential recurring demand for calibration, detector replacement, monitoring software and radiation-protection services. Accountancy instrumentation adds consumables, assay methods and traceability requirements.

A similar pattern applies to control systems and data. LIBRTI intends to connect design models, test-article configurations, neutron exposure, sensor readings, tritium material balances and model calibration. UKAEA targets a tritium-output prediction platform by July 2027 and integrated experimental data in 2029.

Control may therefore accumulate around validated datasets and accepted workflows rather than any single piece of hardware. A supplier that owns calibration histories, model-to-test correlations or regulator-recognised configurations can become harder to replace than one selling a detector or pump.

UKAEA and Eni’s RH3OVA venture extends this logic into services. The venture is intended to provide fuel-cycle work from feasibility studies through engineering, deployment and operational support. No customers, backlog, pricing or revenue have been disclosed, so it should be treated as a commercialisation vehicle rather than an established business.

Public-market exposure remains indirect. Alphabet is an ARC offtaker and CFS investor. Eni is an investor, offtaker and fuel-cycle partner. These relationships expose them to the success or failure of ARC milestones, but they are not evidence of direct lithium-6 supplier economics. The current supplier layer is dominated by government facilities, private fusion and isotope companies, and conventional nuclear instrumentation or engineering vendors.

Who controls the constraint

Control is distributed across several institutions.

Reactor developers control blanket architecture, chemical form, target enrichment, inventory and plant integration. Their decisions determine whether lithium-6 is needed at all and in what quantity.

National laboratories and governments control legacy inventories, isotope infrastructure, strategic funding and security policy. DOE, NNSA, ORNL and Y-12 therefore retain influence even without a disclosed commercial lithium-6 plant.

Export-control authorities control cross-border movement of enriched material and some isotope-separation equipment. The rules are established; the unresolved question is whether licensing produces material schedule delays for civilian fusion.

UKAEA and ITER control scarce test environments and help define the evidence architecture. Their protocols may influence what measurements become credible to regulators, insurers, customers and independent engineers.

Equipment and service vendors control subsystems such as neutron sources, tritium monitors, detritiation units, analytical instruments, control systems and remote-handling equipment. Their leverage will depend on whether contracts attach recurring maintenance, calibration, data or operational responsibilities.

No public document yet shows meaningful risk transfer for the integrated blanket. Reactor developers and governments still appear to retain most performance, leakage, delivery and qualification risk. There is no public blanket warranty, tritium-breeding guarantee, isotope-delivery indemnity, fusion-specific insurance wording or non-recourse financing package.

That missing contractual layer is as important as the missing scientific result. A technology becomes commercially repeatable when counterparties can specify acceptance tests, assign liability and price failure.

The counterargument is stronger than a simple scarcity story

Lithium-6 may never become the first binding constraint.

SPARC-like demonstrations can operate without breeding. Start-up tritium may constrain projects before blanket material does. Plasma performance, component lifetime, neutron damage, remote maintenance, grid interconnection and project finance could delay commercial plants long enough for isotope capacity to scale gradually.

Natural-lithium blanket designs also weaken any universal enrichment claim. Recent conceptual work has reported calculated tritium-breeding ratios that could be viable without enriched lithium-6. These designs have not yet demonstrated integrated performance under plant-relevant neutron exposure, but they create a credible engineering route around the enrichment problem.

A state-backed supply bridge is another possibility. Existing inventories, new ORNL capability or classified programmes could support one or two first-generation plants without creating a visible commercial market. Public forecasts based only on disclosed capacity would then overstate physical scarcity.

Finally, no authoritative record identified in the research shows a named fusion project already cancelled or delayed because a lithium-6 contract failed. The evidence supports a sequential risk for self-sufficient power plants, not a proven historical cause of fusion delays.

The strongest surviving thesis is therefore about qualification. Even a natural-lithium blanket must prove breeding after realistic losses, continuous extraction, containment, accountancy and safe operation. A new isotope supplier could relieve feedstock risk while leaving the integrated fuel-cycle problem unresolved.

What would prove this wrong

The lithium-6 component would weaken sharply if a natural-lithium blanket demonstrated sufficient net breeding margin under fusion-relevant neutron exposure, including structural penalties, extraction losses and continuous material accountancy.

The feedstock constraint would also weaken if a mercury-free supplier disclosed independently assayed lithium-6 production of at least one tonne per year, repeat deliveries, environmental approval and binding customer contracts.

The broader qualification thesis would fail if a commercial D-T plant reached licensing and financed construction without material blanket-evidence requirements, sovereign supply intervention or unusual risk allocation.

A slower fusion timeline could also neutralise the issue. If plasma, neutron-damaged materials or project finance shift commercial operation well beyond the early 2030s, enrichment and testing infrastructure may mature before demand becomes binding.

What to watch

  • By June 30, 2027, a tender or supplier award for LIBRTI tritium-safety diagnostics.
  • By December 31, 2027, final NRC fusion-machine rules and possession-licensing guidance.
  • By March 31, 2028, documented LIBRTI readiness for neutron-source installation.
  • By December 31, 2029, an integrated blanket test reporting quantified net tritium performance.
  • Through 2029, independently assayed mercury-free lithium-6 capacity reaching one tonne per year.

The commercial fusion story will continue to be led by plasma milestones, plant announcements and electricity contracts. But developers that clear those visible gates will inherit a harder systems task: converting a lithium-bearing blanket into a continuously operating, measured, licensable and financeable fuel cycle.

That transition is where physical infrastructure, strategic isotope policy and commercial risk allocation meet. Until a representative blanket demonstrates net tritium production and a credible procurement route, the fuel cycle remains an unfinished part of the power plant rather than a routine subsystem.
