The Hospital Capacity Bottleneck Hiding in a Bag of Salt Water

Hospitals usually describe capacity in beds, nurses, operating rooms, ventilators and reimbursement. Hurricane Helene exposed a less visible limit: the validated industrial system behind a bag of sterile fluid.

Normal saline is simple chemistry. The commercial product is not. It depends on water-for-injection systems, sterile films and ports, high-throughput filling lines, sterilization, leak testing, quality release, expiration dating, distribution contracts and a presentation that clinicians can use safely with the hospital’s administration equipment.

That distinction matters because ordinary demand can be served by a concentrated, efficient manufacturing network. A major outage tests something harder: qualified substitutability at scale. The relevant question is not whether another company knows how to mix salt and water. It is whether another approved site can produce the required solution, concentration, bag size and interface—and whether the hospital has a contract that lets it buy the product when the primary supplier is allocating inventory.

Hurricane Helene turned that abstract issue into a national operating problem. Baxter’s North Cove, North Carolina facility supplied about 60% of U.S. IV-solution volume and reportedly produced about 1.5 million bags per day before flooding stopped production.

The resulting shortage did not close hospitals outright. It reduced their optionality. Pharmacy teams changed order sets. Clinicians substituted oral hydration or smaller volumes where possible. Supply-chain teams chased allocations. Some providers deferred procedures. Capacity was rationed through hundreds of operational decisions rather than a single dramatic failure.

The first signal was allocation, not price

On October 9, 2024, Baxter raised allocations for identified high-demand IV fluids from 40% to 60% of normal for direct customers and from 10% to 60% for distributors. That was an improvement, but it showed how the market was functioning. Hospitals were not simply paying more and sourcing equivalent bags from a liquid spot market. Their access was being administratively rationed through contracted channels.

Premier surveyed up to 257 provider respondents on October 7 and 8, with question-level denominators varying. More than 86% reported IV-fluid shortages. Eighty-eight percent said they were receiving less than half of requested orders, while 54% held ten days or less of inventory against a typical range of 15 to 22 days. Seventeen percent reported that they had already canceled elective surgeries or other procedures.

Those numbers do not establish a precise nationwide loss of surgical capacity. They do establish the transmission mechanism: one concentrated manufacturing outage became lower hospital inventory, conservation protocols and procedure risk within days.

The federal response also reveals the industrial character of the shortage. FDA authorized temporary importation of specified 250 mL, 500 mL and 1,000 mL sodium-chloride bags from Baxter’s Shanghai operation even though those products were not ordinarily approved for U.S. distribution. FDA also permitted shelf-life extensions for affected Baxter inventory covering more than 50 IV and irrigation product codes manufactured before the end of September 2024. (FDA import authorization)

These were not ordinary purchasing decisions. They were emergency mechanisms for a regulated product whose supply could not be recreated quickly inside hospitals or shifted automatically to an alternate factory.

The bottleneck moved below the finished bag

Shortages often migrate upstream. In December 2024, FDA added empty IV containers, product code KPE, to the Medical Device Shortages List, with the shortage then expected to last through March 2025. ASHP warned that compounding saline from sterile water and concentrated sodium chloride was labor-intensive and could worsen shortages in the concentrated ingredient itself.

The fallback therefore had its own stack:

1. empty sterile containers; 2. sterile water and concentrated sodium chloride; 3. pharmacy cleanroom capacity and trained labor; 4. quality procedures and infection-control safeguards; 5. administration sets, ports and product-specific operating changes.

A University of Utah presentation hosted by the National Academies added a commercial layer. It described hospitals commonly buying fluids from one supplier, sometimes within broader pump-and-tubing arrangements, and noted that suppliers may not serve non-contracted buyers during shortages. That evidence does not prove universal technical incompatibility between brands. It does show that physical product availability and practical hospital access are different things.

The control point is therefore not one machine. It is the full path from approved production to usable procurement.

Figures draw on company disclosures, FDA and health-agency records, Reuters reporting and the cited industry materials.

B. Braun said it moved 60 truckloads containing 70,000 cases of IV solutions away from Hurricane Milton’s path. It expected production at Irvine and Daytona Beach to rise by 20% and announced more than 30 million additional IV sets annually from Allentown. Those figures show active relief capacity, but the realized production increase and SKU-level availability were not independently disclosed. (B. Braun supply update)

Reuters reported that Fresenius Kabi had recently begun IV-solution manufacturing at Wilson and was evaluating faster startup of additional lines. Fresenius now publicly markets the site’s ability to fill 50 mL through 1,000 mL freeflex bags. What remains unknown is the commercially relevant number: uncommitted, approved capacity available to customers during another large outage.

Relief arrived before permanent redundancy

North Cove returned to pre-hurricane production in February 2025. FDA formally ended the national shortage of 0.9% sodium-chloride IV solutions on August 8, 2025. Empty IV containers were no longer present on FDA’s June 2026 shortage list. Australia’s regulator reported stable supply across all IV-fluid presentations by April 2026.

These outcomes are the strongest case against treating saline as a continuously binding bottleneck. The system adapted through plant repair, conservation, alternate suppliers, imports, shelf-life extensions and regulatory flexibility. It did not need to wait for a second North Cove-sized U.S. plant to come online.

That is why the surviving thesis must be narrower. The United States does not have an ongoing national saline shortage. The unresolved issue is whether the response created a repeatable resilience architecture or merely restored the old structure.

The distinction will become visible during the next material disruption. If hospitals switch to matching alternate products through existing contracts without allocations, emergency imports, shelf-life extensions or procedure deferrals, the system has achieved qualified substitutability. If the same emergency tools return, the concentration problem remains structural.

Resilience is becoming a service, but only at an early stage

In July 2025, Vizient added IV fluids supplied by Baxter to its Reserve program. Vizient described dedicated manufacturer inventory, U.S. warehousing, inventory management, reporting and logistics. That is more concrete than a policy recommendation: it creates a commercial route through which hospitals can purchase access to reserved supply. (Vizient Reserve)

HIRC has created another layer by assessing supplier resilience across inventory, logistics, visibility, supplier management and contingency planning. Baxter received Gold status for IV Solutions, Nutrition Solutions and Premix Drugs in 2025.

These developments mark early deployment, not repeatable procurement. The public record does not identify participating hospitals, covered product codes, reserve quantities, prices, release events, renewals or priority during manufacturer-wide allocation. A qualification badge indicates process maturity, not guaranteed supply. A reserve announcement indicates a purchasing route, not proven unit economics.

The next commercial step is not another partnership press release. It is evidence that a named health system has paid for a defined reserve or qualified two suppliers for a specific presentation and can execute that arrangement under stress.

The value may stay with incumbents

A resilience market could redirect spending toward inventory reservation, warehousing, rotation, supplier-risk software, regulatory preparation and emergency logistics. But that does not mean a large new margin pool will form outside the incumbent supply chain.

Baxter already combines IV solutions with infusion systems and administration sets. A reserve program can deepen that integrated relationship rather than displace it. Vizient can control the contracting layer. HIRC can influence qualification standards. Warehousing providers can earn service revenue. Yet much of that activity may be bundled, passed through or absorbed inside existing commercial relationships.

ICU Medical provides a useful warning against equating strategic activity with market expansion. In 2025, it transferred 60% of its IV-solutions business to Otsuka Pharmaceutical Factory America for $211.2 million and retained 40%, along with manufacturing, logistics and administrative service relationships. The transaction shifted ownership and risk. It did not create additional hospital demand.

ICU also characterized the transferred IV-solutions operation as lower margin. Its consolidated gross margin rose after the transaction. That suggests the most defensible economics may sit in integrated devices, services, data, contracting and reserved access—not in undifferentiated bags.

Baxter’s insurance recoveries make a second distinction. The company recorded $251 million of recoveries related to Helene, transferring part of the plant-level loss to insurers. Hospitals, however, were not compensated through that mechanism for pharmacy labor, emergency sourcing or canceled procedures. Manufacturer business-interruption insurance and customer supply assurance are separate products.

The historical base rate argues for caution

The closest precedent is the saline shortage surrounding Hurricane Maria in 2017 and 2018. A New England Journal of Medicine perspective reported that saline had already been constrained before the hurricane, that the market had few suppliers and that U.S. demand exceeded 40 million bags per month at the time. FDA used imports and shelf-life extensions, while Baxter restored affected operations. (NEJM perspective)

The similarity is structural: concentrated sterile manufacturing, a weather shock, limited rapid substitution and reliance on regulatory relief.

The difference is the affected product mix and response architecture. Hurricane Maria disrupted Puerto Rico production associated with smaller-volume saline products. Helene hit a large-volume IV-fluid and peritoneal-dialysis hub in North Carolina and triggered more explicit federal logistics support, supplier allocation management and reserve-program development.

Pfizer’s Rocky Mount tornado in 2023 offers another contra-case. The facility produced sterile injectables rather than the same large-volume fluid mix, but it showed that a serious physical disaster at a North Carolina sterile-manufacturing site could be remediated and commercial production restored over a period of months. Australia’s 2024–2026 response likewise showed that national modelling, conservation and imported alternatives could stabilize IV-fluid supply without permanent domestic overbuild.

The base rate therefore supports vulnerability, but not a claim of permanent scarcity.

The strongest counterargument is economic

The system may already have enough capacity for normalized demand. Hospital conservation practices persisted after supply returned, reducing IV-fluid usage and weakening the business case for idle manufacturing lines.

Under this interpretation, the missing ingredient is not technical capability. It is a payer willing to support spare capacity that may sit unused during normal years.

Hospitals want resilience but face storage costs, expiry waste, contracting complexity and pressure to minimize supply expense. Manufacturers operate a bulky, low-margin product and cannot justify large amounts of idle capacity without committed revenue. Government can require risk-management plans and coordinate emergency relief, but FDA’s detailed shortage-risk-management guidance remains draft.

The commercial equilibrium may therefore remain concentrated production plus conservation and emergency intervention. That model can be socially uncomfortable and still be economically stable.

This is also why the public evidence does not yet support a broad “value migration” narrative. Reserved inventory, data and logistics may grow, but incumbents can retain the customer, contract and device relationship. Adoption can occur without margin moving far from the existing system.

What to watch

  • December 31, 2027: Vizient, Baxter or a provider discloses a named IV-reserve participant, SKU, renewal or draw.
  • December 31, 2027: FDA’s 2022 drug-shortage risk-management guidance remains draft or becomes final.
  • December 31, 2027: Any FDA 0.9% saline large-volume shortage persists for 90 consecutive days.
  • December 31, 2028: A named U.S. buyer discloses SKU-level dual sourcing or dedicated IV-fluid reserve access.
  • December 31, 2029: A manufacturer documents one million bags per day of qualified, geographically independent spare U.S. capacity.

The strategic asset is not saline itself. It is the ability to replace a failed site with approved product that hospitals can actually procure and use.

Helene proved that this ability was weak in 2024. The recovery proved that emergency coordination can compensate for it. The next disruption will determine whether the United States built a new resilience system—or simply repaired the old one.
