The Hospital Capacity Bottleneck Hiding in a Bag of Salt Water

Hospital capacity is usually described in visible terms: beds, nurses, operating rooms, ventilators, and reimbursement. Those are real constraints. But the 2024 IV-fluid shortage showed that a hospital can also be throttled by something far less dramatic: a validated bag of sterile salt water.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-22 · 10 min read

  • North Cove supplied roughly 60% of U.S. IV fluids and PD solutions.
  • The plant produced about 1.5 million bags a day.

  • FDA declared the saline shortage resolved in August 2025.

A bag of salt water is not a headline constraint

That sounds too mundane to matter. Normal saline is cheap, bulky, familiar, and everywhere. It is not a breakthrough drug or a scarce biologic. It does not have the glamour of oncology, AI diagnostics, robotic surgery, or hospital-at-home software. Yet it sits underneath almost everything hospitals do: surgery, anesthesia, emergency care, dialysis, drug dilution, hydration, labor and delivery, infusion clinics, and basic inpatient care. When IV fluid becomes scarce, capacity does not disappear all at once. It becomes rationed through conservation protocols, allocation percentages, delayed procedures, changed order sets, and pharmacy workarounds.

Hurricane Helene exposed the concentration

Hurricane Helene made that hidden dependency visible. Flooding shut Baxter's North Cove, North Carolina facility, and the CDC described the site as the largest U.S. manufacturer of peritoneal dialysis and IV solutions. Reuters and the American Hospital Association later put the concentration more plainly: North Cove accounted for roughly 60% of U.S. IV fluids and peritoneal dialysis solutions, or about 1.5 million bags a day. (CDC advisory) (Reuters, August 2025) (American Hospital Association, August 2025)

The important point is not that saline is hard chemistry. It is not. The bottleneck is the industrial system that turns sterile water, salt, plastic film, ports, tubing interfaces, form-fill-seal lines, sterilization, leak testing, particulate control, quality release, expiration dating, hospital contracts, and distribution into a product clinicians trust enough to put directly into patients. That system is efficient in normal times because it is concentrated, high-volume, and tightly validated. The same features make it brittle when a single site goes offline.

Allocation and the emergency response

The first place the shortage appeared was allocation. In October 2024, Reuters reported that Baxter raised allocations of high-demand IV fluids to 60% of normal from 40% for direct customers, and to 60% from 10% for distributors. That was an improvement, but the language itself is the tell. The market was not clearing through price, nor was supply instantly replaced by competitors. Hospitals were being told, through distributor channels and group purchasing organization contracts, how much of a basic input they could receive. (Reuters, October 2024)

What controlled access was not hospital demand alone but the paid, validated path from plant to bedside: large-volume parenteral lines, sterile bags and other containers, compatible IV sets and pump setups, then the GPO and distributor channels that decide which substitute product a hospital can actually buy.

That is why the shortage could spill from finished saline into empty IV containers, and why dual sourcing is harder than adding a second vendor name to a contract. A hospital may know another manufacturer has product, but if the bag format, tubing, or procurement path does not fit, the practical shortage remains. (U.S. FDA device shortage bulletin) (National Academies workshop material)

Premier's provider survey captured how quickly that moved from factory outage to clinical operations. In an October 7-8 survey of 257 provider respondents, more than 86% reported IV-fluid shortages, 88% said they were receiving less than half their requested orders, and 54% had 10 days or less of inventory compared with a typical 15 to 22 days. Seventeen percent had already canceled elective surgeries or other procedures; another 58% were considering cancellations if the situation did not improve. This is what a hidden industrial bottleneck looks like inside a hospital: not a headline collapse, but a quiet shrinking of optionality. (Premier provider survey)

The federal response also shows what kind of bottleneck this was. The CDC told providers to assess inventory, conserve fluids, use oral alternatives where appropriate, consider other IV options, and review standing orders. FDA allowed temporary importation of sodium chloride bags from Baxter's Shanghai facility, even though the letter made clear that those specific products were not FDA-approved in the ordinary U.S. distribution channel. Virginia's health department noted FDA authorization for Baxter to extend use dates on more than 50 IV and irrigation product codes by up to 12 months. (U.S. FDA import authorization) (Virginia Department of Health update)

Why redundancy takes months, not days

Those are not normal procurement moves. They are emergency measures for a supply chain where the missing object is regulated, heavy, sterile, and hard to substitute quickly. A hospital can conserve, compound in limited cases, import under authorization, extend expiration dates, change clinical protocols, and shift to alternative products. But none of those actions is the same as creating a new validated large-volume parenteral plant.

The shortage then moved upstream and sideways. In December 2024, FDA's device center added empty IV containers, product code KPE, to the Medical Device Shortages List, with the shortage expected through March 2025. ASHP's fluid-shortage guidance warned that compounding sodium chloride from sterile water and concentrated sodium chloride is labor-intensive and can worsen the concentrated sodium chloride shortage. This is the pattern to watch in many industrial systems: when the obvious finished product is short, the true constraint often migrates to containers, components, quality systems, labor, documentation, or compatibility. (U.S. FDA device shortage bulletin) (ASHP conservation guidance)

Baxter's recovery timeline is useful because it shows why redundancy cannot be summoned in a weekend. Medical Design & Outsourcing reported that a normal day at North Cove involved about 90 truckloads leaving the facility, and that Baxter moved more than 450 truckloads of product from the site within three weeks after the storm. (Medical Design & Outsourcing recovery report)

With FDA permission, Baxter ramped production at facilities in Canada, China, Ireland, and the U.K. A high-speed line representing roughly one-quarter of pre-storm production restarted by the end of October; a second line brought the site to about 50% of pre-storm production in mid-November; eight of ten lines were running by year-end; the last two restarted in January; and the plant returned to pre-hurricane production in February 2025. The company estimated the recovery required more than 2.5 million labor hours. (Medical Design & Outsourcing recovery report)

Alternative suppliers, and a familiar history

Alternative suppliers mattered, but they did not make the concentration irrelevant. B. Braun said it moved 60 truckloads, or 70,000 cases, of IV solutions north of Florida before Hurricane Milton and was increasing production at Irvine, California and Daytona Beach, Florida by 20%. It also said it had invested well over $1 billion in U.S. capacity over the prior five years and would boost annual IV set output from Allentown, Pennsylvania by more than 30 million sets. (B. Braun supply update)

Reuters reported that Fresenius Kabi had recently started IV-solution manufacturing at Wilson, North Carolina and was exploring ways to accelerate additional lines. That is meaningful relief. It is not the same as instantly replacing 1.5 million bags a day. (Reuters on Fresenius Kabi)

There is also history here. A 2018 New England Journal of Medicine perspective on saline shortages after Hurricane Maria made many of the same points. Saline was already in shortage before the hurricane. Baxter's Puerto Rico plant made small-volume saline bags. The market had only a few suppliers, and the others could not quickly replace the deficit. The U.S. needed more than 40 million bags of saline a month, and imports were costly because saline is heavy and bulky. The details differ, but the structure repeats: cheap generic sterile products can have expensive, concentrated, slow-to-replace manufacturing systems. (New England Journal of Medicine perspective)

The crisis ended; the structural risk didn't

The strongest counterargument is that the acute crisis did end. FDA announced in August 2025 that the national shortage of 0.9% sodium chloride IV solutions was resolved, and the American Hospital Association relayed that decision to hospitals. Baxter's North Cove plant had already returned to pre-hurricane production in February 2025. (Reuters, August 2025) (American Hospital Association, August 2025) (Medical Design & Outsourcing recovery report)

The dates split into two clocks. Acute restoration happened within months: North Cove was back to pre-hurricane production in February 2025, and FDA closed the saline shortage in August 2025.

Redundancy buildout is slower. B. Braun framed its response alongside more than $1 billion of U.S. capacity investment over five years, and Fresenius was still trying to accelerate additional Wilson lines rather than instantly replacing North Cove. The emergency can end before the system has built much spare depth. (B. Braun supply update) (Reuters on Fresenius Kabi)

That matters. The lesson is not that hospitals are permanently out of saline. The lesson is that sterile-fluid capacity behaves more like critical infrastructure than like an ordinary commodity. The bag is cheap; the validated system behind it is not.

If the economics continue to favor concentrated production, thin inventories, single-supplier contracts, and minimal disaster redundancy, the next shortage does not need a new theory. This one began with a storm and then spread into empty-container shortages, allocation rules, and compatibility problems between bags, tubing, pumps, and procurement channels.

What to watch

  • FDA and ASHP shortage notices for saline, lactated Ringer's, dextrose, sterile water, irrigation fluids, and empty IV containers, plus spillover signs in tubing compatibility or peritoneal-dialysis supply.
  • Supplier allocation percentages, hospital days on hand, and elective-procedure cancellations.
  • Emergency import authorizations, expiration-date extensions, and announced capex for U.S. large-volume parenteral lines.
  • Whether hospitals move from single-supplier fluid contracts toward real dual sourcing without breaking pump and tubing compatibility.

The strategic asset is not salt water. It is resilient, geographically redundant, regulator-approved sterile-fluid capacity, plus the contract and compatibility layers that let that capacity reach patients. A hospital system can look well supplied in beds and staff and still find itself constrained by a bag that normally disappears into the background. That is exactly why it is worth paying attention to.
