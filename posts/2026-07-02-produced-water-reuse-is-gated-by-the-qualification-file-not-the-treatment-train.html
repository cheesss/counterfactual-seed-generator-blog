Produced-Water Reuse Is Gated By The Qualification File, Not The Treatment Train

The visible story in produced-water reuse is desalination: oilfield brine goes into a treatment train, clean water comes out, and the Permian Basin gains a new water source for farms, power plants, data centers, construction, or manufacturing.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 13 min read

The first tell is that this looks like a desalination story

The visible story in produced-water reuse is desalination: oilfield brine goes into a treatment train, clean water comes out, and the Permian Basin gains a new water source for farms, power plants, data centers, construction, or manufacturing. That story is directionally right, but it skips the harder gate. A membrane, evaporator, distiller, or hybrid treatment system can make better water. It does not automatically make qualified water.

The tighter constraint is the evidence file that lets a regulator, industrial customer, landowner, insurer, and local community accept a specific produced-water stream for a specific use. That file is not a generic water-quality certificate. It is a chain of source characterization, treatment reliability, toxicity testing, residual-handling proof, monitoring obligations, liability allocation, and customer specification. In oilfield recycling, the customer often accepts treated water back into the wellbore. In off-field reuse, the water enters someone else’s cooling loop, soil, reservoir, construction process, or liability perimeter. The bottleneck moves from “can this be desalinated?” to “who will stand behind this water every day?”

Named cases show where the constraint moves

That distinction explains why the most important near-term projects look more like qualification campaigns than water-supply projects. Western Midstream’s second Permian produced-water treatment pilot, announced in June 2026 near Red Bluff Reservoir in Reeves County, Texas, is designed to receive about 2,000 barrels per day of produced water and produce about 1,000 barrels per day of reclaimed freshwater.

The headline could be the volume, but the disclosed scale is tiny relative to the water system around it. Western says it handles about 3.0 million barrels per day of produced water; its water-solutions page lists more than 130 saltwater-disposal wells, over 3.8 million barrels per day of disposal capacity, a Pathfinder Pipeline capable of moving up to 800,000 barrels per day, and recycling capacity up to 600,000 barrels per day.

The pilot’s strategic output is not the 1,000 barrels per day. It is the operating record: Western says the earlier JIP1 effort generated more than 50,000 data points over 24 months to demonstrate consistent water quality for industrial cooling, irrigation, and surface discharge. Source: https://investors.westernmidstream.com/2026-06-17-Western-Midstream-Announces-Start-up-of-Second-Produced-Water-Treatment-Facility-in-the-Permian-Basin and https://www.westernmidstream.com/water-solutions/

That is how the physical system works. Produced water is not a homogeneous brine. It varies by basin, well, completion chemistry, reservoir, age, oil-water separation practice, and recycling history. A desalination unit can reduce total dissolved solids, but regulators and customers care about more than salt. EPA’s 2020 oil-and-gas produced-water study recorded stakeholder concerns about chemistry data, treatment performance, analytical methods, toxicity, and impacts to downstream users. DOE’s 2024 fact sheet lists mineral salts, organics, heavy metals, and naturally occurring radioactive materials among the relevant constituents. Newer toxicology work from the New Mexico Produced Water Research Consortium is even more pointed: pilot-scale thermal distillation substantially reduced toxicity in tested samples, but post-treatment with activated carbon and zeolite was needed to mitigate residual xenobiotic organics and bioactivity signals. Source: https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2020-05/documents/oil-gas-final-report-2020.pdf, https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-06/Produced%20Water%20from%20Oil%20and%20Gas%20Development%20and%20Critical%20Minerals%20Fact%20Sheet_6.18.24.pdf, and https://nmpwrc.nmsu.edu/2025-Wijekoon-et-al---Comprehensive-cytotoxicity-assessment-of-treated-produced-water-from-thermal-distillation-using-human-cell-lines.pdf

The real unit is cost per qualified barrel

The first evidence point is Texas’s own produced-water roadmap. The 2022 Texas Produced Water Consortium report estimated that the Permian could have about 2 billion barrels per year of produced water available for reuse, with upside to 4 billion barrels per year depending on treatment and recovery assumptions. It also projected a 38-year average of 14 million barrels per day of produced water and 11 million barrels per day of excess water after oilfield reuse. Those figures make beneficial reuse look inevitable. The same report then identifies the gating problem: there was no Permian facility treating water quality beyond oil-and-gas operational needs, and high variability warranted pilot facilities to provide samples and characterization. Source: https://www.depts.ttu.edu/research/tx-water-consortium/downloads/22-TXPWC-Report-Texas-Legislature.pdf

The second evidence point is cost. The same Texas report put disposal through pipelines around $0.60 to $0.70 per barrel, named a target competitive treatment cost of roughly $1 per barrel of recovered treated water, and reported member-estimated treatment costs around $2.55 per barrel, with some treatment estimates as high as $10 per barrel. Those numbers exclude transportation, storage, polishing, and some pre/post-treatment burdens. A buyer of industrial water does not pay merely for purity. It pays for reliability, permitting comfort, uptime, residual-risk transfer, and a discount to competing supplies. If treatment can technically work but costs several times disposal or regional water-supply alternatives, the qualifying customer must either face scarcity, receive a discount, value disposal avoidance, or share regulatory and reputational benefits.

The plant is also a permit stack

The third evidence point is the permit architecture. The Railroad Commission of Texas pilot framework treats non-oilfield beneficial reuse as a limited authorization problem, not as an ordinary commodity sale. Pilot permits are limited, can include volume and reuse restrictions, and require quarterly and final reports. The framework asks for constituents of concern, reuse scenarios, treatment methods, effects on soil and crops where relevant, and constraints. New Chapter 4 rules effective July 1, 2025 require waste characterization and a waste profile record, and allow pilot programs when they prevent pollution and promote beneficial reuse. In other words, Texas is building a documentary pathway before it has a commodity market. Source: https://www.rrc.texas.gov/media/nznn2wsj/240108-produced-water-framework-final.pdf and https://www.rrc.texas.gov/media/hv2jy4ca/chapter4-all-effective-july1-2025.pdf

The fourth evidence point is New Mexico’s caution. NMED’s produced-water pilot page says pilot projects are meant to fill data gaps, and that no-discharge pilot projects are conducted in closed-loop systems. The Infinity Water Solutions Battle Axe Ranch pilot illustrates the boundary: roughly 100 barrels, or 4,200 gallons, of pre-treated and desalinated produced water were to be used on a non-consumptive hemp crop in above-ground closed-loop grow boxes, with the crop disposed of in a certified landfill. NMED found no discharge permit was required because treated produced water would not move directly or indirectly into groundwater or surface water. The design proves that even an agricultural pilot can be structured as containment, not release. Source: https://www.env.nm.gov/new-mexico-produced-water/pilot-projects/ and https://www.env.nm.gov/new-mexico-produced-water/wp-content/uploads/sites/30/2023/11/2023-11-17-Infinity-NoDPReq-Final-SN1.pdf

Who accepts the water sets the price

The fifth evidence point is customer qualification. Industrial cooling is often named as an attractive outlet because power plants, data centers, and manufacturing facilities can use large volumes of non-potable water. Texas’s 2022 report still warns that elevated salinity can reduce cooling performance, estimating about a 1.1% performance-efficiency decline per 10,000 mg/L TDS and about 5% at 50,000 mg/L TDS. It also flags calcium, sulfate, chloride, ammonium, pH, microbes, organics, and inhalation or indoor-air concerns. A cooling-tower owner may care less about whether the water looks clean than whether it voids equipment warranties, accelerates corrosion, increases blowdown, changes biocide needs, creates worker-exposure issues, or leaves the owner exposed to future permit conditions.

The sixth evidence point is that regulators are moving, but not in a finished line. EPA announced in March 2025 that it would revise oil-and-gas wastewater discharge regulations and consider uses such as AI/data-center cooling, rangeland, fire control, power generation, ecosystem support, and critical minerals. EPA’s Unified Agenda entry for RIN 2040-AG42 listed a proposed rule target of October 2025 and final action target of April 2026. Texas Senate Bill 1145 moved land-application permitting authority for treated produced water from the Railroad Commission to TCEQ and required TCEQ standards to prevent pollution of surface and subsurface water. The regulatory direction favors reuse, but the files that satisfy TCEQ, EPA, local water districts, and end users remain the work product. Source: https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-will-revise-wastewater-regulations-oil-and-gas-extraction-help-unleash-american, https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaViewRule?RIN=2040-AG42&amp;pubId=202504, and https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/pdf/SB01145F.pdf

California is the precedent; the residual is the upside

The seventh evidence point is California’s older example. Cawelo Water District has long blended treated oilfield produced water with other irrigation supplies, under Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board orders and monitoring. That history proves that oilfield-water reuse can survive in a real agricultural system. It does not prove that Permian produced water can be treated and sold into Texas industrial systems on ordinary commercial terms. California’s practice depends on a different basin, long-running local arrangements, blending, specific monitoring, and state-specific water-board oversight. A 2023 Pacific Institute / Water Research Foundation project concluded that more work was needed to protect public health and the environment and produced tools for Title 22-style analysis and geospatial screening. Source: https://www.cawelowd.org/recycled-produced-water/ and https://pacinst.org/publication/potential-of-oilfield-produced-water-for-irrigation-in-california/

The eighth evidence point is residual value. If clean water is only one product, the brine and residuals become either a cost sink or a value pool. Select Water Solutions and LibertyStream announced a 2026 agreement to build commercial lithium-carbonate production units at Select water-treatment and recycling sites, with the first 1,000-tonne-per-year facility targeted for commissioning by December 2026. Select and ISE Chemicals then announced a June 2026 agreement to develop commercial iodine extraction and refining facilities using produced water from Select’s infrastructure across Texas, New Mexico, and Oklahoma, with a first Permian facility targeted for 2027 and about 3,000 tonnes per year of iodine production targeted by the end of 2030. These are not proof that mineral recovery subsidizes all reuse. They show why the residual stream may become a separate control point. Source: https://investors.selectwater.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/139/select-water-solutions-and-libertystream-infrastructure-partners-announce-definitive-agreement-to-build-out-commercial-lithium-carbonate-production-units-in-texas-first-1000-tonne-facility-slated-for-commissioning-by-december-2026 and https://investors.selectwater.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/146/select-water-solutions-announces-definitive-agreement-with-ise-chemicals-corporation-providing-for-the-development-of-commercial-iodine-production-facilities-utilizing-produced-water-infrastructure-across-texas-new-mexico-and-oklahoma

Who controls the constraint

The control points therefore sit in a different place than a simple equipment map suggests. Treatment vendors control membranes, distillation systems, adsorbents, oxidation steps, brine concentration, and field-service reliability. Water midstream companies control aggregation, pipelines, storage, blending, disposal fallback, and customer contracting. Regulators control discharge, land application, pilot duration, monitoring, and liability boundaries. Research consortia and test labs control the evidence standards by which a water stream becomes financeable. Industrial customers control the last gate: acceptance into cooling, construction, power, manufacturing, or non-food agriculture under their operating specifications.

Capital markets can miss this because the easy numbers are barrel volumes and treatment recovery percentages. A system that recovers 92% desalinated water, passes Whole Effluent Toxicity testing, or produces 40–200 ppm TDS water may still need a project-specific permit, a named offtaker, residual disposal, continuous monitoring, and a liability structure. TETRA’s Oasis TDS pilot disclosure is useful precisely because it says the treated water met customer KPIs and passed third-party WET testing; the WET result is not decoration, it is part of the qualification path. Source: https://ir.onetetra.com/news/news-details/2024/TETRA-TECHNOLOGIES-INC--INTRODUCES-TETRA-OASIS-TOTAL-DESALINATION-SOLUTION-TETRA-OASIS-TDS-AND-RESULTS-FROM-PERMIAN-BASIN-BENEFICIAL-RE-USE-PILOT-12-16-2024/default.aspx

What would prove this wrong, and what to watch

Three possible paths follow. In the first, industrial cooling becomes the first scalable off-field market. That path would show up as TCEQ and EPA permits tied to named facilities, public monitoring plans, water-quality specifications, and commercial contracts larger than pilot scale. In the second, residual handling dominates. Desalinated water becomes a byproduct of brine concentration, and value accrues to infrastructure owners, mineral-recovery partners, disposal capacity, and chemistry providers that can make the residual stream cheaper or saleable. In the third, the qualification file never becomes standardized enough for broad markets. Pilots continue, oilfield recycling grows, and off-field reuse remains bespoke, discounted, and politically fragile.

The signs to monitor are concrete. Look for a commercial-scale Permian facility above tens of thousands of barrels per day that names the industrial customer, discloses permit conditions, publishes repeated third-party chemistry and toxicity results, and explains brine or spent-media disposal. Look for TCEQ and EPA rules that specify produced-water quality standards rather than only case-by-case reports. Look for insurance and contract language that transfers risk away from the water buyer. Look for data-center, power, cement, or manufacturing projects that accept produced-water-derived supply without describing it as experimental. Conversely, a major toxicity finding, unresolved residual-disposal problem, lawsuit, or customer refusal after treatment success would weaken the thesis.

The restrained conclusion is that beneficial reuse may become a real industrial water source, but not because desalination alone solves the problem. The scarce asset is the qualified, regulator-accepted, customer-accepted water stream. The companies and institutions that can generate, certify, transport, monitor, insure, and residual-manage that stream may control more of the value chain than the owners of any single treatment unit.
