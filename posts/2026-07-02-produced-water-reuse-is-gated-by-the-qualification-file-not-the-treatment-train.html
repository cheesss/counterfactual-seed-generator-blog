Produced-Water Reuse Is Gated By The Qualification File, Not The Treatment Train

The visible story in produced-water reuse is a desalination story. Dirty brine goes into a treatment train, cleaner water comes out, and a dry region gets a new industrial supply.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 11 min read

In brief

  • Western says JIP1 logged 50,000-plus data points over 24 months.
  • TXPWC's 2022 cost map put disposal at $0.60-$0.70/bbl and treatment near $2.55/bbl.
  • New Mexico allows only non-discharging pilots through December 31, 2030.
  • Value sits with whoever can certify, permit, insure, and place residuals.

The first tell is that this looks like a desalination story

That story is directionally right, but it skips the scarcer asset. A membrane, evaporator, or distillation unit can improve water quality. It does not by itself produce water that a regulator, insurer, landowner, and end user will accept every day.

The binding file is broader than a lab certificate. It has to show source characterization, stable treatment performance, toxicity behavior, residual handling, monitoring obligations, and who carries the liability if the water later causes trouble. Inside the oilfield, operators can often recycle water back into the well system. Outside the oilfield, the water crosses into someone else's cooling loop, crop box, construction site, or permit boundary.

The named cases look more like qualification campaigns than water-supply projects

Western Midstream's second pilot near Red Bluff Reservoir is a good example. The announced plant is designed for about 2,000 barrels per day of produced-water feed and about 1,000 barrels per day of reclaimed freshwater. That is tiny against the system Western says it already runs: about 3.0 million barrels per day handled, more than 3.8 million barrels per day of disposal capacity, up to 800,000 barrels per day on Pathfinder, and recycling capacity up to 600,000 barrels per day. (Western Midstream release) (Western water solutions)

So the strategic output is not the barrel count. Western says JIP1 produced more than 50,000 data points over 24 months to demonstrate consistent water quality for industrial cooling, irrigation, and surface discharge. That is the shape of an evidence factory, not a near-term regional water-supply solution. (Western Midstream release)

What ties these cases together is not the treatment hardware. It is the amount of documentation still missing before broad commercial acceptance.

The chemistry problem does not end at salt

Produced water is not one standard brine. It varies by basin, well, completion chemistry, field age, recycling history, and separation practice. So the acceptance problem cannot stop at total dissolved solids.

EPA's 2020 produced-water study recorded concerns about chemistry data, analytical methods, treatment performance, toxicity, and downstream impacts. DOE's 2024 fact sheet likewise lists salts, organics, heavy metals, and naturally occurring radioactive materials among the relevant constituents. (EPA 2020 produced-water study) (DOE 2024 fact sheet)

The NMPWRC Orla work is the sharper warning. In a 2025 paper, researchers described a 500 bpd low-temperature distillation unit handling feed with 100,000 to 170,000 mg/L TDS. Distillation materially reduced toxicity, but post-treatment with activated carbon and zeolite was still needed to mitigate residual xenobiotic organics and bioactivity in tested samples. That is why the bottleneck is qualification evidence, not just desalination yield. (NMPWRC toxicity study)

TETRA's pilot points the same way from a different angle. The company highlighted 92% recovery, 40-200 ppm TDS, customer KPIs, and third-party Whole Effluent Toxicity testing. Those disclosures matter because they imply the relevant test is not "can you make cleaner water?" but "can you produce a file that an end user and regulator will accept?" (TETRA Oasis release)

The cost gap is dated, but the direction still matters

Texas's 2022 Produced Water Consortium report framed the scale aggressively. It estimated roughly 2 billion barrels per year of produced water available for reuse in the Permian, with upside to 4 billion barrels depending on treatment and recovery assumptions. It also projected a 38-year average of 14 million barrels per day of produced water and 11 million barrels per day of excess water after oilfield reuse. (TXPWC 2022 report)

The same report also said the Permian had no facility treating to water quality beyond oil-and-gas operational needs. That is the supply-side clue that the basin lacked not just equipment, but a proof standard and a market path.

Its cost numbers need careful wording. TXPWC's 2022 public figures put pipeline disposal at about $0.60 to $0.70 per barrel, a target competitive treatment cost near $1 per barrel of recovered treated water, and member-estimated treatment cost around $2.55 per barrel, with some estimates up to $10 per barrel. Texas Living Waters / EDF's 2025 report cites a conflicting $2.25 per barrel average, so $2.55 should be read as a dated 2022 consortium estimate, not a live market price. (TXPWC 2022 report) (Texas Living Waters / EDF 2025)

That conflict changes the precision, not the mechanism. In the public figures cited here, treatment still costs more than simple disposal. A buyer therefore is not paying only for purity. It is paying for reliability, monitoring comfort, residual-risk transfer, and a discount to competing local supplies. Until that package clears, the qualified barrel remains expensive even if the treated barrel is technically available.

The plant is also a permit stack

Texas's rulemaking already reads like a document market. The Railroad Commission's pilot framework treats non-oilfield beneficial reuse as a limited authorization problem, with volume limits, reuse limits, quarterly reports, and final reports. New Chapter 4 rules effective July 1, 2025 require waste characterization and waste profile records. Texas Senate Bill 1145 then moved land-application permitting authority to TCEQ and required standards that prevent pollution of surface and subsurface water. (RRC pilot framework) (RRC Chapter 4 rules) (Texas SB 1145)

New Mexico is stricter and more explicit. NMED's pilot page says pilot projects exist to fill data gaps and that no-discharge pilots operate in closed-loop systems. The Infinity Water Solutions Battle Axe Ranch pilot stayed above ground, used about 100 barrels or 4,200 gallons of pre-treated and desalinated produced water on a non-consumptive hemp crop, and sent the crop to a certified landfill. NMED found that no discharge permit was required precisely because the treated water would not move directly or indirectly into groundwater or surface water. (NMED pilot projects) (NMED Infinity no-discharge letter)

New Mexico's 20.6.8 NMAC currently prohibits treated and untreated produced-water discharges to surface water and groundwater, allows only non-discharging pilot projects, and is scheduled to expire on December 31, 2030. That date is the current rule's expiration date, not an automatic post-2030 permit pathway. (New Mexico 20.6.8 NMAC)

This is why the constraint likely stays tight through the late 2020s. Until a named industrial offtake publishes multi-month operating data and regulators turn bespoke review into a repeatable standard, beneficial reuse still looks like a pilot-and-permit market rather than a scaled commodity water market.

The strongest counterargument is that treatment may stop being the bottleneck

The thesis can be wrong in an important way. TETRA and NMPWRC both show that vendors can make real progress on treatment performance. If several systems can repeatedly hit low TDS, pass WET or toxicity screens, and satisfy customer KPIs, then the value may migrate away from the treatment train.

In that scenario, the control point becomes the permit dossier, the residual outlet, or the commercial contract. The bottleneck would not disappear; it would move one layer deeper. Across the named cases, residual handling, customer offtake, and liability transfer remain the unresolved fields.

There is a second counterargument as well. Oilfield recycling may absorb the easier market. Texas Living Waters / EDF describes reuse inside the oilfield as the low-hanging fruit, with recycling rates around 50% and some estimates up to 60%. If that channel keeps expanding, off-field reuse may stay bespoke longer than bullish water-shortage narratives assume. (Texas Living Waters / EDF 2025)

California is a precedent, not a shortcut

The closest U.S. base rate is California, not a sudden Permian commodity market. Cawelo Water District has long blended treated oilfield produced water with other irrigation supplies under Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board orders and monitoring. That proves produced-water reuse can exist in a real agricultural system. (Cawelo recycled produced water) (California Water Boards)

It does not prove that Permian produced water can move into Texas industrial cooling or manufacturing on ordinary commercial terms. California's practice depends on a different basin, long-running local arrangements, blending, monitoring, and state-specific oversight. The 2023 Pacific Institute / Water Research Foundation work also concluded that more analysis is needed to protect public health and the environment. (Pacific Institute / WRF)

That is the closest historical base rate in the public record used here. Reuse can persist. It does not automatically scale into a broad, fast market.

Residual handling may capture more value than the clean-water line

One way this story changes is if the residual stream becomes the real economic center. Infinity's pilot ended with landfill disposal of the crop. The Orla work needed post-treatment media. Every treatment system still has to decide what happens to brine, spent media, salts, and other concentrated leftovers.

Select Water Solutions' 2026 announcements are relevant for that reason. One agreement targeted a first 1,000 tonne per year lithium carbonate facility by December 2026. A second targeted a first Permian iodine facility in 2027 and about 3,000 tonnes per year of iodine production by the end of 2030. Those announcements are forward-looking, not proof of a solved business model. But they show where a separate value pool may appear if residual management becomes the real rent center. (Select lithium release) (Select iodine release)

If that happens, beneficial reuse economics stop looking like a water-utility story. They start to look like a coupled system of treatment, disposal avoidance, mineral recovery, and liability management.

Who controls the constraint, and what to watch

The control points do not sit with one machine vendor. Treatment vendors control recovery, post-treatment, and field-service reliability. Water midstream operators control aggregation, pipelines, storage, blending, and disposal fallback. Regulators control pilot scope, discharge rules, land application, and monitoring. Labs and research consortia help define what evidence counts. Industrial customers control the last gate by deciding whether the water can enter a cooling loop, plant process, or agricultural system under their own specs.

The public-company exposure that most cleanly fits the evidence here runs through those control points: Western Midstream on the infrastructure and pilot side, TETRA on treatment plus qualification claims, and Select on residual-value pathways. None of those names is a recommendation. They are simply the actors with the clearest public exposure to the operating leverage described above. (Western Midstream release) (TETRA Oasis release) (Select lithium release) (Select iodine release)

What would weaken this thesis is straightforward: a public, independently monitored, multi-year commercial contract above pilot scale that names the end customer, discloses regulator-approved chemistry and toxicity monitoring, resolves brine and spent-media handling, and operates without experimental containment.

What to watch:

  • A Permian project above pilot scale that names the industrial customer and permit terms.
  • Repeated third-party chemistry and toxicity results tied to a real offtake, not a lab-only pilot.
  • TCEQ or EPA standards that turn bespoke produced-water review into a repeatable filing path.
  • Residual-handling economics, including landfill, brine, spent-media, or mineral-recovery milestones.
  • A lawsuit, toxicity finding, or customer refusal after treatment performance appears to succeed.

Beneficial reuse may become a real industrial water source. The evidence supports that possibility. But the scarce asset today is still the accepted file around the water, not just the cleaned water itself.
