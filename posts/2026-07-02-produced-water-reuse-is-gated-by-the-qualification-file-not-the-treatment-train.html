Produced-Water Reuse Has Cleared the Treatment Gate. The Commercial Gate Comes Next

Produced-water reuse is usually presented as a desalination problem. The Permian Basin generates enormous volumes of saline wastewater, western industrial users need new water supplies, and treatment vendors say they can connect the two.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 13 min read

That description is increasingly incomplete. Recent pilot results show that thermal, membrane, and hybrid treatment systems can remove most measured contaminants, while polishing can sharply improve toxicological performance. The harder question is what happens after the treatment train works.

A project must still turn a variable oilfield waste stream into a product that a regulator can permit, an industrial customer can place inside its equipment, an insurer can price, and a lender can finance. That requires a source envelope, analytical record, toxicity tests, residual-disposal plan, monitoring protocol, delivery system, backup outlet, and allocation of liability when the water falls outside specification.

The constraint is therefore sequential. Developers reach the qualification gate precisely because they have made progress on the visible treatment problem. Desalination opens the next gate; it does not complete commercialization.

This distinction matters because technical progress has been substantial. The Texas Produced Water Consortium’s 2026 assessment examined 60 samples from five Permian pilot systems and generated 17,172 rows of third-party laboratory data. The report found greater than 99% removal for major contaminant classes and compliance with 75 of 76 parameters that had comparable Texas Commission on Environmental Quality benchmarks.

The toxicology results also showed why desalination alone is insufficient. Unpolished desalinated samples produced no observed toxicity in only 3 of 16 Whole Effluent Toxicity endpoints. After polishing, 85 of 95 endpoints showed no observed toxicity. The evidence no longer supports a broad claim that produced-water treatment is technically unavailable. It supports a narrower claim: treatment must be paired with source-specific polishing, testing, monitoring, and operating controls before the output becomes commercially acceptable. (Texas Produced Water Consortium)

The named projects are evidence factories

Western Midstream’s second joint-industry pilot near Red Bluff Reservoir in Reeves County, Texas, illustrates the current state of the market. The facility began operating in June 2026 with approximately 2,000 barrels per day of produced-water feed and 1,000 barrels per day of reclaimed-water output.

Those volumes are negligible beside Western’s surrounding water network. The company reports handling roughly 3.0 million barrels per day, with more than 3.8 million barrels per day of disposal capacity, up to 800,000 barrels per day on the Pathfinder Pipeline, and recycling capacity of as much as 600,000 barrels per day.

The pilot’s valuable output is the qualification record. Western says the preceding JIP1 program ran for 24 months and generated more than 50,000 data points. JIP2 remains a demonstration whose results are intended to inform a later commercial-scale phase. The project is not yet proof of a regional water-supply business or a signed industrial offtake. (Western Midstream)

TETRA Technologies is further evidence that the treatment subsystem is advancing. In its 2024 Oasis disclosure, the company reported 92% water recovery, treated-water salinity of 40 to 200 parts per million, achievement of customer-defined performance indicators, and a third-party WET pass. In April 2026, TETRA reported 50 days of steady-state operation at 96% uptime and described multiple engineering efforts and commercial engagements.

Those are meaningful operating results. They remain company-reported, however, and the public record does not identify a final end-use permit, a named recurring customer, a complete residual plan, or an executed industrial-water contract. TETRA has demonstrated progress through qualification; it has not yet demonstrated repeatable procurement. (TETRA Technologies)

New Mexico’s Infinity Water Solutions project shows how different the end-use boundary can look. The 2023 Battle Axe Ranch pilot used about 100 barrels, or 4,200 gallons, of pretreated and desalinated produced water on a non-consumptive hemp crop. The water remained in above-ground, closed-loop grow boxes, and the crop was sent to a certified landfill.

The state concluded that no discharge permit was required because the water would not move directly or indirectly into groundwater or surface water. That is a valid research project, but its design demonstrates containment rather than open-loop agricultural commercialization.

The cases span treatment, permitting, agricultural containment, industrial targeting, and residual recovery. Together they show a sector moving from technical feasibility toward qualification, but not yet toward routine purchasing.

Permits are becoming a production input

The commercial plant is also a permit stack.

New Mexico’s current 20.6.8 NMAC requires source characterization, treatment and research plans, containment, transportation controls, residual disposition, monitoring, reporting, exposure controls, financial assurance, and closure provisions. It prohibits treated and untreated produced-water discharges that may directly or indirectly affect surface water or groundwater and permits only non-discharging pilot structures under the current framework.

The rule is scheduled to expire on December 31, 2030. That date does not create an automatic authorization after 2030; it is only the expiration date of the existing rule. New Mexico has an active amendment proceeding, so the operative framework may change before the scheduled expiration.

Texas is moving in the opposite direction institutionally, but it has not completed the transition. Senate Bill 1145 transferred produced-water land-application authority to TCEQ. The agency has proposed changes to Chapters 210 and 309, and several projects—including South Curtis Ranch, Hutt Ranch, TPOT, Thanos Beneficial Reuse, and TWS Reeves 1—have entered the administrative application pathway.

As of the July 23, 2026 base date, the implementing rule remained proposed, not adopted. South Curtis Ranch had achieved administrative completeness on May 18, 2026, but no technical-completeness or final-permit date had been posted. Texas has moved from policy discussion to actual permit processing, yet it has not established a repeatable approval template. (TCEQ proposed rules)

The federal timetable has also slipped. EPA’s Unified Agenda currently targets September 2026 for a proposed revision to the oil-and-gas extraction effluent guidelines and March 2027 for final action. The earlier October 2025 proposal and April 2026 final targets passed without completion. These dates are agenda targets rather than statutory deadlines. (Federal Unified Agenda)

This timing suggests that qualification remains a material constraint through roughly 2027–2030. It could ease sooner if Texas adopts its rules, one of the pending applications reaches a final permit, and a named customer accepts recurring supply under substantially standardized conditions.

Industrial customers buy reliability and risk allocation

Industrial cooling is the most frequently cited large-volume outlet. Power plants, data centers, and manufacturing sites can consume steady non-potable supply, often near Permian production and infrastructure corridors.

But a cooling-water buyer does not purchase low salinity in isolation. It purchases water that will not accelerate corrosion, increase scaling, disrupt biocide programs, raise blowdown volumes, create worker-exposure concerns, breach a discharge permit, or invalidate equipment warranties.

The 2022 Texas Produced Water Consortium report estimated a cooling-performance decline of roughly 1.1% for each 10,000 milligrams per litre increase in total dissolved solids and about 5% at 50,000 milligrams per litre. It also flagged calcium, sulfate, chloride, ammonium, pH, microbial activity, organics, and exposure pathways.

A bankable contract would therefore need to define the permitted source envelope, treatment configuration, volume, quality limits, uptime, sampling authority, data access, off-spec remedies, backup water, residual disposition, change-in-law procedures, equipment compatibility, environmental indemnities, and termination rights.

No public Permian agreement in the audited evidence contains that complete package. Western names industrial cooling as a target application. TETRA reports commercial engagements. Neither disclosure identifies an executed multi-year industrial contract with a final permit and recurring purchase obligations.

That missing document is more important than another recovery-rate announcement. It would reveal who pays, who bears off-spec risk, who owns the operating data, and whether the delivered barrel is priced as water, avoided disposal, infrastructure service, or some combination of the three.

Economics and residuals may still defeat the thesis

Qualification is a demonstrated gate, but it may not be the dominant gate in every project.

The strongest challenge is cost. The 2022 Texas consortium report estimated pipeline disposal at approximately $0.60 to $0.70 per barrel and produced-water treatment at about $2.55 per barrel, with some estimates reaching $10. The report’s competitive target was roughly $1 per barrel of recovered water. These are dated industry estimates, not current quotations, and they can exclude transport, storage, polishing, monitoring, and residual handling.

Even a fully qualified stream may have no customer if its delivered cost exceeds disposal plus conventional water. Water scarcity, seismic restrictions on disposal, customer resilience needs, regulation, or mineral recovery may close the gap in specific corridors. The public evidence does not yet show that they do so consistently.

Residuals create the second challenge. Treatment produces concentrated brine, spent media, salts, maintenance waste, and off-spec water. New Mexico’s Infinity pilot sent the crop to landfill. The NMPWRC Orla study required granular activated carbon and zeolite after distillation to reduce residual biological activity. A commercial plant must reserve disposal capacity or create another outlet for these materials.

Select Water Solutions is testing whether residual streams can become a value pool. Its agreement with LibertyStream targets a 1,000-tonne-per-year lithium carbonate facility by December 2026. Its agreement with ISE Chemicals targets an initial Permian iodine facility in 2027 and approximately 3,000 tonnes per year of iodine production by the end of 2030.

The contractual structure is notable: technology partners are expected to fund and operate extraction facilities, while Select contributes produced-water sourcing, transport, storage, pretreatment, recycling, and related infrastructure and receives royalty-type economics. The milestones remain forward-looking. No operating production, achieved recovery cost, or recurring royalty has yet demonstrated that mineral extraction can subsidize beneficial-reuse water broadly.

The thesis therefore survives only in narrower form. Qualification evidence currently delays off-field commercialization, but economics, residual disposition, customer demand, insurance, and liability can be co-binding—and may ultimately matter more.

The closest precedent took decades, not quarters

California’s Cawelo Water District is the clearest U.S. historical base rate.

Cawelo has used treated and blended oilfield produced water in agricultural irrigation for more than two decades under Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board orders, monitoring programs, and additional studies. The case proves that produced-water reuse can operate durably in a real end-use system.

It is not a shortcut for the Permian. Cawelo depends on basin-specific chemistry, blending, local treatment arrangements, crop patterns, a long operating history, and California’s regional regulatory regime. The Permian proposals involve different source waters, industrial customers, infrastructure corridors, residual streams, and state and federal rules.

The similarity is institutional: both require local characterization, monitoring, accepted operating practices, and a regulator willing to maintain a long-lived framework. The difference is that California built that framework incrementally. The current Permian thesis assumes a faster transition from pilot evidence to industrial procurement, but the closest precedent suggests that qualification systems mature through repeated operating records rather than one decisive technology demonstration. (Cawelo Water District)

Control belongs to the integrated operator

If off-field reuse enters early deployment, the commercially defensible product will probably be an assured water-and-risk service rather than a desalination unit.

Treatment vendors control process performance, energy use, recovery, polishing, uptime, consumables, and warranties. Certified laboratories control accepted analytical results and chain of custody. Regulators control use restrictions, monitoring schedules, residual conditions, and reporting. Industrial customers control the final release decision.

Water-midstream operators occupy the connective position. They can aggregate source water, equalize variable flows, locate treatment near pipelines and power, deliver reclaimed water, maintain recycling and disposal fallback, and preserve the operating history that regulators and customers use to judge future batches.

Western Midstream and Select Water Solutions have the clearest public exposure to this integrated model. TETRA has exposure through treatment and qualification performance. Select also has exposure to residual monetization through its lithium and iodine partnerships. These are operating exposures, not investment recommendations.

The principal risk is double counting. A reclaimed barrel may create water-sale revenue and avoided-disposal value, but those benefits do not automatically accrue to the same party. Mineral revenue cannot be added without subtracting extraction capital, energy, reagents, refining, partner economics, and remaining waste costs. Laboratory spending may be recurring but low margin. Disposal may remain essential for concentrate, maintenance outages, and rejected water even as gross reuse volumes grow.

The winner may therefore be less visible than the treatment technology. It may be the party willing to guarantee specification, own the customer interface, maintain fallback capacity, and absorb the contractual consequences when the water cannot be delivered.

What to watch

  • TCEQ adopts rule project 2026-006-309-OW by March 31, 2027.
  • One pending Texas application posts a technical package by June 30, 2027.
  • EPA publishes the Subpart E proposal by March 31, 2027.
  • A final Permian permit authorizes at least 10,000 bpd by December 31, 2030.
  • A named industrial customer signs a two-year, 10,000-bpd contract by December 31, 2030.
  • Three permits reuse materially similar qualification conditions within 24 months by December 31, 2033.

Produced-water treatment is no longer merely a laboratory proposition. The sector now has credible removal results, better polishing data, operating pilots, active applications, and infrastructure owners preparing for scale.

What it does not yet have is repeatable procurement. The market will change when a final permit, named customer, residual outlet, monitoring schedule, reliability guarantee, and liability structure appear in the same project—and then appear again without being rebuilt from the beginning. Until that happens, the scarce asset is not simply clean water. It is an accepted operating package around the water.
