Sterilization capacity only counts when a device can actually use it

Ethylene oxide is often discussed as an emissions problem. It is also a capacity problem, but not in the usual industrial sense. A medical-device maker cannot simply buy an empty chamber somewhere else when a sterilizer is constrained. The replacement site, cycle, package, materials, residual testing and regulatory evidence all have to line up. That makes the scarce asset a bundle: permitted chamber time plus a device-specific record that allows the chamber to be used.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-18 · 7 min read

In brief

The distinction matters because FDA has already treated interruptions at ethylene-oxide facilities as a medical-device supply issue. Its response to the 2019 closures was not merely to find more industrial capacity. It helped manufacturers move particular products through qualified alternative sites and created later pathways intended to reduce duplicated review when an acceptable change is possible. (FDA sterilization overview)

The bottleneck is not a chamber count

In a conventional industrial shortage, spare machinery can be reallocated. Sterilization is different because the process is embedded in the product. The sterilization cycle has to deliver the intended sterility assurance level without degrading the device, its package or its components. For EtO, the manufacturer must also manage residual limits. The relevant evidence can include process validation under ISO 11135, EtO-residual work under ISO 10993-7 and sterile-barrier packaging validation under ISO 11607.

That is why nominal spare volume can be a misleading measure. A chamber with an opening is useful only if it can accept the particular validated load, execute a compatible cycle, meet the applicable residual profile and fit within the manufacturer's regulatory change path. The physical asset and the evidence package are complements, not substitutes. (FDA 510(k) sterility guidance)

EtO holds an unusually difficult position in that system. FDA says it is used where material compatibility, penetration of complex configurations, packaged loads and throughput matter. The agency has also said that alternatives do not yet replace EtO for many devices. Gamma radiation handles a large share of the remaining market, while the other modalities collectively start from a much smaller base. That is the reason a simple instruction to "use another sterilant" does not solve a sudden loss of qualified EtO capacity. (FDA alternative-sterilization work)

The historical stress test was 2019

The 2019 closure episode is the clearest demonstration of the mechanism. Following the Illinois seal order at Sterigenics' Willowbrook facility, FDA tracked multiple temporary or permanent contract-sterilizer closures. It later reported elevated shortage risk across more than 100 types of sterile medical devices and at least one actual shortage involving Bivona tracheostomy tubes.

The revealing detail is the remedy. Bivona supply did not recover because the industry found generic unused machinery. FDA described work to move the product to another Sterigenics facility and accelerate the relevant validation and release steps. That is what validated capacity looks like in practice: an alternate site that is operationally available and can be made acceptable for a particular product. (FDA EtO facility updates)

The same episode also demonstrates why capacity risk can become nonlinear. FDA said commercial sterilization facilities had historically operated at or near full capacity. When several sites are unavailable, the system does not simply lose a few percentage points of national output. It loses the ability to absorb device-specific rerouting work at the same time that manufacturers seek it.

Regulation is one constraint, not the whole story

EPA's 2024 commercial-sterilizer rule made the industrial layer visible. The agency's regulatory impact analysis estimated approximately $313 million of capital expenditure and $74 million of annualized cost for the selected option, and considered the risk that upgrades could temporarily constrain capacity. Those figures show why emissions control is more than a compliance memo: it can involve enclosures, monitoring, abatement equipment and outage planning. The RIA's hypothetical 10% and 20% capacity-loss figures are sensitivity cases, not EPA forecasts.

But a public article should not turn that 2024 rule into a deterministic shortage forecast. The policy path changed. The federal government subsequently granted certain relief, and EPA later proposed reconsidering significant elements of the rule, explicitly citing medical-device supply concerns. That weakens a narrow story in which federal retrofit deadlines alone create a nationwide cliff. (EPA EtO rule history)

The more durable point survives the policy reversal. State and local requirements, community acceptance, legal exposure, equipment upgrades, product validation and alternate-modality limitations sit on different clocks. A federal rule can be delayed or softened without turning a closed, litigated or unqualified facility into immediately usable capacity.

Who controls the constraint

The control point is distributed across several participants. Contract sterilizers control chambers, trained operations and multi-site networks. Device manufacturers control the product and packaging knowledge needed to validate a transfer. Test labs control cycle, residual and packaging evidence. Regulators decide when a change needs a supplement, a new submission or can use an established Master File route. Environmental and local institutions determine whether a facility can keep operating or expand.

FDA's Master File pilots are therefore more consequential than they may appear. They are attempts to make some evidence reusable: a sterilization provider can supply a standardized package of process information that qualifying manufacturers reference rather than reproduce independently. The approach does not make every switch automatic, and it does not erase material or packaging incompatibilities. It can, however, reduce the time between identifying an alternate capacity source and using it. (FDA radiation Master File pilot)

This also changes the industrial map. A multi-site provider with established customer validations may be more useful in a disruption than a single-site operator with comparable hardware. Likewise, emissions-control vendors, residual-testing laboratories, packaging-validation services and regulatory teams participate in the productive capacity of the network even though none of them is a sterilization chamber.

Alternatives are a release valve, not a reset button

The counterargument is important. Alternatives are progressing. FDA placed vaporized hydrogen peroxide in an Established Category A for suitable 510(k) devices, and its programs include radiation, nitrogen-dioxide, supercritical-CO2 and other approaches. That work can reduce dependence on EtO for compatible device families. (FDA VHP recognition)

Yet physical availability and qualification still move at different speeds. An X-ray, e-beam or VHP facility may add capacity, but the economic result depends on the set of products whose materials, geometry, packaging, residual requirements and regulatory files can migrate. The relevant question is not whether an alternative exists in a laboratory or even commercially. It is whether it can accept an existing, high-volume product portfolio without creating a new validation queue.

The proposition is therefore not that EtO is permanently irreplaceable. It is that the transition away from it is likely to be uneven. Some products can move early. Others may need years of evidence, changed packaging, new process development or a different supply-chain design. During that transition, capacity belongs to the facilities and evidence stacks that have already been accepted for the product at hand.

The real test is observability

The idea can be tested rather than treated as a narrative. Facility utilization, outage days, lead times, state-permit status, alternative-modality openings, Master File participation, site-transfer notices and FDA shortage communications are all observable. The mechanism strengthens if outages and validation transfers repeatedly determine which devices can be supplied even while headline chamber capacity looks adequate.

It weakens if alternate sites are routinely available, device makers move portfolios across modalities quickly, utilization falls materially below capacity and facility changes no longer demand consequential incremental evidence. That would mean the system has become genuinely fungible.

For now, the useful framing is narrower. Medical-device sterilization has revealed an industrial scarcity that is easy to miss in aggregate-capacity data: capacity matters only when it is simultaneously operational, permitted, compatible, validated and regulatorily usable. The limiting asset is not always the right machine. Sometimes it is the right machine with the right proof attached.
