Stable isotopes are not one bottleneck—and that is the point

Fusion developers, quantum-computing companies and radiopharmaceutical manufacturers appear to need the same thing: a rare stable isotope. That framing is directionally useful and operationally incomplete.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-03 · 14 min read

Lithium-6, silicon-28 and ytterbium-176 do not share one separator, one market or one current shortage. They share a sequence:

isotope-specific separation → controlled chemical form → assay and documentation → customer-process qualification → repeat procurement

The commercial gate sits wherever that sequence stops.

For lithium-6, the unresolved question is whether a modern, mercury-free process can reach application-relevant scale before fusion developers freeze their blanket designs. For silicon-28, the upstream physics has advanced far enough that wafers, yield and device performance now matter more than the ability to enrich silicon. For ytterbium-176, precursor capacity is expanding, and the constraint is already moving into reactor schedules, radiochemistry, batch release and patient delivery.

That distinction matters because an indispensable material does not automatically become a high-margin business. The supplier that enriches the isotope may not control the accepted chemical form, manufacturing workflow, qualification data or customer relationship.

Oak Ridge has already changed the silicon story

The United States did lose broad stable-isotope enrichment capability after Oak Ridge’s calutron era ended in 1998. But it is now inaccurate to describe the country as waiting passively for one future facility to restore every capability.

In July 2026, the Department of Energy said domestic electromagnetic and gas-centrifuge enrichment capabilities had returned. DOE described ytterbium-176 as being enriched routinely and said silicon-28 enrichment had been demonstrated. Its Stable Isotope Production and Research Center, or SIPRC, remains important, but primarily as an expansion of scale, resilience and product breadth.

The roughly 64,000-square-foot Oak Ridge facility carries a current project-cost point estimate of $325 million. DOE’s formal schedule places the electromagnetic-separation subproject’s completion in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2030 and full project completion in the third quarter of fiscal 2033. DOE has also described phased operations beginning in 2028. Those dates should be read as a commissioning sequence, not as the first moment any domestic enrichment becomes useful. (DOE SIPRC budget)

The sharper change came on July 16, 2026. DOE reported production of silane enriched to 99.9999% silicon-28, with silicon-29 below one part per million, and said the material would be made available through the National Isotope Development Center.

That result moves the commercial question downstream. Silicon-28 is no longer primarily a forecast about whether enrichment can be done. It is a qualification problem: can the gas be converted, transported and introduced into a semiconductor process without contamination or isotopic dilution, and can the resulting devices show repeatable performance?

DOE’s route also shows why the value chain extends beyond the separator. Oak Ridge performed the enrichment; Pacific Northwest National Laboratory handled conversion and purification. PNNL used automated controls monitoring hundreds of process variables. The saleable output is therefore not a percentage printed on an assay. It is a traceable production lot with chemical-purity evidence and process history. (DOE silicon-28 result)

Silicon-28 value is migrating into the fab

ASP Isotopes offers a useful private-sector test of the same mechanism. The company completed its Pretoria silicon-28 facility in 2024 and has described nameplate output above 50 kilograms per year at 99.995% enrichment. By May 2026, it reported that 18 stages had restarted and were operating at target enrichment levels, with three commercial contracts in place.

The commercial evidence remains incomplete.

ASP disclosed that valves, compressors, piping and other non-core components required modification after customer visits. Initial commercial shipments moved to the third quarter of 2026. Its quarterly filing showed no enriched-isotope revenue through March 31, 2026.

Those facts do not invalidate the facility. They identify the actual gate: accepted production lots and recurring orders. Nameplate capacity is not realized output, and a signed agreement is not necessarily recognized revenue. ASP’s next meaningful milestone is not another enrichment percentage. It is a named or verifiable customer accepting material and returning for another lot. (ASP SEC-filed update)

The downstream European chain is further along physically but still unresolved commercially. In December 2025, Soitec’s custom silicon-28 FD-SOI wafers entered STMicroelectronics’ 300 millimeter Crolles fab for Quobly’s process development. The participants expected prototype metrics in the first quarter of 2026, but no public yield or device-performance result had appeared by July.

That missing evidence determines who controls the economics. If isotope-enriched wafers produce materially better fidelity, coherence or yield, silicon-28 can support repeat procurement. If the decisive problems remain fabrication variability, control electronics, cryogenics, packaging or error correction, upstream material becomes an enabling input rather than the central source of value.

Soitec controls substrate engineering. STMicroelectronics controls the 300 millimeter manufacturing process. Quobly controls the quantum-device programme and the interpretation of device results. Even abundant silicon-28 would not automatically transfer workflow control or margin to the enrichment supplier.

Lithium-6 is the strongest unresolved upstream case

Lithium-6 presents a different industrial problem. It is not a kilogram-scale specialty-gas market. Some fusion blanket studies use tens to more than 100 tonnes of enriched lithium.

A 2024 academic comparison reported enriched-lithium inventories of 46.4 tonnes for an HCPB concept, 152.8 tonnes for WCLL, 118.6 tonnes for ARC and 155.8 tonnes for HELIAS. A separate 2026 analysis used a modeled stored-inventory range of 50 to 100 tonnes. These figures are design studies, not procurement commitments, but they establish the possible scale of the capital decision.

The historical process, COLEX, separated lithium isotopes through a lithium-mercury amalgam exchange route. Official records confirm industrial operation and major mercury liabilities. The exact production quantity often cited in secondary literature lacks primary verification and should not be treated as settled.

Modern Western developers are therefore trying to prove a different process and business model.

Molten Salt Solutions announced strategic agreements with Type One Energy and Gauss Fusion in March 2026. The company described possible kilogram-scale test material from 2027 and later expansion if customer testing and reactor deployment justify it. Those agreements identify potential customers, but they do not disclose binding tonnage, enrichment specifications, price or take-or-pay obligations.

Hexium is pursuing atomic-vapor laser isotope separation with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. LLNL is developing high-power laser systems while Hexium builds the separation system. The programme targets pilot demonstration and eventual commercial production, but it has not disclosed qualified throughput, plant cost or accepted customer material. (LLNL-Hexium programme)

This is the report’s strongest present-tense scarcity case because no operating, qualified Western mercury-free route at tonne scale has been verified. Yet even here the bottleneck could be misidentified.

Fusion developers have not universally selected a breeder material or enrichment level. Blanket corrosion, tritium extraction, materials damage, startup-tritium access, test infrastructure and financing may bind first. Natural-lithium or lower-enrichment designs could reduce demand before a dedicated plant is financed.

The lack of supply may therefore reflect uncertain demand as much as technical scarcity. A real commercial transition requires a fusion buyer to specify chemical form, enrichment, quantity and delivery timing—and then support the plant with binding procurement.

Radiopharmaceuticals show how the bottleneck migrates

Ytterbium-176 and lutetium-177 provide the clearest operating example of bottleneck migration.

No-carrier-added lutetium-177 can be produced by irradiating highly enriched ytterbium-176, allowing ytterbium-177 to decay and then separating lutetium chemically. European product documentation specifies greater-than-99% ytterbium-176, high neutron flux, chromatographic separation and a lutetium-177 half-life of 6.647 days.

The stable precursor can be stored. The radioactive product cannot be buffered indefinitely. Once the precursor is available, the system becomes a scheduling and manufacturing network.

Oak Ridge produced a nearly 30 gram batch of 99.7% ytterbium-176 in November 2023. In Toronto, Kinectrics announced four second-generation electromagnetic isotope separators and capacity above 500 grams per year by the end of 2025. The company said that capacity could support more than 150,000 treatments annually, although that treatment-equivalent figure is not interchangeable with grams shipped or doses delivered.

Kinectrics’ announced capacity is meaningful but not yet transparent commercial utilization. Public records do not identify its realized 2026 output, customers, price per gram or contract terms.

Downstream, the evidence is stronger. SHINE’s Cassiopeia facility in Janesville reported capacity for 100,000 lutetium-177 doses per year, with potential expansion to 200,000, distribution to 19 countries and greater-than-95% on-time, in-full performance. The European Medicines Agency authorized SHINE’s Ilumira product in March 2026.

Novartis provides the historical contra-case. Pluvicto faced a supply shortage in 2023. The company expanded Millburn, advanced Indianapolis and operated additional sites in Ivrea and Zaragoza. It subsequently reported unconstrained supply and network capacity of approximately 250,000 radioligand-therapy doses per year.

The feared shortage did materialize, but precursor enrichment was not the final answer. Approved manufacturing, batch release, logistics and certified treatment sites resolved the practical constraint.

That is the closest useful base rate for this broader thesis: an isotope-linked shortage emerged, capital entered multiple downstream nodes, and the bottleneck moved rather than disappearing. The difference from lithium-6 is that lutetium-177 decays quickly, while stable lithium and silicon can be accumulated once qualified supply exists.

The value does not necessarily stay upstream

The investment and policy significance of stable isotopes lies less in total tonnage than in control over an accepted workflow.

For silicon-28, value can move from the separator into high-purity gas conversion, cylinders, metrology, engineered substrates, fab-development lots and cryogenic testing. ASP Isotopes has direct enrichment exposure. Soitec and STMicroelectronics have downstream process exposure. Air Liquide has specialty-gas adjacency through the broader Quobly ecosystem, although its exact silicon-28 role has not been disclosed.

For ytterbium-176 and lutetium-177, Kinectrics’ position inside BWX Technologies combines isotope capacity with a broader nuclear-services platform. Reactor operators can capture value through irradiation access. ITM has priority access to part of the available capacity at the Institut Laue-Langevin High-Flux Reactor. SHINE and Novartis control regulated manufacturing and distribution closer to the end customer.

Public supply complicates the economics. DOE and NIDC can support products too small or uncertain for dedicated private plants, absorb market-formation risk and establish reference specifications. That can accelerate adoption while limiting private scarcity rents.

There is still no public evidence that isotope suppliers hold durable pricing power. Prices, gross margins, rejected-lot provisions, performance warranties, switching rights and service contracts remain largely undisclosed. A necessary input can support a valuable downstream market without becoming its dominant profit pool.

The analogy is narrower than it first appears

The strongest objection is straightforward: these may be three separate markets joined by a generic word—qualification.

Lithium-6 involves bulk materials, nuclear controls and environmental permitting. Silicon-28 involves electronic gases, wafers and semiconductor yield. Ytterbium-176 involves rare-earth enrichment, reactors and pharmaceutical quality systems.

No common machine serves all three. No common qualification standard has emerged. No evidence shows that customer acceptance in one isotope chain transfers into another.

That objection narrows the thesis without eliminating it. The transferable capability is institutional rather than mechanical: designing an isotope-specific process, operating difficult equipment, preserving purity, documenting the lot and proving it in the customer’s exact workflow.

The claim should stop there. It should not imply one cross-sector shortage, one unified market or automatic upstream value capture.

What to watch

  • 2027-03-31: ASP or a named customer reports an accepted commercial silicon-28 shipment.
  • 2027-06-30: Quobly, Soitec or ST publishes a quantitative silicon-28 device or yield result.
  • 2027-12-31: MSS, Hexium or LLNL reports an assayed kilogram-scale enriched-lithium batch.
  • 2028-12-31: A Western lithium-6 developer identifies a site and submits a substantive permit filing.
  • 2028-12-31: DOE or ORNL reports the start of phased SIPRC operations.
  • 2031-12-31: A fusion buyer discloses binding lithium-6 quantity, enrichment and delivery terms.

The conclusion is a sequence, not a shortage call

Stable-isotope enrichment remains strategically important, but the industrial picture has split.

Silicon-28 has moved from upstream availability into customer and device qualification. Ytterbium-176 is moving from precursor scarcity into irradiation, GMP manufacturing and distribution. Lithium-6 remains the strongest unresolved upstream case, but only for fusion designs that ultimately commit to enriched-lithium blankets at substantial scale.

The common lesson is not that every isotope will be scarce. It is that enrichment capacity becomes economically relevant only after someone converts it into an accepted material and connects it to a repeatable operating chain.

That is where the next evidence should appear—and where control is most likely to migrate.
