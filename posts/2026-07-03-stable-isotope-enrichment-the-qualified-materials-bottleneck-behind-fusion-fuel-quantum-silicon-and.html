Stable-Isotope Enrichment: The Qualified-Materials Bottleneck Behind Fusion Fuel, Quantum Silicon, and Radioligands

The obvious story in fusion, quantum computing, and radiopharmaceuticals is usually a demand story. Fusion needs magnets, power electronics, blankets, and tritium. Quantum processors need better qubits, lower error rates, cryogenic control, and usable manufacturing yield.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-03 · 13 min read

The obvious story in fusion, quantum computing, and radiopharmaceuticals is usually a demand story

The obvious story in fusion, quantum computing, and radiopharmaceuticals is usually a demand story. Fusion needs magnets, power electronics, blankets, and tritium. Quantum processors need better qubits, lower error rates, cryogenic control, and usable manufacturing yield. Radioligand therapy needs more approved drugs, more treatment centers, more nuclear pharmacists, and more isotope logistics. Those explanations are real, but they can miss a smaller upstream constraint: the qualified stable isotope.

The recurring bottleneck has the shape of qualified-materials. A usable isotope is not merely an element with a favorable atomic mass. It is a material in a specified isotopic ratio, in a specified chemical form, with verified purity, documentation, export status, safety handling, customer acceptance, and a downstream process that has been tuned around it. That is why lithium-6 for tritium breeding, silicon-28 for spin qubits, and ytterbium-176 for lutetium-177 radiopharmaceuticals belong in the same industrial conversation even though they live in different end markets.

The hidden mechanism is separation plus qualification

The hidden mechanism is separation plus qualification. Stable isotopes do not decay, so scarcity is not caused by half-life. Scarcity comes from the fact that nature usually gives the wrong isotopic mixture. Natural lithium contains only a minority lithium-6 fraction; natural silicon includes silicon-29, whose nuclear spin is a decoherence source for silicon spin qubits; no-carrier-added lutetium-177 uses highly enriched ytterbium-176 as a precursor in an irradiation and chemical-separation route. Separating isotopes is hard because the atoms are chemically similar. The practical difference is mass, nuclear spin, vapor pressure, spectral line, or a process-specific molecular behavior. The resulting industry looks less like ordinary mining and more like an overlap of semiconductor gases, nuclear safeguards, vacuum equipment, lasers, high-voltage separators, radiochemistry, and regulated logistics.

The United States had a broad stable-isotope enrichment base once. Oak Ridge’s calutrons at Y-12 produced more than 230 stable isotopes from 1945 to 1998, after which the country lost broad-scope domestic enrichment capability and began drawing down stockpiles. DOE is now rebuilding part of that industrial base at Oak Ridge through the Stable Isotope Production and Research Center, a roughly 64,000-square-foot facility with electromagnetic isotope separator systems, gas centrifuge isotope separation, test cascades, and future expansion space. DOE budget documents put the project’s current total project cost point estimate at $325 million and show staged completion: the EMIS subproject reaches CD-4 in 4Q FY2030, a test cascade in 4Q FY2032, and full project CD-4 in 3Q FY2033. That schedule matters more than the headline that “isotopes are available,” because qualified capacity is rebuilt on construction, validation, staff, feedstock chemistry, and operating evidence, not on demand forecasts alone. See DOE/NIDC and DOE budget records: https://www.isotopes.gov/department-energy-awards-88m-contract-build-stable-isotope-production-and-research-facility and https://www.energy.gov/media/346289.

Lithium-6 shows the most extreme scale mismatch. Fusion narratives often treat lithium as abundant because total lithium resources are large. A D-T fusion blanket does not buy generic lithium; it may need tens of tonnes of lithium enriched to high lithium-6 fractions. Kyoto Fusioneering’s IAEA presentation put typical requirements at 30–60% lithium-6 for beryllium-based blanket concepts, roughly 1–10 tonnes per reactor, and 40–90% for lead-based blanket concepts, roughly 10–100 tonnes per blanket. A later academic analysis put representative enriched lithium inventories at 46.4 tonnes for HCPB, 152.8 tonnes for WCLL, 118.6 tonnes for ARC, and 155.8 tonnes for HELIAS. A 2026 paper by Ward, Pearson, Scott, and Lopes Cardozo frames the stored lithium inventory at 50–100 tonnes and argues that enrichment, not variable lithium consumption, can behave like upfront capital expenditure. Source trail: https://conferences.iaea.org/event/285/contributions/22687/attachments/11981/19520/IAEA%20Resource%20availability%20%26%20supply%20RJPearson_8June2022.pdf, https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/a-theoretical-study-on-the-suitable-enrichment-methods-for-lithiu/, and https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04707.

The old industrial answer was COLEX, a lithium-mercury amalgam exchange process

The old industrial answer was COLEX, a lithium-mercury amalgam exchange process. It worked at scale: the Y-12 plant produced roughly 442 tons of lithium-6 from 1954 to 1963. It also used mercury, created environmental liabilities, and is now politically and practically unattractive under modern mercury controls. U.S. export controls still treat lithium isotope separation facilities and equipment as nuclear-sensitive: 10 CFR Part 110 Appendix N lists facilities or plants for lithium isotope separation, packed liquid-liquid exchange columns for lithium amalgams, mercury or lithium amalgam pumps, lithium amalgam electrolysis cells, and evaporators for concentrated lithium hydroxide solution under NRC export licensing authority. The important point is not only that the material is scarce; the machinery and know-how live in a controlled category. Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-10/chapter-I/part-110.

Two private attempts now mark the early relief path. Molten Salt Solutions announced strategic agreements in March 2026 with Type One Energy and Gauss Fusion, describing kilogram-scale enriched lithium materials for testing as early as 2027 and possible expansion to several hundred tons as customers perform larger-scale tests and deploy first power plants. The company says its solvent-exchange approach compresses many separation steps into an integrated system rather than thousands of discrete stages. Hexium is pursuing a laser route. Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory says it is partnering with Hexium under a DOE Technology Commercialization Fund award and CRADA to adapt atomic vapor laser isotope separation for lithium, with LLNL prototyping high-power lasers and Hexium building the separation system; LLNL states the team is aiming for full commercial production in the next three years. Those are credible search leads, not proof of solved tonnage supply. Both still need demonstrated throughput, cost, licensing, customer qualification, and an answer to the question of whether fusion blanket designs stabilize before the supply plant is financed. Sources: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/molten-salt-solutions-signs-strategic-agreements-to-supply-enriched-lithium-for-fusion-energy-302723666.html and https://www.llnl.gov/article/54321/revitalized-laser-technology-captures-commercialization-grant.

Silicon-28 is a smaller tonnage problem but a harsher qualification problem. The material must enter a semiconductor chain without compromising chemical purity. ASP Isotopes completed construction of a silicon-28 facility in Pretoria in November 2024 and said it expected more than 50 kg per year of silicon-28 enriched to 99.995%, up from earlier guidance above 10 kg per year. The company had two U.S.-based customer agreements at that point and said the facility sat under a Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement with IAEA inspections. That sounded like a supply answer. The later SEC-filed update shows why the bottleneck is not just the separator. In May 2026, ASP said the first 18 stages had restarted and operated for more than three weeks at target enrichment levels, but non-core components such as valves, compressors, and piping had needed modifications after customer visits, and commercial shipments were pushed to Q3 2026. The controlling nodes were not only mass separation physics; they included helium-tight compressors, pressure-actuated relief valves, molar mass meters, laminar flow meters, and customer acceptance. Sources: https://ir.aspisotopes.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/48/asp-isotopes-inc-completes-construction-and-starts-the and https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1921865/000119312526237916/aspi-ex99_1.htm.

The downstream silicon chain is starting to reveal the same qualified-materials logic

The downstream silicon chain is starting to reveal the same qualified-materials logic. Quobly announced in December 2025 that Soitec’s custom silicon-28 FD-SOI substrates were cycling through STMicroelectronics’ 300 mm Crolles fab for process development and validation. Quobly framed the path as “from purified gas to quantum chip delivery” and said the first prototype-device performance metrics were expected in the first quarter of 2026. This is the semiconductor version of an isotope bottleneck: isotope ratio is necessary but not sufficient. The chain needs a gas supplier, substrate engineer, 300 mm fab, device designer, and test evidence. A kilogram of enriched silane has no value unless it can be translated into wafers and qubit performance without ruining the rest of the process. Source: https://www.quobly.io/press-releases/quobly-marks-a-new-milestone-towards-industrialization-with-soitecs-28si-fd-soi-substrates-now-cycling-in-sts-300mm-fab.

Ytterbium-176 and lutetium-177 sit between the fusion and quantum cases. The tonnage is tiny relative to lithium, but the document trail and time decay are unforgiving. No-carrier-added lutetium-177 can be produced by irradiating highly enriched ytterbium-176, generating ytterbium-177, which decays to lutetium-177, then separating lutetium chemically from the ytterbium target. The European EndolucinBeta product information specifies highly enriched greater-than-99% ytterbium-176, neutron flux between 10^13 and 10^16 cm^-2 s^-1, chromatographic separation, and a lutetium-177 half-life of 6.647 days. That half-life migrates the bottleneck downstream: once the isotope is made, scheduling, labeling, release testing, treatment-center access, and logistics matter. Source: https://ec.europa.eu/health/documents/community-register/2017/20170921138647/anx_138647_en.pdf.

There is evidence of real relief in Yb-176. DOE reported a nearly 30 gram batch of 99.7% Yb-176 from its modernized EMIS platform in November 2023, the largest U.S.-produced batch since calutron operations halted in 1998. Kinectrics announced in September 2025 that it had commissioned four second-generation EMIS units in Toronto, lifting annual Yb-176 production capacity above 500 grams by year-end and supporting more than 150,000 patient treatments annually. Kinectrics also disclosed a 26,000-square-foot expansion under construction at its Toronto headquarters. SHINE’s Lu-177 case offers a downstream complement: its Cassiopeia facility in Janesville reports 100,000 Lu-177 doses per year with potential expansion to 200,000, customers in 19 countries, and more than 95% on-time, in-full delivery for Ilumira’s first year. These records do not prove that Yb-176 will remain binding. They show capacity is arriving, and that the bottleneck may migrate from enriched precursor to reactor irradiation slots, GMP separation, labeled-dose manufacturing, or treatment-center throughput. Sources: https://www.kinectrics.com/media-center/news-releases/2025-kinectrics-significantly-increases-isotope-production and https://www.shinefusion.com/blog/shine-1year-milestone-ilumira-lutetium177.

Novartis demonstrates that migration

Novartis demonstrates that migration. In 2023, Pluvicto supply was constrained; CIDRAP reported that the drug could not be stockpiled, had been listed on FDA’s shortage list, and had to be used within five days. By October 2023, Novartis said Pluvicto supply was unconstrained after doubling weekly production, expanding Millburn, advancing Indianapolis, and operating sites in Ivrea and Zaragoza. By January 2024, the Indianapolis facility increased radioligand therapy capacity to 250,000 doses in 2024 and beyond across four active manufacturing facilities. The supply story moved from isotope availability to batch production, facility approval, dose timing, and certified treatment sites. Sources: https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/resilient-drug-supply/prostate-cancer-drug-shortage-should-ease-soon-novartis-says, https://www.novartis.com/news/media-releases/novartis-confirms-unconstrained-supply-pluvicto-and-continues-significantly-expand-number-treatment-centers, and https://www.biospace.com/novartis-expands-production-of-pluvicto-with-addition-of-its-largest-and-most-advanced-radioligand-therapy-manufacturing-facility-in-indianapolis.

The strongest objection is that the three isotope markets may be too siloed to share a bottleneck

The strongest objection is that the three isotope markets may be too siloed to share a bottleneck. Lithium-6 enrichment is a bulk, nuclear-sensitive, environmentally constrained industrial process. Silicon-28 is a semiconductor gas and wafer-qualification process. Yb-176 is a gram-scale EMIS or laser/rare-earth process tied to reactors, radiochemistry, and GMP controls. The same building does not automatically serve all three. Even within DOE’s portfolio, gas centrifuge, thermal diffusion, and electromagnetic separation cover different isotope ranges and products. ASP’s silicon facility stumbled on valves and compressors; a Yb-176 line can stumble on lasers or power quality; lithium may stumble on mercury replacement, licensing, and blanket-design uncertainty. The transferable constraint is not a universal machine. It is a scarce institutional capability: build and operate isotope-specific separation systems, certify material, keep a trained staff, satisfy regulators, and prove to downstream customers that the material works in the exact process.

Capital markets and policymakers can miss this because the quantities look small. Thirty grams of Yb-176 sounds trivial next to battery metals. Fifty kilograms per year of silicon-28 sounds irrelevant next to commodity silicon. One hundred tonnes of enriched lithium sounds small compared with lithium chemicals for electric vehicles. The mistake is to compare mass rather than role. A gram-scale material can gate a radiopharmaceutical dose. A kilogram-scale gas can gate a 300 mm quantum wafer flow. A tens-of-tonnes inventory can gate a fusion fuel cycle. In each case, the isotope is not a commodity input; it is a control point whose absence can idle a much larger asset.

The signals to watch are concrete

The signals to watch are concrete. For lithium-6, watch actual kilogram shipments in 2027, license filings, process environmental permits, measured enrichment levels, cost per kilogram, and blanket developers signing take-or-pay or qualification contracts rather than non-binding frameworks. For silicon-28, watch accepted commercial shipments from ASP, prototype metrics from Quobly/Soitec/ST’s Crolles path, wafer-yield evidence, and the identity of industrial-gas counterparties. For Yb-176 and Lu-177, watch Kinectrics’ 500 gram annualized run rate, DOE SIPRC’s CD milestones, SHINE and ITM shipment reliability, reactor irradiation priority access, and whether future radioligand approvals strain capacity again.

One fact would weaken the whole analogy: if each sector secures qualified isotope supply at the required

One fact would weaken the whole analogy: if each sector secures qualified isotope supply at the required scale before the downstream asset is ready, and project delays migrate elsewhere without recurring material bottlenecks, stable-isotope enrichment becomes a niche procurement issue rather than a strategic control point. The early evidence does not prove a broad industrial chokepoint, but it justifies treating isotope enrichment as a small-mass, high-leverage constraint that can shape spend paths, vendor leverage, and national industrial policy far beyond its tonnage.
