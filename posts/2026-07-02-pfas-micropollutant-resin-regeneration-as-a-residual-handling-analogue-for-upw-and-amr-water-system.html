PFAS Micropollutant Resin Regeneration and the Residual-Handling Constraint

PFAS policy is usually framed as a removal race. Regulators tighten limits, utilities choose granular activated carbon, ion exchange, reverse osmosis, or nanofiltration, and engineering firms build vessels to get the water back into compliance. That is the visible part of the story.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 12 min read

In brief

  • 2029 remains the baseline EPA compliance deadline; 2031 is still only a proposed PFOA/PFOS option, while EPA also proposes rescinding some non-PFOA/PFOS limits.
  • Orange County cites 35 systems and about $275 million of PFAS treatment buildout.
  • Yorba Linda treats up to 25 MGD with 22 ion-exchange vessels.
  • Calgon targets 12,300 tons/year of added reactivation capacity by Q1 2028.

PFAS treatment does not end at the vessel

The less visible part starts after capture. A treatment train that removes PFAS from water has not eliminated PFAS; it has concentrated it into spent carbon, exhausted resin, regeneration brine, still bottoms, membrane concentrate, landfill leachate risk, or an acceptance file for incineration or injection. EPA lists granular activated carbon, anion exchange, reverse osmosis, and nanofiltration as treatment options. The harder question is what each option leaves behind. (EPA PFAS drinking-water overview)

That makes the public thesis narrower than a generic "PFAS is growing" story. As municipal treatment moves from pilots into fleets of plants, the control point can migrate from who captures the molecule to who can accept, regenerate, destroy, document, or legally warehouse the concentrated residual.

Regulation sets a synchronized demand pulse

EPA finalized drinking-water limits in April 2024, including 4 ng/L maximum contaminant levels for PFOA and PFOS, 10 ng/L limits for PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA, and a Hazard Index for mixtures. The baseline compliance date remains April 2029. EPA's 2026 proposal would keep the PFOA and PFOS standards while allowing some eligible systems to seek up to two extra years, which would move those compounds to April 2031. The same proposal would also rescind the PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and Hazard Index portions of the 2024 rule, so the timing pulse is clearer for PFOA/PFOS than for the full PFAS package. (EPA final PFAS rule) (EPA proposed compliance extension)

That still matters because the rule creates a timed PFOA/PFOS demand pulse. Even if some systems get extra time, every installed adsorber, membrane, or polishing train still needs a residual outlet. The front end may be paced by construction and procurement, but the back end is paced by who will take the material, under what permit, with what proof. How broad that pulse becomes for other PFAS compounds remains unsettled until EPA finishes the proposal and any related legal or state response plays out.

Scale turns removal into a fleet problem

Orange County shows why PFAS treatment stops looking like a single-project procurement and starts looking like a portfolio logistics problem. State advisory levels helped force nearly 60 well shutdowns in the basin, and the Orange County Water District moved toward more than 30 PFAS treatment facilities. A 2021 ASCE account cited 35 systems and roughly $275 million of capital expense. (ASCE on Orange County PFAS systems)

Named facilities show the range. Yorba Linda's plant is one of the larger ion-exchange installations in the public record, with 22 vessels arranged as 11 lead-lag trains and up to 25 million gallons per day of capacity. Tustin Main Street discloses 9.216 MGD. SCV Water's Santa Clara-Honby facility discloses 2.88 MGD and restored those wells in 2024. Publicly, these look like treatment-plant success stories. Operationally, they also imply years of future media changeouts, hauling, and endpoint contracting. (Wastewater Digest on Yorba Linda) (Tustin Main Street plant) (SCV Water treatment facility)

Orange County's pilot work also shows why media choice can postpone the bottleneck without removing it. In the OCWD pilot report, Evoqua PSR2+ showed 7.1 months to initial PFOA breakthrough, Calgon CalRes 2301 and Purolite PFA694E each showed 4.3 months, and Cyclopure DEXSORB+ showed 2.7 months. Better media can reduce truck rolls and changeout frequency. It does not erase the residual endpoint; it changes how often the operator has to use it. (OCWD PFAS pilot report)

Regeneration changes the question, not the need for an endpoint

Single-use resin keeps the operating model simple at the plant. Once breakthrough approaches, a contractor removes the exhausted bed and replaces it. That simplicity just pushes complexity downstream to incineration, landfill, or another approved outlet.

Regenerable systems change the residual form rather than making it disappear. SERDP's Willow Grove work found that regenerating PFAS-loaded anion exchange resin required concentrated salt brine and high alcohol co-solvent, with the life-cycle result heavily shaped by how the brine or still bottoms were treated afterward. In other words, the resin bead is only the first half of the system. (SERDP Willow Grove project)

Bellmawr, New Jersey, makes the tradeoff concrete. Veolia's case materials describe a PFAS-selective single-use anion-exchange system in lead-lag operation, with exhausted resin removed for incineration and replacement expected after roughly 12 to 15 months in the temporary configuration. A later Veolia note says the permanent system, installed in 2023, had still not needed a media exchange and would send spent resin to permitted hazardous-waste incineration. That is an elegant wellhead solution, but it still depends on off-site thermal treatment and the proof that treatment remains acceptable. (Veolia Bellmawr case study) (Veolia Bellmawr case PDF) (Veolia ion-exchange implementation note)

The best counterexample in the report is the Australian regenerable ion-exchange case study. It reports multiple groundwater systems and one municipal drinking-water system, with 26 regeneration cycles since 2019 and no public indication of PFAS buildup or resin bead breakdown. That does not kill the residual-handling thesis. It refines it. If regenerable systems win, the winner is not just the resin supplier; it is the integrated loop that can regenerate media, handle concentrated eluate, recycle or destroy regenerant, and document the outcome. (Australian Water e-Journal RIEX case study)

Acceptance capacity is where the constraint can tighten

EPA's 2026 destruction-and-disposal guidance is the hinge because it makes clear that the endpoint question is still unsettled. The guidance discusses thermal treatment, landfills, and underground injection as large-scale capacity pathways, while also noting that, as of January 2026, there is no federal PFAS-specific disposal requirement that applies solely because a residual contains PFAS. That sounds flexible, but it also shifts risk into contract terms, state policy, insurance, and future liability allocation. (EPA 2026 destruction and disposal guidance)

EPA's own example costs show that the route still changes economics.

Residual routeEPA example disposal-plus-replacement cost
GAC reactivation$1.63/lb
Landfill$2.21/lb
Incineration$3.35/lb

The same guidance says permitted Class I injection wells may have lower release potential under some conditions, but it also notes logistics constraints, limited wells receiving off-site PFAS material, and no national inventory of which Class I wells can accept PFAS-bearing waste. EPA reports 886 Class I wells nationwide, including 745 non-hazardous industrial-municipal wells and 141 hazardous wells, but says the PFAS-accepting subset is not nationally mapped. EPA also says the effectiveness of high-temperature combustion for complete PFAS destruction and byproduct control is still not fully understood, despite broader field evidence. That is why the most defensible version of the thesis is not "residual handling is already the universal blocker." It is that the next bottleneck may tighten around permitted, verified, financeable acceptance capacity for PFAS-bearing residuals.

Who gets leverage if the bottleneck moves

The companies with leverage in that world are not limited to front-end treatment vendors. Clean Harbors highlights PFAS incineration testing at its Aragonite hazardous-waste incinerator and says its 2024 EPA and DOD-associated work demonstrated commercial-scale performance. EPA's own research page is more restrained: Clean Harbors and Reworld worked with EPA under cooperative research agreements to evaluate emissions and operating conditions, and EPA presents that work as helping determine the conditions for PFAS destruction. The gap between company proof and regulator confidence is itself part of the control point. (Clean Harbors PFAS page) (EPA incineration research update)

Calgon Carbon, owned by Kuraray, offers a cleaner example of a published relief valve. The company says it is adding 12,300 tons per year of reactivated carbon capacity at Columbus, Ohio, with about $100 million of capital investment and a first-quarter 2028 startup target, explicitly tied to U.S. PFAS drinking-water demand. That matters because spent GAC that can be reactivated is a different residual problem from exhausted single-use resin or regenerant still bottoms. Relief in one branch of the chain does not automatically solve the others. (Kuraray on Calgon expansion)

That is also why value capture fragments. Engineering firms capture design and permitting spend. Vessel and system integrators capture installed equipment. Resin and carbon suppliers capture consumables. Service providers capture sampling, transport, changeout, regeneration, incineration, landfill, injection, and documentation. Regulators and insurers influence which endpoint remains financeable. One PFAS plant can support several different bottleneck owners over its life.

The UPW and AMR analogy is possible, not proven

The report's analogies matter only if they stay narrow. A LANXESS release grouped PFAS, micropollutants, fluoride wastewater, and ultrapure water inside one specialty-ion-exchange product pitch. A secondary industry account of Samsung Austin Semiconductor's water system described ion exchange inside a larger UPW train and wastewater projects that reduced chemical use and reclaimed a large share of makeup water. That is enough to show vendor adjacency and a shared operating pattern: tighter purity or reuse targets can create harder residual problems downstream. It is not enough to claim the same bottleneck has already appeared in semiconductor water systems. (Ultrafacility Portal on semiconductor water systems) (LANXESS filtration release)

The antimicrobial-resistance analogy is earlier still. WHO frames AMR as a major health and economic threat, and UNEP treats wastewater and related waste handling as part of the environmental side of the problem. That supports a procedural comparison, not a chemistry claim. If future AMR-related water rules push operators toward tighter capture and proof obligations, they may rediscover the same pattern: measurement and front-end removal are easier to announce than destruction, sludge handling, brine management, or proof of non-transfer. (WHO AMR fact sheet) (UNEP wastewater guidance for manufacturing)

What would prove this wrong, and what to watch

There are three real counterarguments. First, residual handling may remain a cost line rather than the binding bottleneck. Yorba Linda moved from March 2021 construction to December 2021 operation, Bellmawr deployed quickly, and SCV Water restored the Santa Clara-Honby wells in 2024 with an $11.5 million ion-exchange facility. Those are reminders that siting, procurement, financing, and civil execution can dominate the early project clock. (Wastewater Digest on Yorba Linda) (Veolia Bellmawr case study) (SCV Water treatment facility)

Second, technology can move the endpoint. If regenerable resin plus on-site destruction becomes cheap and verifiable, single-use logistics lose leverage. If GAC reactivation expands quickly enough, carbon systems may route around incineration constraints. If regulators settle on durable standards for incineration, landfill acceptance, and injection, uncertainty becomes easier to price.

Third, water chemistry can still dominate technology choice. Veolia's 2026 implementation note says ion exchange works best in cleaner groundwater with low total organic carbon, low suspended solids, and manageable competing ions, while surface water and more complex wastewater can favor carbon, reverse osmosis, or additional pretreatment. In that world, the decisive control point moves back toward pretreatment and site-specific design. (Veolia ion-exchange implementation note)

The thesis weakens materially if utilities begin publishing spent-media contracts that show abundant acceptance at stable prices and short lead times, if regulators lock in durable destruction standards that resolve emissions and byproduct disputes, and if regenerable or reactivated loops scale without simply transferring the problem into brine, solvent, landfill leachate, or air permitting.

What to watch:

  • Utility bids that specify spent-media ownership, disposal outlet, and indemnity terms.
  • GAC reactivation expansions, incinerator test data, and Class I well permit changes.
  • State restrictions on PFAS incineration or landfill acceptance.
  • Media breakthrough curves and actual first changeout dates.
  • UPW or AMR projects only when they create documented residual obligations.

PFAS treatment will still require vessels, media, membranes, labs, engineers, and project finance. The sharper claim is that the strategic control point may sit one layer below the plant, at the legally durable endpoint for the contaminant after the water looks clean. If that migration happens, the most valuable position will belong to whoever can close the loop with capacity, documentation, and acceptable cost.
