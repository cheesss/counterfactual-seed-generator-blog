PFAS / Micropollutant Resin Regeneration As A Residual-Handling Analogue For UPW And AMR Water Systems

PFAS policy is usually framed as a removal race. Regulators lower the allowable concentration. Utilities choose granular activated carbon, ion exchange resin, reverse osmosis, or some hybrid. Engineering firms design vessels. Media suppliers sell carbon or resin.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 14 min read

The first tell is that this looks like a removal race

PFAS policy is usually framed as a removal race. Regulators lower the allowable concentration. Utilities choose granular activated carbon, ion exchange resin, reverse osmosis, or some hybrid. Engineering firms design vessels. Media suppliers sell carbon or resin. Communities watch the compliance deadline.

That is the visible story. The less visible constraint starts after the contaminant has been captured.

A water system that removes PFAS has not eliminated PFAS. It has concentrated a dilute, politically explosive contaminant into spent carbon, exhausted anion-exchange resin, regeneration brine, still bottoms, membrane concentrate, incinerator stack data, landfill leachate, or an injection-well acceptance file. The front-end technology may be a water-treatment problem. The back-end endpoint is a residual-handling problem.

The one-sentence thesis is simple: as PFAS removal moves from pilot programs into fleetwide municipal, industrial, semiconductor, and future AMR-related water systems, the scarce control point may migrate from “who can capture the molecule” to “who can accept, regenerate, destroy, document, or legally warehouse the concentrated residual.”

The regulation sets the clock

The timing comes from regulation. EPA finalized enforceable drinking-water limits in April 2024, including 4.0 ng/L maximum contaminant levels for PFOA and PFOS and 10 ng/L limits for PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA, plus a Hazard Index for mixtures. EPA’s 2026 proposal would keep the PFOA and PFOS standards but allow eligible systems to seek up to two extra years, potentially moving compliance for those compounds from 2029 to 2031. That extension would soften installation pressure, but it would not remove the physical question: every adsorber, membrane, or polishing train still needs a residual outlet. Sources: EPA PFAS drinking water rule and proposed compliance extension.

The operating mechanism is well established. Granular activated carbon adsorbs PFAS into a porous carbon bed. Anion exchange resin binds PFAS through charged functional groups and hydrophobic interactions. Reverse osmosis and nanofiltration reject PFAS into a concentrate stream. On paper, each can produce compliant water. EPA lists granular activated carbon, anion exchange, reverse osmosis, and nanofiltration as treatment technologies for drinking-water PFAS control. The difference is what each technology leaves behind.

Waste and qualification are the quiet gates

A single-use resin strategy simplifies the utility’s day-to-day operating model. When the resin approaches breakthrough, a contractor removes the bed and replaces it. That lowers process complexity at the water plant. It also turns the spent resin into a concentrated waste that typically goes to incineration, hazardous-waste landfill, or another approved endpoint. A regenerable resin strategy avoids discarding the entire bed, but it creates a smaller, more concentrated regenerant stream. SERDP’s Willow Grove work found that regeneration of PFAS-loaded anion exchange resins required concentrated salt brine and high alcohol co-solvent, with downstream treatment of the brine or still bottoms controlling much of the life-cycle result. The resin bead is not the whole system; it is the front end of a residual logistics chain.

Named cases show where the constraint moves

Orange County shows the scale problem. In California’s Orange County Groundwater Basin, state advisory levels led multiple retail providers to shut nearly 60 wells. The Orange County Water District moved toward more than 30 PFAS treatment facilities, with 35 systems cited in a 2021 ASCE project account and capital expenses around $275 million. Yorba Linda’s plant became one of the largest ion-exchange PFAS treatment facilities in the United States, with 22 vessels arranged as 11 lead-lag trains and up to 25 million gallons per day of treatment capacity. The impressive detail is not only the plant size. It is the industrial stack behind the plant: six consulting engineering firms, vessels, resin systems, prefiltration, backup power, grant and WIFIA financing, future media replacement, and unresolved long-term residual routing.

Orange County’s pilot data also shows why media selection can appear to solve the problem while pushing it downstream. OCWD tested multiple GACs, ion-exchange resins, and alternative adsorbents on local groundwater. Evoqua PSR2+ showed the longest ion-exchange media life for PFOA and short-chain compounds in the pilot, while other single-use resins such as Purolite Purofine PFA694E and Calgon CalRes 2301 were also part of the evaluated set. Longer bed life matters because it reduces changeout frequency, truck rolls, and residual tonnage per gallon treated. But it does not remove the residual endpoint. A better resin postpones the handoff; it does not erase it.

Regeneration is the sharper test

Bellmawr, New Jersey, makes the same tradeoff easier to see. Veolia’s case materials describe a PFAS-selective single-use anion exchange system with lead-lag operation. Exhausted resin was to be removed and incinerated, with replacement expected after roughly 12 to 15 months in the temporary configuration. A later Veolia technical note says the permanent system, installed in 2023, had treated water to non-detect levels with no media exchange yet and that the spent resin would undergo high-temperature incineration at permitted hazardous-waste facilities. The result is operationally elegant at the wellhead. The critical external dependency is incineration access, emissions proof, and liability acceptance.

The best counterexample comes from regenerable systems. An Australian five-year case study of regenerable ion exchange reported multiple PFAS groundwater treatment systems and one municipal drinking-water system, with 26 regeneration cycles since 2019 and no residual PFAS buildup or resin bead breakdown reported in the public paper. The authors explicitly frame regenerable ion exchange as a way to minimize spent-media waste and reduce exposure to future landfill and thermal-treatment liabilities. That does not falsify the residual-handling thesis. It sharpens it. The winner in such a system is not simply the resin; it is the integrated loop that can regenerate media, handle concentrated eluate, recycle or destroy regenerant, document performance, and avoid creating a more dangerous waste stream than the one it replaced.

The real gate is disposal capacity, not vessels

EPA’s 2026 destruction and disposal guidance is the hinge. The guidance discusses three large-scale capacity technologies: thermal treatment, landfills, and underground injection. It also notes that, as of January 2026, no federal PFAS-specific disposal requirement applies solely because a residual contains PFAS. That legal gap can look like flexibility. It can also shift risk into contract language, state policy, public opposition, insurance, and future CERCLA claims. EPA states that permitted Class I injection wells may have lower potential for releases under certain conditions, but it also identifies long-timescale uncertainties, limited wells receiving off-site PFAS material, logistics limits, and no national information on which Class I wells can accept PFAS or off-site PFAS waste. In the same guidance, EPA says the effectiveness of high-temperature combustion for complete PFAS destruction and byproduct control is still not fully understood, despite expanding field-scale evidence.

Those caveats define the bottleneck. The deepest nameable constraint is not a shortage of vessels. It is permitted, verified, and financeable acceptance capacity for PFAS-bearing residuals. That capacity lives in hazardous-waste incinerators, GAC reactivation kilns, Subtitle C landfills, Class I injection wells, analytical laboratories, state permitting files, and the documents that prove the residual did not simply move from water into air, leachate, or long-term legal liability.

Who controls the constraint

Some actors are already positioning around that endpoint. Clean Harbors points to PFAS incineration testing at its Aragonite hazardous-waste incinerator and says its 2024 EPA and DOD-associated work demonstrated commercial-scale performance. EPA’s own research page is more restrained: Clean Harbors and Reworld worked with EPA under cooperative research agreements to evaluate emissions and operating conditions, and EPA describes the work as helping determine operating conditions for PFAS destruction. That distinction matters. Company proof and regulatory confidence are related but not identical assets.

Calgon Carbon, owned by Kuraray, is adding 12,300 tons per year of reactivated carbon production capacity at its Columbus, Ohio plant, with about $100 million of capital investment and a first-quarter 2028 startup target. The company explicitly ties the expansion to U.S. PFAS drinking-water demand. That is a concrete relief valve for one part of the residual chain: spent GAC that can be reactivated rather than disposed. It does not directly solve exhausted single-use resin, regeneration still bottoms, membrane concentrate, or locations too small or distant to fit large reactivation logistics.

The value-capture map is therefore more granular than “PFAS treatment.” Engineering firms capture design, permitting, and construction-management spend. Vessel and system integrators capture installed equipment. Resin and carbon suppliers capture consumable media economics. Service providers capture sampling, changeout, transport, regeneration, incineration, landfill, injection, and documentation. Insurers and plaintiffs’ lawyers affect the cost of residual liability. Regulators determine which endpoint remains acceptable. The same treatment plant can distribute value to several different control points across its life.

The AMR and UPW parallels

That is why the market can miss the constraint. Capital budgets are visible. Treatment capacity is visible. A press release can show beige vessels, flow rate, and a ribbon cutting. Residual contracts are less visible. Media exhaustion curves live in pilot reports, not headlines. Disposal outlets are often private, regional, and condition-specific. A $275 million program of treatment systems may be easier to model than the thirty-year stream of media replacement, hauling, incineration, landfill, injection, monitoring, and liability documentation.

The analogy to ultrapure water is not exact, but it is useful. Semiconductor fabs use large multistage water systems that include reverse osmosis, ion exchange, UV, degasification, and ultrafiltration to make ultrapure water. Samsung Austin Semiconductor described ion exchange as part of its UPW process and described wastewater projects that reduced chemical use, eliminated solids, and reclaimed a large share of UPW makeup. LANXESS, in a 2026 filtration release, links PFAS, pharmaceutical micropollutants, fluoride wastewater, and ultrapure water through specialty ion-exchange resin platforms. The common pattern is that tighter purity targets and higher water reuse tend to concentrate impurities into harder residuals. The stronger the front-end polishing train, the more valuable the back-end residual design becomes.

The antimicrobial-resistance water analogy is earlier and less proven. WHO treats AMR as a major global health and economic threat, and environmental agencies increasingly discuss wastewater, antimicrobial residues, resistant bacteria, and resistance genes as part of the One Health problem. The AMR water-control stack may not settle on PFAS-style resins; it may use disinfection, membranes, advanced oxidation, activated carbon, biological treatment, surveillance, or source controls. The useful comparison is not the molecule. It is the operating pattern. If regulators or pharmaceutical customers push water systems toward tighter control of antibiotic residues, ARB, or ARGs, the industry may again discover that measurement and capture are easier to announce than residual destruction, brine management, sludge handling, and proof of non-transfer.

What would prove this wrong, and what to watch

There are serious counterarguments. The first is that PFAS residual handling may be a cost line, not a binding bottleneck. Yorba Linda moved from March 2021 construction to December 2021 operation. Bellmawr deployed treatment quickly. SCV Water restored the Santa Clara and Honby wells in 2024 with a $11.5 million ion-exchange facility. These cases show that permitting, real estate, financing, and procurement can dominate near-term deployment, while residual handling remains contracted in the background.

The second counterargument is that technology can migrate the endpoint. If regenerable resin plus onsite destruction proves cheap, certifiable, and robust across water chemistries, single-use media logistics lose leverage. If GAC reactivation capacity expands quickly enough, carbon’s installed base may route around incinerator constraints. If EPA’s compliance extension reduces simultaneous demand, disposal capacity may grow without a crunch. If state and federal regulators converge on clear standards for incineration, injection, and landfill acceptance, uncertainty becomes manageable.

The third counterargument is that water chemistry, not residuals, may decide the market. Veolia’s 2026 note says ion exchange works best in clean groundwater with low TOC, low suspended solids, and manageable competing ions, while surface water and complex wastewater can favor carbon, reverse osmosis, or additional pretreatment. In that case, the dominant control point moves back to pretreatment, system design, and site-specific chemistry.

The thesis weakens if three facts appear together: utilities publish disposal and media-changeout contracts showing abundant PFAS residual acceptance at stable prices and short lead times; regulators publish durable, accepted destruction or disposal standards that resolve emissions and byproduct uncertainty; and regenerable or reactivated loops scale across small and large systems without transferring the problem into brine, solvent, landfill leachate, or air permitting. Until then, the prudent way to read PFAS treatment is as a system of residual endpoints, not just a system of vessels.

The signals to monitor are concrete. Utility bids that specify spent-media ownership, disposal outlet, and indemnity terms are more informative than generic PFAS treatment announcements. GAC reactivation expansions, hazardous-waste incinerator test data, Class I well permit modifications, state restrictions on PFAS incineration, and Subtitle C landfill acceptance policies tell more about long-term capacity than nominal treatment MGD. Media breakthrough curves, bed-volume claims, and actual first changeout dates reveal whether lifecycle cost estimates survive contact with local water chemistry. Semiconductor water-reuse projects and AMR wastewater rules deserve attention only where they create concentrated residual streams with documented handling requirements.

PFAS removal will still require vessels, resins, carbon, membranes, engineers, labs, and project finance. But the strategic control point may sit one layer below the plant: the legally durable endpoint for the contaminant after the water looks clean. The companies and public agencies that can close that loop with capacity, documentation, and acceptable cost will hold a different kind of leverage from those that only sell the front-end capture device.
