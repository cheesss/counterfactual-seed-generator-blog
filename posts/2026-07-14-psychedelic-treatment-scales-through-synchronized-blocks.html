The launch bottleneck may be conversion-ready treatment capacity

Most debate still treats psychedelic-treatment capacity as room count or calendar slots. The more precise unit is a conversion-ready synchronized block: protected room time, paired monitors, discharge buffer, and response coverage that must all be available at once.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-14 · 9 min read

In brief

  • July 13, 2026 guidance kept 2 monitors in the investigational model.
  • Oregon recorded 4,628 individual sessions and 747 group sessions in 2025.
  • ZULRESSO carried a 60-hour monitored block before commercial exit.
  • The constraint appears only when approval, payment, and site conversion align.

The bottleneck appears only after the earlier gates open

FDA's July 13, 2026 clinical-investigation guidance kept the two-monitor structure and the 15-minute physician-response concept while replacing the draft's 12-hour wording with "several hours or longer." The July 2026 document governs clinical investigations. It is final guidance, not a product label or REMS. (FDA guidance) (FDA status page)

That makes the mechanism concrete, but only under specific conditions. Approval must clear first. Controlled-substance implementation, coding, coverage, and site payment must then make delivery lawful and worth offering. Only after those gates open do providers have to convert ordinary clinics into reimbursable monitored blocks. The live thesis is therefore a launch-period conversion gate, not a claim that psychedelic care already faces a national room shortage. (COMPASS 10-Q) (COMPASS July 2026 update)

The named cases show a family of delivery architectures

The evidence does not point to one universal operating model. It points to a range of delivery architectures, from tightly specified monitored products to group and off-site systems that loosen the block on purpose.

One case belongs outside that table because it failed before capacity could matter. FDA's August 2024 complete response letter to Lykos asked for another adequate and well-controlled trial and raised concerns about data reliability, generalizability, intervention standardization, auditing, and discharge standards. In that case, evidence and governance bound before room count or monitor supply could bind. (FDA complete response letter)

Oregon's live market looks demand-bound

Oregon is the cleanest live test of whether legal concurrency turns into used capacity. Administration must occur in licensed service centers. Group sessions can reach 25 clients, but dose-dependent facilitator ratios, occupancy limits, and a minimum of 25 square feet per person present still bind. Sessions that run past 11:59 p.m. trigger next-day reporting. On paper, that looks like a market where floorplan and staff coordination should dominate. (Oregon group rule) (Oregon session rule)

The usage data point elsewhere. Oregon reported 5,935 clients in 2025, with 4,628 individual sessions and 747 group sessions. Average group size ran between 3.03 and 3.62 in 2025, then fell to 2.46 in the first quarter of 2026. Individual sessions accounted for 86.1% of reported administration events in 2025 and 88.38% in 2026 Q1. That is not a system pressing hard against its legal group ceiling. It is a system where individual delivery remains the operating norm. (Oregon data dashboard) Oregon changed reporting methods in 2026, some averages are center-calculated, and clients may opt out of data collection. (Oregon data archive)

Price and format help explain the gap. Oregon says it does not regulate prices. SPACE in Portland advertises a six-hour private all-inclusive package at $925 and a five-hour public-group package at $500. A peer-reviewed workforce survey found mean planned session charges of $1,388 among Oregon trainees. Early demand could still run hot at single sites: 2023 reporting on EPIC Healing Eugene put its waitlist above 3,000. (Oregon access page) (SPACE pricing) (Oregon workforce study) (EPIC reporting)

But waitlists, posted packages, and statewide session counts still point to a market where willingness to pay, privacy, group matching, and provider risk tolerance may bind before room supply does. (Oregon data dashboard)

Colorado and NUNM show the relief valves

Colorado shows how fast the architecture can loosen. Current rules allow one facilitator for up to four clients, groups up to 64, and off-site administration in some circumstances. Once those permissions exist, the constraint moves away from fixed-room count and toward facilitator supply, client aggregation, supervision, transport, and compliance outside a single building. (Colorado facilitator rules)

NUNM's low-income study at Chariot makes the same point in miniature. The program planned 24 participants in four groups of six, moved preparation and integration to video, and used the licensed center only for the administration sessions. That is not proof of commercial scale. It is proof that operators can preserve scarce in-room time by moving remote-compatible work out of the room. (NUNM study) (LIGPATD trial record)

The cycle matters more than the headline hours

The timing window is short if federal approval arrives on the sponsor's timetable. On July 7, 2026, COMPASS said rolling NDA submission and initial FDA review were underway, expected completion of submission in the fourth quarter of 2026, and pointed to a potential first-half 2027 launch. Six days later, FDA finalized its clinical-investigation guidance without settling the commercial staffing or monitoring coefficients. That combination speeds the calendar while leaving the size of the block unresolved. (COMPASS July 2026 update) (FDA status page)

That is why cycle time matters more than the headline session length. The tightness horizon sits in the final prelaunch period and the first six to twelve months after any federal launch, potentially running through 2027 on COMPASS's stated schedule. If labels stay heavy and payment works, sites will have to certify rooms, recruit trained staff, set up documentation and audio-visual workflows, and protect long blocks before referrals arrive. If payment misses the labor, buffer, and overrun cost, the same rooms can remain idle and reimbursement becomes the binding gate. (COMPASS 10-Q)

The base rates narrow how long scarcity can last

The historical record argues against a single outcome. ECT shows that some multi-resource mental-health procedures can stay coupled for decades because anesthesia, assisted ventilation or oxygenation, specialized staff, and recovery are part of the treatment itself. TMS shows the other path. Remove anesthesia and recovery, and the capacity equation changes even if patients still return for repeated sessions. (CMS ECT memo) (CMS TMS LCD)

The product comparators tell the same two-sided story. SPRAVATO shows that a monitored outpatient block can become routine once product rules, site workflow, and payment line up. CMS created G2082 and G2083 to give that block a billing pathway, and FDA's 2026 REMS actions preserved the monitored-setting architecture. (SPRAVATO label) (CMS 2020 code rule) (SPRAVATO 2026 action)

ZULRESSO shows the harsher lesson. It carried a 60-hour monitored infusion, lost commercial availability on December 31, 2024, and had its NDA withdrawn in April 2025 after a lower-burden oral alternative reached the market. Scarcity can persist. It can also vanish when the route changes. (Sage 10-K) (FDA Zurzuvae announcement)

Who controls the constraint, and where value could sit

The control points sit upstream of the room. FDA and any future REMS author decide whether the commercial block keeps two dedicated monitors, allows pooled coverage, or tolerates remote observation. CMS and commercial payers decide whether the block pays as a viable bundle or as an undercompensated burden. Sponsors decide how much site qualification, training, equipment, and documentation sit inside the protocol. Hospitals, psychiatric networks, service centers, and trial sites then decide whether to commit rooms and staff. (COMPASS 10-Q)

Value capture follows that chain only if the heavier model survives and reimbursement covers the full block. In that case, sponsor drug sales come first, and qualified sites may capture facility and professional payments. Training, scheduling, compliance, and emergency-coverage vendors could participate.

But the evidence stops well short of proving durable rents. There is no sourced dataset here for site-level utilization, monitor overtime, contribution margins, contract terms, or a public pure-play in rooms or staffing. The only direct public exposure in the evidence is COMPASS as sponsor; SPRAVATO functions as the approved comparator, not as proof that every future network will earn the same economics. (COMPASS release) (SPRAVATO 2026 action)

What would prove this wrong

This thesis fails under concrete operating outcomes, not abstract skepticism.

  • A product-specific label or REMS allows one monitor, pooled or remote observation, or a short fixed release window.
  • The first two commercial quarters show ordinary psychiatric or procedure networks meeting demand without repeated waitlists, capacity-coded denials, monitor overtime, or room buildout.
  • Adequately reimbursed sites keep substantial compliant capacity idle while demand fails on price, eligibility, travel, or participant preference.
  • Group, off-site, or remotely decomposed delivery supplies a large share of completed sessions.
  • A shorter-acting or oral alternative captures referrals before monitored-session sites fill up.

The missing numbers all sit inside the block: site-level utilization, active trained-worker hours, empirical overrun tails, site-payment terms, contribution margins, and room-conversion costs. Until those numbers appear, the honest claim stays limited. Conversion-ready capacity is a plausible post-approval gate. It is not yet a proven national shortage or a proven moat.

What to watch

  • Any first product-specific label or REMS that cuts monitoring from two dedicated monitors to one or permits pooled or remote observation.
  • COMPASS NDA and launch disclosures that state site-payment terms, staffing assumptions, room requirements, or REMS-readiness buildout ahead of a potential first-half 2027 launch.
  • The first two commercial quarters of any approved product for waitlists, capacity-coded denials, monitor overtime, or high utilization of compliant rooms.
  • Oregon quarterly data for average group size rising materially above 2.46 and the individual-session share falling from 88.38%.
  • Colorado operating records that show off-site administration or larger groups working in routine practice rather than only on paper.
  • Any approval of a shorter-acting or oral alternative that diverts referrals from long monitored sessions.
