High-Recovery Industrial Water Reuse And The Brine Outlet Bottleneck

Water reuse projects usually present themselves through the clean parts of the system: membrane skids, ultraviolet reactors, polished water, drought resilience, and the arithmetic of local supply. The harder industrial question starts after the recovery number goes up.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 11 min read

The real unit is where the salt goes

Water reuse projects usually present themselves through the clean parts of the system: membrane skids, ultraviolet reactors, polished water, drought resilience, and the arithmetic of local supply. The harder industrial question starts after the recovery number goes up. A high-recovery system can turn a large waste stream into a smaller one, but it cannot make the salts, metals, nutrients, antiscalants, or trace contaminants vanish. It changes their shape. The diluted problem becomes a concentrated residual.

The hidden constraint is residual handling: a legally usable, physically connected, commercially tolerable outlet for brine or salt residuals. Membrane performance matters, but the controlling node can sit outside the treatment plant: an ocean outfall, a brine line, a deep injection formation, an evaporation pond, a crystallizer, a landfill acceptance route, or a discharge permit. In investment language, the control point is not always the membrane supplier. It may be the party that owns the last mile of salt disposal.

The operating mechanism is simple and unforgiving. Industrial and municipal reuse systems push water through microfiltration, reverse osmosis, advanced oxidation, carbon, or related treatment steps. Higher recovery means more product water per unit of feed. It also means the reject stream gets smaller and more concentrated. That residual then has to fit into someone else's permit, pipe, geology, land base, or solids market. When the outlet exists, high recovery can reduce cost. When it does not, the project may have to buy capacity, build a pipeline, amend a discharge permit, find injection geology, reserve evaporation acreage, or add thermal treatment.

Named cases show where the constraint moves

Southern California shows the difference between a water-technology story and a salt-outlet story. The Inland Empire Brine Line is a regional brine conveyance system that transports salty wastewater from inland treatment plants and industries to the Orange County Sanitation District system for treatment and ocean discharge. SAWPA describes it as a way to remove roughly 500,000 pounds of salt per day from the Santa Ana River watershed and support desalters, recycled water, and industrial dischargers that otherwise face total dissolved solids limits in inland sewers (https://sawpa.gov/inland-empire-brine-line/). The system is described by local agencies as roughly 93 miles long with about 30 MGD of capacity (https://www.sbvmwd.com/projects-and-planning/inland-empire-brine-line/).

That infrastructure turns salt disposal into a contracted service. SAWPA's connection materials quote indicative disposal costs of about $0.01 per gallon for direct disposal, about $0.05 per gallon for trucked disposal, and as much as $0.25 per gallon elsewhere in the Los Angeles Basin for comparable waste streams (https://sawpa.gov/inland-empire-brine-line/connect/). Those numbers are not a market index; they are a local agency's customer-facing comparison. They still reveal the economic shape. A brine outlet can behave like a toll road for industries and water agencies that need salt moved out of an inland basin.

The same system also shows why capacity rights matter before concrete is poured. Yucaipa Valley Water District's Henry N. Wochholz Regional Water Recycling Facility already discharges reverse-osmosis concentrate to the Brine Line. In late 2024, SAWPA meeting materials recorded Yucaipa's request for about 0.505 MGD of additional treatment and disposal capacity, tied to a facility expansion expected around December 2026. The agency wanted capacity secured before awarding a construction contract (https://sawpa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/PA24Committee_Presentations_12032024_WEB.pdf). That is not a generic sustainability statement. It is a project-level gating item: the brine outlet had to be reserved before the expansion could proceed.

Scale is possible when the outlet already exists

Orange County's Groundwater Replenishment System shows the opposite condition: scale becomes possible when the outlet is already embedded in the system. OCWD and OC San operate a 130 MGD potable reuse system that uses microfiltration, reverse osmosis, and ultraviolet advanced oxidation to produce purified water (https://www.ocwd.com/gwrs/). The residual stream does not need a new inland sink. OC San states that final effluent from Plant No. 2 is combined with OCWD reverse-osmosis concentrate and released through a 120-inch outfall extending five miles offshore under an ocean monitoring program governed by EPA Region 9 and the Santa Ana Regional Water Quality Control Board (https://ocsan.gov/ocean-monitoring-lab-program/). The lesson is not that brine is always binding. It is that large-scale reuse often depends on a pre-existing residual outlet that sits below the headline water-production number.

Pure Water Monterey puts the same issue into permitting language. The advanced water purification facility uses ozone, membrane filtration, reverse osmosis, and UV/hydrogen peroxide, then injects purified water into the Seaside groundwater basin (https://www.montereyonewater.org/262/Purification-Technology). Expansion documents show a peak product capacity of roughly 7.6 MGD and a reverse-osmosis concentrate stream approaching 1.78 MGD. The concentrate is discharged through the existing regional ocean outfall, but that required updated dilution analysis and water-board permitting under California Ocean Plan rules (https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/rwqcb3/board_decisions/tentative_orders/2024/proposed-order-r3-2024-0045-m1w.pdf). In a coastal setting, the gating item can be less about building a new brine sink and more about proving that an existing one can legally absorb a changed waste stream.

Inland, the outlet problem gets physical

Move inland and the outlet problem becomes more physical. Rancho California Water District's small-scale indirect potable reuse work in Temecula is a useful search lead because project presentations identified reverse-osmosis concentrate disposal as the largest hurdle. The design response was not a simple pipe to the ocean. The project evaluated high-recovery reverse osmosis, brine ponds, and enhanced evaporation, with a design basis around 90% RO recovery and a local net evaporation rate of about 56 inches per year (https://watereuse.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/WRCA-IE-05-2022-Brine-Minimization-Maximizes-Potential-for-Inland-Potable-Reuse-Project.pdf). That is the inland residual-handling problem in miniature: higher recovery reduces pond area and waste volume, but the limiting resource can become land, evaporation physics, aerosol controls, salt solids, and local acceptance.

El Paso offers a different analogue. The Kay Bailey Hutchison Desalination Plant is not a municipal reuse plant, but it is one of the clearest inland examples of brackish water treatment being constrained by concentrate disposal. TWDB materials describe a plant with 27.5 MGD design capacity and a total project cost around $90 million, including roughly $19 million for concentrate disposal. The concentrate is transported about 22 miles to deep-well injection into suitable formations (https://www.twdb.texas.gov/innovativewater/desal/worthitssalt/doc/Worth_Its_Salt_Jan2014_KBH.pdf). The plant works because geology, pipeline rights, and injection infrastructure were available. That is a different control point from membrane recovery.

Not every delay is a salt-outlet delay

El Paso's Pure Water Center adds a second lesson. EPWater says the direct-to-distribution advanced purification facility is designed for up to 10 MGD, has completed design, has state regulator approval, and is under construction with opening planned in 2029 (https://www.epwater.org/our-water/water-resources/pure-water-center). Carollo's 2025 release puts the project cost at about $295 million, names Carollo as designer and a PCL/Sundt joint venture as constructor, and says the project received final TCEQ design approval in late 2024 (https://carollo.com/press-releases/pure-water-center-el-paso/). The most visible bottleneck there may have been regulatory validation, funding, and delivery timing, not the residual outlet. The brine-routing details remain less transparent in the accessible public records than the treatment train and approval pathway. That uncertainty is important: not every reuse delay is a salt-outlet delay.

Las Virgenes-Triunfo makes the residual pipe explicit in the procurement structure. The Pure Water Project in the Los Angeles area packages an advanced water purification facility with a reverse-osmosis concentrate pipeline. Walsh describes a roughly $303.7 million contract and a 14-mile ROC pipeline spanning Thousand Oaks, Westlake Village, and Agoura Hills (https://www.walshgroup.com/ourexperience/water/watertreatmentplants/purewaterprojectlasvirgenes.html). Federal grant material describes purified water going to Las Virgenes Reservoir and concentrate moving through pipelines to the Calleguas Salinity Management Pipeline for eventual ocean discharge (https://www.highergov.com/grant/R23AP00474/). Here the residual outlet is not an afterthought. It is built into the project delivery package.

Who controls the constraint

The control points in this market are therefore concrete: OC San's ocean outfall, SAWPA's Brine Line, Calleguas' salinity management infrastructure, TCEQ approvals, California regional water board discharge orders, injection formations near El Paso, evaporation acreage in inland basins, and the vendors that reduce residual volume enough to make those outlets financeable. The value chain includes membrane suppliers, high-recovery process vendors, pressure-exchanger suppliers, EPC contractors, public-agency capacity owners, pretreatment labs, SCADA and compliance software, haulers, and residual solids handlers.

The counterargument

Markets and policymakers can miss this because the constraint hides in low-glamour records: board packets, NPDES orders, CEQA appendices, capacity purchase memoranda, hydraulic models, pretreatment limits, and utility rate schedules. The visible narrative says drought or scarcity creates demand for reuse. The procurement record often says something narrower: the project advances when someone secures a salt outlet.

The strongest counterargument is that high recovery can relieve the very constraint described here. If a project already has an outfall or brine line, increasing recovery can shrink the reject stream and reduce transport, pond, or thermal-treatment cost. Coastal reuse projects may treat residual management as routine permitting rather than a binding constraint. Some vendor materials argue that 90%-plus recovery can reduce total cost by cutting brine volume and downstream disposal burden. That counterargument is real. It means the constraint is not "brine exists"; it is "brine has no cheap, permitted, scalable outlet."

What would prove this wrong, and what to watch

The watch list should follow documents rather than slogans: capacity purchases before construction awards, NPDES permit amendments for reverse-osmosis concentrate, dilution-factor disputes, brine-line master plans, TDS and metals discharge limits, treatment-and-disposal rate schedules, deep-injection permits, evaporation-pond land acquisition, crystallizer orders, landfill acceptance for salt solids, and the moment a project budget moves residual conveyance from a minor line item into the critical path.

High-recovery reuse can still be a powerful tool. The point is narrower and more industrial: in water reuse, the scarce asset may be the place where the salt goes. When that place is controlled by a public agency, an outfall permit, a geologic formation, or a contracted pipeline slot, the economics of reuse follow the outlet as much as the membrane.
