The hard part of resilient navigation is proving the whole system works

Critical infrastructure does not lack ideas for operating through a GPS disruption. Anti-jam receivers, authenticated signals, terrestrial timing, inertial sensors and alternative navigation systems all exist. The slower step is turning those components into a trusted operating capability: testing them against realistic interference, qualifying receivers, integrating them into platforms, clearing spectrum issues, obtaining certification and teaching operators what to do when navigation degrades.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-09-24 · 3 min read

In brief

A better sensor is not yet a deployable system

Positioning, navigation and timing underpin aircraft, military systems, telecom networks, energy systems and financial timing. The apparent solution to interference is often a device: buy a protected antenna, add an inertial sensor or use an alternative signal. For high-consequence users, the device is only the start.

A complete assured-PNT system needs a threat model, performance requirements, repeatable test methods, qualified hardware, evidence that sensors fail safely together, integration with the host platform, compatible spectrum, a procurement path and operating procedures for degraded conditions. The scarce resource is increasingly the evidence bundle that proves those pieces work as a system.

Aviation shows why integration takes time

Civil aviation is an instructive case because it combines safety certification, international standards and diverse fleets. EASA's 2026 update on GNSS interference reflects a real operating problem. Yet a resilient replacement or supplement has to move through standards, avionics integration, certification and fleet adoption before it changes the system's practical resilience.

Work on controlled-reception-pattern antennas and DME-related standards illustrates the same point. A technology can be technically attractive while the institutional path to broad acceptance remains measured in years. That delay is not necessarily failure; it is the price of proving that a high-consequence system behaves safely under conditions it was not originally designed to face.

The control point is interoperability

PNT resilience has a network quality. A receiver, antenna or timing source must interoperate with host platforms, spectrum rules, maintenance systems, test equipment and operational procedures. The IEEE conformity work, Galileo's OSNMA service and U.S. policy efforts are all signals that assurance is becoming more formalised. They are not substitutes for field evidence.

This also explains why procurement can lag a technical demonstration. A buyer responsible for aircraft, a grid, a port or a defense platform needs evidence appropriate to its own safety and mission context. The more critical the consequence of failure, the harder it is to rely on a laboratory claim alone.

Policy can accelerate, but it cannot skip validation

The U.S. path from Executive Order 13905 in 2020 to a proposed FAR implementation in 2026 shows both momentum and lead time. Government policy can assign responsibility, fund trials and create demand for resilient timing and navigation. It cannot collapse qualification, integration and operating-procedure work into a single purchase order.

M-code follows a similar logic. Its value depends on receiver, platform, key-management and mission-system integration, not only on the signal in space. The deployment unit is a working chain, not a component on a shelf.

What would show real progress

The useful signals are specific: published test methods and vectors; common receiver-conformity regimes; certified platform integrations; spectrum compatibility decisions; procurement language that specifies degraded-mode operation; and evidence from exercises where multiple sensors fail and recover together. Progress is also visible when operators can train and audit a procedure, rather than rely on an engineer's claim that a device should work.

Alternative PNT technologies still matter, and in some situations the underlying signal or hardware will remain the limiting factor. But across critical systems, resilient navigation is becoming an assurance-and-integration industry. The bottleneck is not another standalone sensor. It is trusted proof that the complete system will keep operating when GNSS does not.
