The Scarce Asset in Meat-AMR Surveillance Is Evidence, Not Genomes

Antimicrobial-resistance surveillance in meat looks like a sequencing story. Regulators collect samples, laboratories generate genomes, databases identify related isolates, and investigators search for a common source.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-01 · 15 min read

In brief

  • 17 samples per site per month define FDA’s 2025 retail-meat protocol.
  • August 23, 2026 starts the EU’s outbreak-associated WGS reporting rule.
  • 78% of FoodNet infections used CIDTs in 2024, weakening isolate availability.
  • Mid-2028 is the EU accreditation horizon for official WGS laboratories.

That description misses the difficult part. A genome is useful only when investigators can connect it to a viable isolate, a resistance phenotype, an accepted laboratory method, a correctly described sample, an exposure history, a product record, a facility and a time window.

The sharper thesis is sequential: food-safety systems encounter validation and attribution gates precisely because they have already routed around the earlier sequencing-capacity problem.

The United States illustrates the distinction. FDA’s GenomeTrakr network now includes 62 laboratories across 35 states, and FDA says the network has sequenced more than 1.8 million isolates. FSIS had already completed its transition from PFGE to whole-genome sequencing by April 2019. In a 2020 account, FSIS reported analysing more than 100,000 food and environmental samples and molecularly characterising more than 12,000 isolates annually. Those are dated figures, but they establish that federal sequencing was operating at meaningful scale years ago. (FSIS WGS programme)

Yet more genomes do not guarantee more attributable sources. FDA’s March 17, 2026 NARMS update integrated evidence from 261 ill people, animals at slaughter, ill animals and food products to identify increasing azithromycin-resistant Salmonella. The surveillance system detected and published a cross-source One Health signal. It did not link the U.S. illnesses to specific sources. (FDA NARMS interim update)

That gap—between detecting a pattern and proving what caused it—is where the operating constraint now sits.

The U.S. sampling pipe is narrower than the genomic database

FDA’s 2025 retail-meat protocol requires each participating site to buy 17 fresh samples per month: four retail-chicken samples, six ground-turkey samples, four ground-beef samples and one each of chicken liver, gizzard and heart.

The protocol also requires processing within 96 hours of purchase. It specifies 17 Salmonella tests, 11 Campylobacter tests, 17 E. coli tests and seven Enterococcus tests each month. Ground pork was excluded from the 2025 protocol. (FDA 2025 retail-meat protocol)

This is a controlled surveillance design, not a continuous national sensor network. It can produce representative information only when sampling sites buy the correct products, in the assigned locations and weeks, and laboratories process them within the prescribed window.

FDA tightened that design in 2021, replacing convenience sampling with a population-based, stratified double-clustering method. The redesign improved statistical representativeness, but it also made execution more dependent on specific clusters, store types, products and calendar periods. FDA’s methods document notes that some sites suspended testing for periods ranging from months to a year because public-health agencies faced competing priorities. (FDA NARMS methods)

The genomic estate is much larger than the monthly retail intake might suggest. NARMS reported 60,344 genomes with susceptibility data on November 24, 2021: 17,524 from retail meat, 25,864 from food-production animals, 12,179 from humans and 4,777 from veterinary diagnostic laboratories. Those figures are historical, not current database totals, but they show that the system already has enough data to support substantial cross-source comparison. (NARMS One Health surveillance)

The limiting question is no longer whether a comparison database exists. It is whether a relevant sample entered the system, produced a viable isolate and retained enough metadata to support the next investigative step.

Turkey and beef show where genomic evidence stops

Two FSIS cases provide the cleanest comparison.

During the 2018–2019 Salmonella Reading outbreak associated with turkey, related isolates appeared in homes, raw turkey pet food, products from 24 slaughter establishments, products from 14 processing establishments and 120 retail-turkey samples collected through NARMS.

WGS showed that the outbreak strain was distributed across the turkey system. Investigators did not identify one common supplier, product type, brand, manufacturing facility or production source. The genome revealed the extent of the reservoir without collapsing it into one attributable commercial source.

The 2019 Salmonella Dublin ground-beef investigation followed a different path. Investigators found six related raw-beef samples from slaughter and processing facilities. They also had leftover ground beef from a patient’s home and purchase-history evidence linking the product to an FSIS-regulated establishment. The combination supported a lot-level recall.

These cases also narrow the thesis. “Validation-evidence” is not one uniform shortage. Depending on the outbreak, the operative gate can be isolate availability, method validity, incomplete metadata, missing batch records, product commingling, diffuse pathogen ecology or legal authority.

Europe is formalising a chain that already partly works

Regulation (EU) 2025/179 will apply from August 23, 2026. It requires competent authorities to perform WGS and report results to the EFSA system for at least one isolate of specified pathogens associated, or suspected to be associated, with a foodborne outbreak.

The covered organisms include Salmonella enterica, Campylobacter jejuni, Campylobacter coli, Listeria monocytogenes and E. coli. Food businesses are not subject to a general requirement to operate their own sequencing programmes. They must cooperate with competent authorities and provide available isolates or WGS results when requested. (Regulation 2025/179)

Official laboratories must use methods covered by ISO/IEC 17025 quality controls. The Commission’s implementation material gives laboratories until mid-2028 to complete the relevant accreditation process. EFSA issued reporting guidelines in December 2025 covering sequence-quality acceptance, required metadata, confidentiality and institutional responsibilities. (EFSA reporting guidelines)

This regulatory sequence creates a visible implementation cycle: method validation, accreditation-scope extensions, proficiency testing, LIMS integration, data-quality controls and staff training.

But it does not start from zero. The EFSA–ECDC One Health WGS system has operated since July 2022 and already exchanges genomic profiles between food-safety and human-health platforms. European investigations have already converted genomic evidence into recalls and facility actions in some cases.

The strongest counterargument is therefore that Regulation 2025/179 may standardise an operating system rather than expose a major new capacity shortage. Member States may absorb the requirements through existing public laboratories, existing frameworks and already-developed data infrastructure. If that happens, accreditation will be a transitional compliance task rather than a durable bottleneck.

Clinical diagnostics can erase the human isolate

The human side of the chain faces a different problem. Clinical laboratories increasingly use culture-independent diagnostic tests because they are faster and easier than conventional culture.

FoodNet’s 2024 data show that 77.6% of covered bacterial infections were diagnosed using CIDTs, compared with 47.1% in the 2016–2018 comparison period. Forty-one percent were diagnosed using CIDTs without a positive culture.

When laboratories attempted culture after a positive CIDT, only 59.3% produced a positive result, down from 70.6% in the earlier comparison period. (CDC FoodNet preliminary data)

A positive diagnostic result can therefore enter the clinical record without producing the bacterial isolate required for conventional WGS, phenotypic susceptibility testing and outbreak comparison. Reflex culture restores part of the surveillance chain, but it adds labour, transport requirements and cost after the diagnostic decision has already been made.

Direct-from-sample metagenomics could eventually reduce this dependence. WHO has nevertheless identified unresolved issues involving sample handling, database quality, bioinformatics standardisation, coverage and assignment of resistance genes to their microbial hosts. WGS also detects known resistance mechanisms more readily than unknown ones and does not automatically replace phenotypic susceptibility testing. (WHO GLASS WGS report)

The near-term system therefore has to support two workflows at once: fast culture-independent diagnosis for patient care and isolate-preserving laboratory work for public-health surveillance.

Spending moves toward the workflow, but public institutions retain control

The obvious commercial exposure sits in sequencers, reagents and library-preparation kits. Mature networks, however, reduce the probability that new instruments capture most incremental spending.

The UK Health Security Agency awarded a £1 million genome-sequencing consumables call-off covering May 2025 through April 2026. That contract is not specific to meat AMR, but it demonstrates the recurring reagent expenditure created by an installed public-genomics base. (UKHSA consumables award)

Software and integration can create a different form of control. The UK Food Standards Agency awarded Oxford University Innovation a £450,000 contract for continued deployment and integration of the PATH-SAFE genomic data platform. The award rationale cited intellectual property, institutional trust and the interoperability risk of changing suppliers. (PATH-SAFE contract)

That is a more defensible source of switching cost than genome production alone. Historical data, accepted pipelines, schema mappings, user permissions and links to epidemiological systems accumulate inside the workflow. Replacing the interface can require revalidation, migration and retraining even when competing analytical software exists.

External laboratories may also capture outbreak-surge work. ECDC’s 2025 procurement notice contemplated variable-volume, high-priority WGS, long-read sequencing and contractor-arranged sample shipment across multiple jurisdictions. The notice demonstrates a potential operating model, not awarded revenue or proven utilisation. (ECDC sequencing-services notice)

Instrument vendors such as Illumina, Thermo Fisher, QIAGEN, Oxford Nanopore and PacBio have relevant technical exposure. Eurofins and SGS offer food and microbiology testing capabilities, while Neogen supplies upstream pathogen-screening tools. The available evidence does not show how much meat-associated AMR surveillance contributes to their revenue, backlog, pricing or margins.

Public institutions still control the consequential assets: statutory authority, official-laboratory designation, national data ownership, submission standards and the decision to recall or enforce. Private suppliers can monetise pieces of the chain without owning the chain.

The historical base rate points to standardisation, not permanent scarcity

The closest prior episode is the replacement of PFGE with WGS in foodborne surveillance.

That transition required new instruments, protocols, staff training, proficiency testing and shared databases. FSIS nevertheless completed its laboratory transition by April 2019, while NCBI, PulseNet and GenomeTrakr supplied a common comparison layer.

The precedent argues against treating every qualification challenge as a permanent shortage. Public-health systems can standardise a complex laboratory technology and move it into routine operation.

The present transition is harder in one important respect. PFGE-to-WGS primarily changed how laboratories compared isolates. Meat-associated AMR surveillance must also preserve isolates, retain phenotypic testing, harmonise food and animal metadata, connect clinical and regulatory systems and recover product-level traceback records.

The likely outcome is uneven relief. Sequencing and basic data exchange may continue to scale, while the dominant constraint shifts from one investigation to another. In one case it will be accreditation; in another, a missing patient isolate; in another, a retailer receipt or batch number; and in another, a pathogen reservoir distributed across an entire production system.

A competing U.S. regulatory catalyst has already weakened. FSIS withdrew its proposed 2024 raw-poultry Salmonella framework effective April 25, 2025. That proposal cannot support a current claim that enforceable poultry-product standards are already redirecting industry spending toward interventions and process control. (FSIS withdrawal)

The useful thesis is narrower

The evidence does not establish one system-wide bottleneck with a common queue, cost or relief date. No comparative dataset currently shows which step causes the longest delay across jurisdictions and commodities.

What the evidence does establish is a sequential constraint. Genomic detection has advanced faster than the institutional ability to turn every genomic relationship into an attributable source and defensible action.

That distinction matters for policymakers. Genome counts and network size measure surveillance capacity, but they do not measure closure. Better performance metrics would include isolate-recovery rates, metadata-rejection rates, time from cluster detection to source attribution, the share of investigations resolved to a facility or lot and the precision of resulting recalls.

It also matters commercially. Regulation and genomic growth may create spending without creating attractive private value capture. Accreditation work can be temporary. External sequencing can be low-volume and episodic. Logistics and reagents can be pass-through costs. Public laboratories may absorb most implementation work.

The scarce asset is not a sequencing machine. It is a repeatable institutional process that can prove what a sequence means, where it came from and what action it supports.

What to watch

  • August 23, 2026: Regulation 2025/179 begins applying unless the EU postpones or amends it.
  • December 31, 2026: an EU authority publishes its first post-application operating or accreditation procedure.
  • March 31, 2027: FDA publishes a NARMS 2026–2030 plan or a dated replacement schedule.
  • June 30, 2027: FoodNet reports whether CIDT use remains at or above 70%.
  • December 31, 2027: an EU buyer awards a Regulation-linked sequencing, validation or integration contract.
  • June 30, 2028: accreditation records show whether official laboratories cleared implementation without material backlogs.
