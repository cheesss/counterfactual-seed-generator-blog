The Shell Bottleneck Starts Before the Shell

Western rearmament still looks like a shell-body problem because steel is the visible object and shell-line announcements are easy to count. The harder constraint may sit earlier in the chain, inside the explosive-fill and propellant plants that turn steel into a complete round.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-21 · Updated 2026-06-28 · 10 min read

In brief

  • 100,000 rounds/month still depends on fill, propellant, primer, and fuze.
  • $435M restarts a U.S. TNT node after decades of no domestic production.
  • Mid-2026 was the Army's expected 100,000-round timing.
  • 36-48 months is the GAO schedule band for Kentucky TNT commissioning.
  • EUR 500M of ASAP money reaches explosives and powder, not just shell lines.

National Defense reported that the Army was still not expected to reach 100,000 155mm rounds per month until mid-2026. That makes the current window easier to frame: if the target is still tight while Graham remains inside the GAO's 36-to-48-month TNT commissioning band, imports and existing permitted nodes still matter to the system (National Defense on the Army's mid-2026 target timing; GAO TNT schedule and capacity).

The Army itself defines a complete 155mm shot as a system of fuze, primer, loaded projectile, and propelling charge. A steel casing without explosive fill, propellant, primer, and fuze does not solve an artillery shortage (Army 155mm overview).

That distinction matters because energetic materials do not scale like ordinary metal parts. TNT, nitrocellulose, RDX, IMX fills, propelling charges, acid recovery, wastewater treatment, hazardous-waste handling, explosives safety, air permits, and local approvals all sit upstream of the round. A country can fund more shells and still wait on nitration capacity.

The first tell is TNT

The clearest named case is the Army's attempt to rebuild a domestic TNT node. A GAO protest record says the 2023 sources-sought notice targeted a continental U.S. facility capable of 5 million to 15 million pounds of TNT per year, with a preferred construction-and-commissioning schedule of 36 months and a 48-month outside target after award (GAO TNT schedule and capacity).

That schedule explains why TNT is the clearest early warning. In December 2024, the Army awarded REPKON USA Defense a contract ceiling of $435 million to design, build, and commission a TNT production facility in Graham, Kentucky. The Army said the award would restore U.S. TNT production for the first time in decades and that the existing supply chain relied entirely on overseas sources (Army TNT contract).

That is the shape of the constraint. The bottleneck does not clear when a government signs a check. It clears when a chemical plant exists, passes its permitting path, reaches usable output, handles waste streams, and produces volume at military quality.

Defense News reported that a senior Army acquisition official identified enough explosives as the biggest concern for the 100,000-round-per-month 155mm target. That is a narrower claim than a per-shell arithmetic model, but it is enough to keep TNT at the center of the current ramp story (Defense News on the explosives shortfall).

The plant is also a permit stack

Holston Army Ammunition Plant shows the mechanism in operating form. BAE Systems runs the Tennessee site under a long-term Army contract with an $8.8 billion ceiling, and Holston modernization includes new nitration and weak acetic-acid recovery infrastructure backed by more than $1 billion of Army investment (Holston operating contract; Holston modernization).

The Holston IMX supplemental environmental assessment is effectively part of the plant diagram. It ties the project to urgent Defense Department demand, then walks through industrial wastewater, hazardous waste, air-quality effects, static detonation chambers, and a flashing furnace (Holston IMX environmental assessment).

Radford Army Ammunition Plant shows the propellant side of the same story. Army modernization materials describe nitrocellulose as critical to propellants and call out water infrastructure as a key need, while Joint Munitions Command materials describe roughly $715 million of environmental investment, open-burning reduction, and a planned energetic-waste incinerator (Army modernization overview; JMC Radford profile).

That is why "permit stack" is not a metaphor. A nitration line, acid recovery train, waste-treatment path, and compliant operating base can matter more than another press release about shell-body throughput.

Waste and qualification are the quiet gates

The least visible part of the chemical stack is the part most likely to bind. Energetic-materials plants do not just react chemicals; they must legally dispose of what the reaction leaves behind. Radford's environmental investment and Holston's IMX assessment both read like operating constraints, not side paperwork (JMC Radford profile; Holston IMX environmental assessment).

Commissioning is not the end of the clock. Greenfield capacity still has to clear safe startup, qualification, and military acceptance before it matters at the round level. That is why an existing operating base starts ahead of a greenfield project. Money can fund a line quickly; it cannot compress every environmental, safety, and acceptance step at once.

Europe's subsidy pattern points the same way. The EU's roughly EUR 500 million ASAP program sits inside about EUR 1.4 billion of total supply-chain investment, and the named grants reach chemical nodes: EUR 66.7 million for Chemring Nobel to more than double explosives production in Norway, and more than EUR 130 million for Rheinmetall projects spanning ammunition, powder, and explosives (European Commission on ASAP; Commission on Chemring Nobel; Rheinmetall on ASAP funding).

Public money is not flowing only to shell bodies. It is also chasing the harder layer of permitted chemical throughput.

Europe is running the same experiment

The European Union framed ASAP around bottlenecks, not around shell bodies alone. The Commission named explosives, propelling powder, shells, missiles, testing, and raw materials as target categories (European Commission on ASAP).

That framing shows up in the named cases. Chemring Nobel is expanding Norwegian explosives with EU backing. Eurenco is restoring large-caliber propellant-powder production at Bergerac. Rheinmetall's funded projects span ammunition, powder, and explosives across several countries (Commission on Chemring Nobel; Reuters on Eurenco Bergerac; Rheinmetall on ASAP funding).

Poland's Nitro-Chem shows the import-bridge version of the same problem. Reuters reported a 1.2 billion zloty agreement, roughly $310 million, to supply 18,000 tonnes of TNT to the U.S. market over 2027 to 2029, and described Nitro-Chem as Europe's largest TNT producer (Reuters on Nitro-Chem's U.S. TNT deal).

Sweden Ballistics shows the greenfield-permit version. Reuters reported that Sweden's Land and Environmental Court approved the Nora TNT project, which targets 4,500 tonnes per year by 2028. The court approval is the industrial event. Without it, the capacity remains theoretical (Reuters on the Nora TNT permit).

The real unit is the complete shot

The useful question is not how many shell bodies a country can cut, forge, or machine. The useful question is how many complete shots can move through explosives, propellant, fuzes, primers, load-assemble-pack, inspection, storage, and transport at the same time.

That creates a different map of exposed assets. A permitted TNT plant matters. A nitrocellulose line matters. Acid recovery matters. Hazardous-waste treatment matters. A GOCO operator with modernization experience matters. Existing permitted nodes also matter because they can ship before greenfield capacity clears environmental review, commissioning, and acceptance.

The migration is sequential, which is what makes it easy to miss. The first visible bottleneck is shell-body machining and load-assemble-pack capacity. Relieve that and the binding layer can move to bulk explosives and propellant. Relieve that and it can move again to permits, acid recovery, and waste commissioning. The last turns are often qualification, skilled operators, and durable orders.

An artillery surge is therefore not won when a new machining line is announced. It is won when the least visible chemical and permitting layer can keep pace with the metal one.

Who controls the constraint

In the United States, the energetics base is largely government-owned and contractor-operated, which concentrates influence in a short list of operators and owners. BAE Systems runs Holston under the $8.8 billion operating contract and is modernizing nitration and acid recovery there, while Army materials identify Radford's nitrocellulose and water systems as core inputs to propellant output (Holston operating contract; Holston modernization; Army modernization overview; JMC Radford profile).

REPKON USA Defense is building the missing domestic TNT node in Kentucky, but the GAO schedule still gives that project a multi-year commissioning window. Above the operators sit the Army organizations that own, fund, and contract for the plants. Beneath them sit the environmental and safety systems that the Holston and Radford records describe as integral to capacity, not as add-ons (Army TNT contract; GAO TNT schedule and capacity; Holston IMX environmental assessment; Army on Radford environmental progress).

Europe's map is wider and the ultimate beneficiary split is less settled, but the currently advantaged positions are still easy to recognize. Nitro-Chem is monetizing existing TNT capacity into U.S. supply. Eurenco is restoring Bergerac powder. Chemring Nobel is expanding explosives in Norway (Reuters on Nitro-Chem's U.S. TNT deal; Reuters on Eurenco Bergerac; Commission on Chemring Nobel).

Rheinmetall's funded projects span ammunition, powder, and explosives, and public reporting also points to nitrocellulose integration through Hagedorn-NC (Rheinmetall on ASAP funding; Financial Times on Hagedorn-NC).

That does not prove a single sole scarce position. It does support a narrower claim: the nodes with an operating plant, a live permit path, and a qualified production base have the clearest current advantage over everyone still trying to build one.

What would prove this wrong

The thesis is falsifiable. If complete-shot output rises in step with shell-body and load-assemble-pack capacity, then chemistry is only one constraint among several. Metal parts, fuzes, primers, charge modules, barrels, labor, transport rules, and demand durability can all bind first, and the Army's own plan treats 155mm as a full-system problem (Army 155mm overview).

The thesis holds only if shell bodies and load-assemble-pack lines expand faster than TNT, nitrocellulose, IMX, propellant, and waste-permitted throughput. It also weakens if allied imports stay abundant enough to bridge the gap, or if new projects such as Graham, Bergerac, or Nora come online on schedule and quickly move from construction to usable military output.

What to watch

  • Whether the Army actually reaches 100,000 rounds per month around the mid-2026 timing cited by National Defense.
  • REPKON's Graham, Kentucky TNT plant against the GAO's 36-to-48-month schedule band.
  • Holston modernization milestones for nitration, acid recovery, and IMX-related waste systems.
  • Radford milestones for nitrocellulose, water infrastructure, and energetic-waste handling.
  • Eurenco Bergerac's return to large-caliber propellant powder.
  • Whether import bridges such as Nitro-Chem's U.S. deal expand or shrink after 2027 while domestic projects ramp.
  • Court and permit events, because those are the moments when theoretical capacity becomes legal capacity.

Energetics may not remain the binding constraint. But if shell-body and load-assemble-pack lines keep moving faster than the chemical stack behind them, the decisive factory in this cycle will be the one that can legally and safely make the explosive material before the shell ever reaches assembly.
