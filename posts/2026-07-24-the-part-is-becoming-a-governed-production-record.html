The Part Is Becoming a Governed Production Record

Defense additive manufacturing is usually presented as a machine story. Put printers at depots or aboard ships, send a model, and manufacture the replacement close to the point of need.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-24 · 15 min read

In brief

  • 1,300 F-15 bumpers | DLA’s first competitive additive contract tests alternate sourcing.
  • 196 subplates | NAVSEA plans a class-level destroyer rollout in FY2027–FY2028.
  • 300+ packages | USS Bataan shows repository scale ahead of repeat procurement.
  • 3 flight-worthy parts | FRCE establishes early deployment, not routine provisioning.
  • 2 production sites | Same-part acceptance would mark the next commercialization state.

The programmes that have moved beyond demonstrations reveal a more consequential transition. A spare part is becoming a governed production record: legal authority to use and release the design, an authoritative geometric definition, material and process requirements, machine controls, inspection plans, acceptance criteria, configuration history and evidence from service.

A printer can reproduce geometry. It cannot decide whether the government has manufacturing rights, whether a worn sample is an authoritative reference, whether a machine change invalidates prior evidence, or whether the resulting part belongs on an aircraft or submarine.

This makes the current transition a disruption of the sustainment workflow rather than a simple substitution of one manufacturing technology for another. The sequence runs from an unavailable or uneconomic part to rights review, package creation, qualification, accepted production, installation, field evidence and another operational order. The load-bearing step is the closed procurement loop. Machine installations and digital libraries are enabling infrastructure, not proof that the loop works.

The commercialization state is therefore early deployment. Operational parts exist. Qualified depot capabilities exist. A major commercial competition exists. Repeat orders and portable approvals remain scarce.

The evidence has crossed into operational use

NAVAIR’s 2016 titanium link-and-fitting assembly for an MV-22 engine nacelle established the technical frontier. The safety-critical component flew on July 29, 2016 and remained aboard the test aircraft for continued evaluation. NAVAIR also warned that substantial work remained before safety-critical parts could be produced routinely in theater. (NAVAIR MV-22 demonstration)

Nine years later, Fleet Readiness Center East showed a more operational model. The Cherry Point depot qualified an aluminum metal-additive capability in under six months and delivered three flight-certified, non-flight-critical components during 2025: an AH-1Z weapons-pylon fitting, a V-22 landing-gear repair fitting and a C-130 blanking plate. The V-22 fitting addressed a component that maintainers had difficulty obtaining through the conventional supply system. (FRCE fleet deliveries)

The difference between those two cases is more important than the parts themselves. The 2016 flight proved that a printed metal component could survive a demanding environment. The 2025 deliveries showed that a depot could combine qualification, manufacturing, inspection and fleet acceptance into a functioning pathway.

The Air Force developed a parallel route at Tinker Air Force Base. Oklahoma City Air Logistics Complex installed and engine-tested a digitally engineered anti-ice gasket for the TF33 engine in 2020. By 2022, the complex reported two airworthy metal-additive engine-related parts flying and more than a dozen additional components in development. (TF33 gasket)

The associated Air Force–GE REACT II arrangement is especially instructive. The Air Force acquired technical-data rights, installed GE equipment at the depot and received training and process support. Production work moved inward, but the incumbent did not disappear. GE changed its position in the value chain from finished-part supplier toward rights, equipment, process knowledge and qualification support. (Air Force–GE REACT II)

Lower-consequence polymer applications have moved faster. Travis Air Force Base designed, secured authorization for and installed a reverse-engineered C-17 lavatory floor panel on November 29, 2023 after the original panel became unavailable for procurement. Boeing, the C-17 programme office, the Air Force additive-manufacturing office and Air Mobility Command all participated in the approval pathway. (C-17 floor panel)

These are operational deployments, but they do not yet demonstrate routine provisioning. Most public records end at first delivery or installation.

Figures and milestones come from official NAVAIR, Air Force, Army, DLA and NAVSEA records.

The CH-47 case deserves narrower treatment than it often receives. Army engineers began with a two-dimensional drawing, produced aluminum cargo-ramp links, tested them at Redstone and flew two on a Chinook without OEM involvement. But the Army described those aluminum links as proof-of-concept articles and identified titanium links as the intended permanent phase. (Army CH-47 links)

That distinction captures the larger state of the market. The technical and administrative pathway is real. The evidence of durable procurement is still incomplete.

The procurement loop remains open

The clearest attempt to turn an approved package into repeatable commercial production is DLA’s F-15 pylon-bumper contract.

The Air Force had already made the bumper at Tinker’s Reverse Engineering and Critical Tooling laboratory. It then supplied DLA with a technical-data package containing three-dimensional PDF drawings, print files and quality-assurance requirements. DLA competed a requirement for almost 1,300 parts and completed contracting in roughly six months. First articles were expected to be tested by the end of 2024. (DLA pylon-bumper contract)

That record establishes package release and commercial competition. It does not yet establish first-article passage, full-lot acceptance, operational field use or another order. The absence matters because those later events are where an innovation project becomes a supply mechanism.

The Navy’s helicopter-hangar-door valve subplates offer the strongest class-replication pathway. The subplates were installed on three Flight IIA destroyers. NAVSEA reported that the additive route reduced the approximate cost from $100,000 to $10,000 per valve and from $600,000 to $60,000 for a six-valve ship set. Ship-change documents were submitted to support 196 subplates during FY2027 and FY2028. (NAVSEA DDG subplates)

The 196 units are planned, not installed. The decisive threshold is completed installation and satisfactory field performance, not the announced quantity.

A third case demonstrates assembly disaggregation. Southeast Regional Maintenance Center reverse-engineered a chilled-water-pump cooling-fan rotor that could not be purchased separately. The conventional remedy was a complete motor quoted at $316,544.16. The depot printed four design iterations, produced a final aerospace-grade polymer rotor for a reported $131.21, tested it, certified it internally and installed it aboard a destroyer. (NAVSEA cooling rotor)

The comparison shows the strategic value of controlling a subcomponent definition. It is not a complete unit-economic calculation. The reported rotor cost excludes engineering labour, equipment depreciation, inspection, failed builds and lifecycle replacement costs.

Who controls the transition

The valuable asset is the authority to move a part through the entire chain.

Programme offices and contracting officers determine whether the government possesses usable manufacturing and release rights. Configuration authorities decide which geometric and process definition is current. Laboratories and delegated engineering authorities create admissible qualification evidence. DLA and the services decide whether an approved package becomes a funded procurement. Maintainers provide the field record that should update the package after installation.

MIL-STD-31000 formalizes the technical-data package as the authoritative item definition. A 2025 data-item description, DI-SESS-82465, specifies the manufacturing-process information required when additive manufacturing must be controlled within a product-level Type 3D package. These standards make the production record more explicit, but they do not make approval automatically portable across machines, materials or sites. (DI-SESS-82465)

Data rights remain a structural dividing line. GAO found that technical-data shortfalls contributed to vendor dependence across major weapons programmes. Operation-and-maintenance rights do not automatically include detailed manufacturing or process information, and programmes have limited remedies after entering sustainment. (GAO data-rights review)

Repository control is also becoming operational. NAVSEA’s N-RAM centralizes policy, specifications and technical guidance and maintains a tracker refreshed nightly across roughly 140 candidate parts moving through requirements, contracting, modelling, printing and approval. USS Bataan had access to more than 300 approved packages as early as 2022.

A mature system would go further. It would attach rights status, approved machines and producers, production lots, installed serials, inspections, removals, failures and revisions to each package. The organization controlling that authoritative record could govern the workflow even without owning the printer or original design.

This is where public-company exposure becomes more nuanced. GE Aerospace can earn from rights, equipment, training and process support. Huntington Ingalls can retain naval integration and qualification work while adopting AM internally. Boeing can remain inside the design-authority loop. 3D Systems and Stratasys have equipment exposure, but machine revenue does not prove recurring part demand. The strongest position may belong to whichever actor controls approval, configuration and lifecycle evidence.

The historical base rate argues for gradual change

The closest prior episode is the adoption of CNC manufacturing and digital technical data across defense production.

Digital geometry and numerical control eventually became standard. They improved reproducibility, reduced dependence on physical tooling and made production definitions easier to transmit. They did not eliminate configuration control, supplier approval, specialised processes or incumbent integration authority.

Additive manufacturing follows the same direction but carries an additional complication: material properties depend heavily on the machine, feedstock, build parameters and post-processing route. A geometry file that moves cleanly between systems does not imply an equivalent finished part.

Commercial aerospace provides a second reference. Additive fuel nozzles reached recurring safety-critical production only after extensive material, process and component qualification. That precedent proves serial AM production is possible. It is a poor direct economic analogue for obsolete sustainment parts, where demand may be tiny, irregular or disappear before qualification cost can be recovered.

The base rate therefore supports a staged transition. Tooling, training aids, polymer interiors and low-risk ship components move first. Higher-consequence metal parts follow through tightly controlled programmes. Durable portability arrives last, if it arrives at all.

Three paths could emerge by 2030

The first path is controlled expansion through low- and medium-consequence parts. DLA accepts the pylon-bumper lot, FRCE delivers additional components, and NAVSEA installs a material share of the 196 subplates. Packages multiply because each new approval reuses more of the preceding process and documentation.

The second path is cross-site assurance. Standards, machine equivalence and production monitoring allow the same part configuration to be accepted from two geographically separate sites. That event would matter more than another printer installation because it would show that qualification evidence can travel with the production record.

The third path is incumbent-led licensing. More programmes resemble the Air Force–GE arrangement: the government gains organic or regional production, while the OEM retains process support, proprietary knowledge or design authority. Adoption proceeds, but value shifts within the incumbent relationship rather than away from it.

A fourth possibility competes with all three. Printed molds, cores and tooling may generate more accepted sustainment output than directly printed critical parts. That route captures digital setup advantages while preserving familiar casting and machining qualification. It would expand additive workflows without creating a large market for direct-printed operational components.

The strongest counterargument is economic, not technical

The central thesis survives, but in a narrower form.

Qualification can dominate part economics. NIST has described historical programmes requiring thousands of tests, millions of dollars and five to fifteen years. Those figures do not apply uniformly to every sustainment part, but they show why an inexpensive build can remain an expensive approved product. (NIST qualification programme)

Demand may also vanish. A platform can retire, a supplier can restore production, a conventional shop can quote a better lot, or an approved repair can remove the need for replacement. A package with one successful installation may never receive a second order because the underlying requirement was genuinely one-off.

The public evidence is also biased toward firsts. Programme offices, depots and equipment vendors benefit from announcing first flights, first installations and lead-time reductions. Those milestones justify programmes and budgets. Reorders, scrap, maintenance burdens and unsuccessful qualifications receive less attention.

Incumbents have their own incentives. They may overstate the danger of alternate production when it protects proprietary data and sole-source revenue. They can also embrace the transition when licensing, equipment, qualification or integration preserves their economics.

The result is selective redistribution of manufacturing authority, not broad supplier displacement. The thesis fails if repositories grow while consequential parts remain OEM-dependent, site-specific and uneconomic to reproduce.

The next state transition has a date

The sector should remain classified as early deployment through roughly 2028–2030 unless the evidence changes.

Movement to repeatable procurement requires at least one consequential part to complete the full chain: a releasable authoritative package, accepted production by an alternate source, satisfactory field service and a second operational order. Acceptance of the same configuration from two separate production sites would provide stronger evidence that the package is portable rather than merely digital.

The pylon bumper is the nearest commercial settlement event. The DDG subplates are the nearest replication test. A same-part, two-site approval is the system-level threshold.

Until those events occur, package counts, equipment installations and successful first articles should be treated as evidence of capability formation rather than scaled operation.
