In cell and gene therapy, scale creates a custody-of-proof problem

Most discussion of cell and gene therapy scale starts with the factory. People watch vector yield, cleanroom time, release testing, treatment-center readiness, and CDMO slots. Those limits matter. The public review record shows a second gate that often appears after a sponsor solves part of the physical problem.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-14 · 13 min read

In brief

  • >2,600 active OTP products in December 2024 show how much evidence can pile up.
  • 249 days was CASGEVY's FDA clock, yet the bridge stayed case-specific.
  • 4 KRESLADI CMC deficiencies triggered a complete response and commitments through 2028.
  • 25% was one NIST study's upper bound for candidate LVV material variation.
  • June 2026 draft relief still leaves product-specific proof with the license holder.

Scale creates the gate

That gate is custody of proof. When a sponsor adds a site, changes scale, swaps an input, or rewrites an assay, it has to show that older nonclinical and clinical evidence still speaks for the new version of the product. FDA's May 2026 CMC-flexibility guidance is already final and gives sponsors more room on validation and specifications, but the agency's June 2026 prior-knowledge guidance remains draft and still requires product-specific applicability, verification where needed, and permission to use third-party information (FDA CMC-flexibility guidance) (FDA prior-knowledge draft).

That distinction matters because the license holder still owns the regulatory conclusion. A sponsor can reuse internal, public, consortium, or some third-party knowledge only if it can show that the evidence fits the product under review, and 21 CFR 601.2 limits how freely a biologics license application can rely on manufacturing information held in someone else's master file (21 CFR 601.2). The issue is not whether the industry has enough data. The issue is whether the sponsor can measure the relevant delta, trace it to source, and legally carry the proof into the file.

The cases show several versions of the same bridge

The named approvals do not point to one uniform hurdle. They show several recurring patterns: a clean bridge, a bypass, a remediation loop, a facility handoff, a clinical-surrogate failure, and a commercial handoff after approval.

The cycle time is uneven, and the tail runs past approval

The review clocks matter because they show where this gate bites. CASGEVY moved from submission to approval in 249 calendar days, and KEBILIDI in 243. Those figures prove that the existence of a bridge does not by itself create a long visible delay. KRESLADI shows the other end of the range: 968 days from initial filing to approval, 650 days from complete response to approval, and 181 days from resubmission to approval. BREYANZI's public chronology adds another pattern, with a three-month extension tied to a major manufacturing and facility amendment (CASGEVY FDA review) (KEBILIDI FDA review) (KRESLADI FDA review) (BREYANZI FDA review).

The public record does not let you assign every one of those days to comparability alone. The stronger reading is narrower. This gate can stay small when a sponsor can bound the change quickly, and it can stretch across cycles when assay validity, stability evidence, sampling, sterility, or facility readiness remain unsettled.

The horizon also runs beyond approval. KRESLADI's longest cited commitment extends through December 2028. KEBILIDI's next specification check depends on reaching ten commercial lots, which makes relief volume-dependent rather than calendar-driven. The June 2026 prior-knowledge guidance is still draft. Those three facts support a restrained conclusion: evidence continuity remains a recurring sequential gate through at least 2028, even if it never becomes the dominant delay across the whole sector (KRESLADI FDA review) (KEBILIDI FDA review) (FDA prior-knowledge draft).

Measurement still lacks enough shared anchors

The deepest recurring constraint in this record is measurement assurance tied to provenance. A sponsor needs a defined measurand, a method that can resolve the decision-relevant difference, reference materials that stay traceable over time, and transfer performance that survives across labs and sites. That is a long way from a generic document-management problem.

The standards base is moving, but it is not settled. FDA's recognized regenerative-medicine standards list reached 37 through 2025, with 14 additions in 2023, 9 in 2024, and 14 in 2025. That count spans heterogeneous transport, analytical, tissue, and data standards. It does not amount to a universal kit for product-specific potency, capsid, cargo, or cell-source questions (FDA recognized standards list).

The same caution applies to the headline variability numbers. In one 2026 NIST-led interlaboratory effort, vial-to-vial variation ran about 0.1% to 5% for the candidate AAV material and 2% to 25% for the candidate LVV material. That 25% figure does not describe the whole industry. It does show why shared anchors are still hard to treat as routine. ATCC's public VR-1616 page adds a more concrete sign of scarcity by limiting that specific rAAV2 reference material to three vials per customer per year (NIST interlaboratory work) (ATCC VR-1616).

The control points sit outside any single sponsor

The sponsor holds the filing, but it does not hold every useful evidence object. CDMOs and technology providers hold transfer history, facility experience, and some permissioned third-party information. Analytical laboratories hold validated methods and method-transfer know-how. Reference-material providers and standards bodies shape the shared measurement vocabulary. Software vendors try to organize the lineage from batch genealogy to change control and regulatory commitments.

If this gate creates durable rent, the capture is likely to come from switching costs and recurring validation work rather than from a single public utility. Charles River, Thermo Fisher, Danaher's Aldevron, BIOVIA, Veeva, and IDBS each touch a different part of the route.

Public filings do not disclose comparability-specific backlog, pricing, margin, or revenue for any of them. BIOVIA and Alchemi publish large workflow claims, but those remain vendor claims. The Brammer Bio, Aldevron, and Cognate transactions show that buyers paid for bundled capacity, people, materials, and operating history, not for a separately priced comparability asset (Charles River CGT services) (Thermo Fisher Brammer Bio) (Danaher Aldevron) (Cognate acquisition) (BIOVIA Discoverant) (IDBS and Alchemi) (Veeva QMS).

The evidence supports a narrower architecture than the original "cross-sponsor graph" idea. The likely destination is a sponsor-controlled evidence core with selective, permissioned references around its edge. That is enough to matter operationally. It is not the same thing as a broad, open network that lets one company's regulatory proof flow freely into another company's biologics license application.

The closest base rate is Q5E, with weaker tools this time

The closest precedent is ICH Q5E. That framework matured in the mid-2000s as protein analytics became strong enough for many manufacturing changes to clear without new clinical trials. The similarity to today's problem is direct: regulators still need to know whether historical clinical evidence remains interpretable after a process change (ICH Q5E).

The difference is the condition of the toolkit. Cell and gene therapy programs carry living cells, complex vectors, smaller datasets, and less settled shared standards. The vaccine-platform experience during 2020 and 2021 is a useful partial analogue because it showed how prior platform knowledge can shorten development and change evaluation. It does not erase the need for product-specific proof in edited cells, AAV, or lentiviral programs (FDA platform experience in vaccines).

That is why this bottleneck looks sequential rather than universal. Solving the visible factory problem often creates the comparability problem. Then the sponsor either clears it, routes around it, or hands the issue to another gate such as clinical validity, treatment-center readiness, reimbursement, or demand.

The counterargument is strong, and it narrows the thesis

The public record does not support treating comparability as the master explanation for delay or weak commercialization. Relief is already operating through final FDA CMC-flexibility guidance. KEBILIDI shows that a sponsor can bypass a failed historical bridge by grounding pivotal evidence in current-process material. HEMGENIX and LYFGENIA show that ordinary dossiers can clear difficult bridges without any visible graph architecture. ELEVIDYS shows that the hard problem can sit in clinical validity, not manufacturing continuity (FDA CMC-flexibility guidance) (KEBILIDI FDA review) (HEMGENIX EMA review) (LYFGENIA FDA review) (ELEVIDYS FDA review).

That counterargument does not kill the thesis. It narrows it. The supported claim is that evidence continuity is a recurrent sequential regulatory gate. The unsupported claim is that it is the dominant sector-wide bottleneck, that every historical bridge must be solved, or that a machine-readable graph is required for regulatory acceptance.

The clean falsifier is also narrow. If a coded public sample of at least ten material site, scale, process, input, or analytical-method changes shows no major amendment, complete response, restricted pooling, added clinical work, or explicit comparability-related delay, then this thesis weakens sharply. It also weakens if shared methods and reference materials become broadly available and reproducible enough to sit well below the product differences that matter in review.

What the record still does not show

The public record leaves four important unknowns open. It does not quantify how often this gate decides the outcome across the full FDA and EMA denominator. It does not measure how much time or money accepted prior knowledge actually saves after controlling for modality and change complexity. It does not show who captures the economics, because comparability-specific pricing, backlog, and margin data were not found. It also does not put a dated relief schedule on standards supply, interlaboratory convergence, or authorized third-party evidence reuse.

Those gaps matter because they keep the thesis in its proper lane. The evidence supports saying that solving physical scale often creates an evidence-continuity problem. It does not show that the sector has already converged on a winning infrastructure model or that any one listed company has demonstrated standalone rent from that problem.

What to watch

  • FDA's final action on the June 2026 prior-knowledge draft, especially whether it narrows cross-program reuse.
  • Any FDA or EMA decision across AAV, LVV, or edited cells that accepts cross-program or authorized third-party evidence without a major amendment or restricted pooling.
  • NIST or ATCC releases that expand reference-material supply or lift the VR-1616 three-vial allocation limit.
  • KRESLADI postmarketing updates, especially the December 2028 acceptance-criterion milestone.
  • A coded public sample of at least ten material CGT manufacturing changes that shows where review time actually goes.
