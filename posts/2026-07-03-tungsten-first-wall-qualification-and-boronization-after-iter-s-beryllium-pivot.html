Tungsten first-wall qualification and boronization after ITER's beryllium pivot

The visible story around ITER’s new baseline is schedule: more commissioning, a later deuterium-tritium phase, a larger electron-cyclotron heating system, and a change in the first-wall armor from beryllium to tungsten. The less visible story is industrial qualification.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-03 · 12 min read

The visible story around ITER’s new baseline is schedule: more commissioning, a later deuterium-tritium

The visible story around ITER’s new baseline is schedule: more commissioning, a later deuterium-tritium phase, a larger electron-cyclotron heating system, and a change in the first-wall armor from beryllium to tungsten. The less visible story is industrial qualification. A fusion power plant does not buy “tungsten” the way a machine shop buys metal stock. It buys a tested, traceable, cooled, replaceable plasma-facing system that has survived thermal cycling, neutron exposure, defect screening, remote-maintenance assumptions, wall conditioning chemistry, and regulator-facing residual-handling rules.

That distinction matters because ITER’s beryllium-to-tungsten pivot exposes a procurement problem that commercial fusion projects may inherit even if their machines are smaller, faster, or privately financed. Tungsten is not rare in the abstract. Qualified tungsten first-wall evidence is scarce.

ITER’s blanket page gives the industrial scale

ITER’s blanket page gives the industrial scale. The machine’s blanket covers about 600 square metres of inner wall surface, is actively cooled, and is designed to remove up to 736 MW of thermal power. ITER says the 2023 decision to replace beryllium first-wall armour with tungsten reflects the direction expected for future burning-plasma devices, reduces beryllium-related toxicity, dust, melting, tritium-retention, and radwaste complications, and avoids the later need to replace the whole first wall. The same change creates new work: tungsten panels must be developed, qualified, procured, installed, supported by additional electron-cyclotron heating, and protected by boronization so oxygen does not poison the plasma-facing surface. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

That is the reversal. Tungsten is the future-facing choice, but choosing it earlier pulls future-facing qualification bottlenecks into the present.

The panel supply chain already shows why the issue is not simply commodity metal. ITER’s first wall is divided into 440 panels. Europe’s share has been normal-heat-flux panels; China and Russia hold the enhanced-heat-flux share. Fusion for Energy’s original European production note described 215 European panels, with China and Russia supplying the rest. Its 2025 update described Europe as responsible for 212 panels plus spares and named the two European production consortia: Alsymex and Fusion Business Leadership, the latter joining Leading and Empresarios Agrupados. Each factory was moving through pre-series acceptance tests covering pressure, heat, dimensional checks, joints, and leaks; Fusion for Energy also singled out tungsten tile procurement as critical. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1} :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

The enhanced-heat-flux side is harder

The enhanced-heat-flux side is harder. ITER’s Russia update says China and Russia share 225 enhanced-heat-flux panels exposed to 4.7 MW/m², while Europe’s normal-heat-flux panels face up to 2 MW/m². The same update says Russia had passed the full-scale beryllium prototype sequence but tungsten qualification had restarted and boron-carbide coating was under assessment. China’s earlier beryllium prototype work had already reached 7,500 cycles at 4.7 MW/m² and 1,500 cycles at 5.9 MW/m²; recent tungsten first-wall R&D papers report acceptable W mockups and full-size fingers at 15,000 cycles, while noting that the 30,000-cycle full-life heat-sink target remains a challenge. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3} :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4} :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

Those numbers are the anatomy of a hidden gate. A commercial fusion plant can announce a first-wall concept in a slide deck, but procurement begins only when someone can make repeatable joints, cycle them under representative heat loads, inspect them without ambiguity, and explain how failed or activated units come out of the vessel. The difficult object is not a tungsten tile. It is a tungsten-armoured heat sink and structural assembly that carries a document trail.

The test infrastructure looks like a bottleneck in its own right. Fusion for Energy says high-heat-flux testing is essential because plasma-facing surfaces may experience several MW/m², and only a few facilities worldwide can perform these tests. HELCZA in Plzeň tested three full-scale first-wall prototypes and two partial-scale units from 2021 to 2024, with each prototype running 9,000 cycles at 2 MW/m². ITER’s earlier HELCZA note described normal panels at 2 MW/m², enhanced panels at 4.7 MW/m², and an 800 kW electron-beam facility configured for plasma-facing component tests. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6} :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

If a private fusion company wants to convince a regulator, insurer, utility partner, or power-plant

This turns the first wall into a capacity-rationed qualification problem. If a private fusion company wants to convince a regulator, insurer, utility partner, or power-plant customer that its wall can survive a duty cycle, the limiting asset may be neither CAD nor capital. It may be the test slot, the accepted test protocol, the irradiation history, the non-destructive examination procedure, and the supplier whose process is already inside the evidence chain.

ITER’s own new-baseline reports reinforce that point. The baseline technical report frames the tungsten move as risk-reducing for tritium retention, dust, and disruption-load issues, but risk-increasing for core radiation and radiative collapse. The mitigations are not just “better tungsten.” They include a modified heating and current-drive mix and boronization. The integrated research plan points to a stepwise machine configuration: start-of-research operation with an inertially cooled tungsten first wall, 40 MW of ECH, 10 MW of ICH, and partial glow-discharge cleaning electrodes; later deuterium-tritium operation uses water-cooled tungsten plasma-facing components assessed under Q≥10-like fluxes and substantially higher auxiliary heating. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8} :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

Boronization is a second hidden system. ITER’s boronization system is not a small lab procedure scaled by optimism. ITER describes a 10–100 nm boron layer, 5% diborane diluted in carrier gas, a secured gas cabin outside the Diagnostics Building, 21 gas injection points, more than 1 km of injection lines in the Tokamak Building, roughly 400 metres of in-vessel piping, and four added anodes. The same article stresses that the technology is proven in tokamaks but has never been used at ITER scale in a tritiated environment. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

The physics need is clear

The physics need is clear. Without beryllium, the baseline report says ITER needs boronization to control oxygen. ASDEX Upgrade’s full-tungsten operation without boronization had restricted H-mode space; boronization removed oxygen for hundreds of discharges. WEST saw reduced oxygen, improved startup, and its first L-H transition after fresh boronization. ITER’s 2026 EAST experiment executed six boronizations and more than 400 tokamak pulses to test tungsten-wall conditioning. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11} :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

This has commercial implications because boronization is not only a startup aid. In a power-plant setting, it becomes a recurring residual-handling question: toxic and explosive precursor gas, deposition uniformity, tritium retention, vessel access, cleaning cadence, and possibly isotope-removal cycles. ITER’s baseline report warns that regular boronization every two weeks would increase tritium retention and that ion-cyclotron wall conditioning may be needed to remove retained tritium. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

The third adjacent system is microwave heating. ITER’s 2026 gyrotron update says the new baseline requires 48 gyrotrons at the start of operations and another 24 for the first deuterium-tritium phase, with additional units from Japan and Russia expected by 2032. The original procurement structure was smaller: Europe six, Japan eight, Russia eight, India two, and the United States transmission lines. Fusion for Energy separately described the original 24 gyrotrons as 1 MW units and framed European manufacturing as a revival of industrial know-how, skills, and facilities. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14} :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}

The commercial transfer is not one-for-one

The commercial transfer is not one-for-one. ITER uses tungsten on copper-alloy heat sinks and steel support structures under a research-machine duty cycle. A power plant may require tungsten on reduced-activation ferritic-martensitic steel, advanced alloys, or sacrificial replaceable sections, and it may experience neutron damage far above ITER’s first-wall exposure. UKAEA material strategy documents discuss DEMO blanket stages around 20 dpa and possibly 50 dpa. A 2025 tungsten-fleet study says ITER first-wall tungsten faces roughly 3 dpa while DEMO and FPP components may face 10–100 times higher damage; it also says operational fusion-neutron test facilities at fusion energies and fluxes are not yet available. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16} :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}

That weakens simplistic claims that ITER qualification “solves” commercial first-wall procurement. The more accurate statement is narrower and more interesting: ITER may define the qualification grammar. It can create accepted testing rituals, supplier learning curves, interface tolerances, wall-conditioning practices, gyrotron scale-up paths, and radwaste-handling assumptions. Commercial plants may then rewrite the materials stack while reusing the grammar.

STEP makes that link visible. UKAEA and Oxford Sigma have announced collaboration on novel tungsten plasma-facing wall designs for fusion power stations, including sacrificial sections and manufacturing pathways that try to avoid difficult tungsten-to-steel/copper joins. Oxford Sigma also says it is leading ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code work around fusion construction and material qualification. STEP’s own public materials describe a prototype power plant at West Burton targeted by 2040 and ongoing supplier engagement. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18} :contentReference[oaicite:19]{index=19}

Commonwealth Fusion Systems is less explicit publicly on first-wall procurement

Commonwealth Fusion Systems is less explicit publicly on first-wall procurement. Its technology page focuses on SPARC, net-energy milestones, and ARC as a commercial plant concept, while first-wall materials details remain comparatively undisclosed. That absence is itself useful. If a privately financed tokamak can raise capital, build magnets, and pour concrete before publishing the full first-wall procurement chain, then public markets may underweight the later qualification gate until procurement language becomes visible. :contentReference[oaicite:20]{index=20}

The tungsten commodity layer still matters, but probably later and differently. A fleet-scale study estimates wide tungsten demand ranges: ARIES-ST consumes 4,231 tonnes at 500 MWth and 29,034 tonnes at 2,000 MWth over 40 full-power years; EU-DEMO1 consumes 3,945 tonnes at 500 MWth and 9,554 tonnes at 2,000 MWth. USGS 2026 data put 2025 world mine production at about 85,000 tonnes of tungsten content, with China at 67,000 tonnes and no U.S. mine production. :contentReference[oaicite:21]{index=21} :contentReference[oaicite:22]{index=22}

Those figures do not imply an immediate tungsten shortage from fusion. They show why the bottleneck migrates. In the near term, the scarce item is qualified first-wall evidence. If dozens of power plants actually move from prototype to fleet procurement, the bottleneck may migrate toward tungsten mining, powder metallurgy, heavy-alloy processing, recycling, export controls, and non-China conversion capacity. A market that watches only ore supply can miss the nearer gate; a market that watches only prototype plasma performance can miss the industrial document trail.

Three scenarios deserve attention

Three scenarios deserve attention.

First, ITER’s qualification stack may become the template for power-plant procurement. In that case, suppliers that can deliver repeatable tungsten armour, heat sinks, non-destructive examination, high-heat-flux test records, irradiation records, and remote-maintenance assumptions gain leverage before large plant fleets exist. The signal would be STEP, ARC-like concepts, DTT, EU-DEMO, or other programs writing ITER-like cycle tests and named test facilities into procurement packages.

Second, the wall-conditioning and heating systems may capture more value than the metal. ITER’s move from the original 24-gyrotron structure toward 48 plus 24, combined with the large boronization plant, suggests that tungsten operation shifts spending toward RF systems, gas handling, diagnostics, cleaning, and operations. The signal would be power-plant designs specifying high-frequency, megawatt-class gyrotron supply chains and recurring wall-conditioning hardware as part of first-wall readiness, not as auxiliary lab equipment.

Third, commercial designs may diverge so far from ITER that the ITER evidence chain becomes a historical

Third, commercial designs may diverge so far from ITER that the ITER evidence chain becomes a historical analogy rather than a procurement bridge. Thin tungsten armour, sacrificial sections, liquid metals, advanced steels, stellarator duty cycles, or lower wall-load operating strategies could reduce the direct relevance of ITER’s tungsten-copper qualification path. The signal would be public qualification data showing non-ITER first-wall architectures surviving representative thermal and neutron loads without ITER-like boronization and microwave-heating escalation.

The strongest falsifier is straightforward: a credible fusion power plant reaches regulator-facing procurement with an independently qualified, non-tungsten or radically low-tungsten plasma-facing system, no ITER-style boronization dependency, no high-heat-flux test-slot bottleneck, and no expanded gyrotron dependency. Short of that, ITER’s pivot should be read less as a materials substitution and more as an industrial rehearsal. The first wall is becoming a proof package.
