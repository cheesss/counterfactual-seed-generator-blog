Fusion’s first wall is becoming an evidence business

The most visible changes to ITER’s programme are its later operating milestones and longer commissioning sequence. The more consequential industrial change may be the 2023 decision to replace beryllium armour with tungsten across the first wall.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-03 · 15 min read

In brief

  • 600 m²: ITER’s tungsten wall turns a material decision into a plant-level qualification programme.
  • 15,000 cycles: reported tungsten evidence remains below the 30,000-cycle heat-sink target.
  • 72 gyrotrons: ITER’s revised baseline expands RF demand through the first deuterium-tritium phase.
  • 2031: a commercial procurement package would test whether ITER’s qualification logic transfers.

ITER’s blanket covers roughly 600 square metres and must remove as much as 736 MW of thermal power. Changing its armour material therefore affects far more than metal procurement. ITER must develop and qualify new panels, control tungsten impurities in the plasma, expand electron-cyclotron heating, install a large boronization system and preserve a credible maintenance and waste pathway. (ITER blanket)

The resulting constraint is best understood as sequential. Magnets, licensing, plasma performance, tritium systems, financing and construction may bind first. Projects that clear those gates must still convert a wall concept into a traceable, cooled, inspectable and replaceable component whose manufacturing and operating evidence can withstand customer and regulatory scrutiny.

That is narrower than claiming that tungsten is fusion’s dominant bottleneck. It is also more defensible. The scarce object today is an accepted lifecycle evidence package for an integrated plasma-facing system.

The qualified object is an assembly and its document trail

A first-wall supplier does not deliver generic tungsten stock. It delivers armour joined to a heat sink and structural support, with cooling passages, dimensional tolerances, leak integrity, inspected joints and a controlled manufacturing history.

Europe’s current ITER responsibility is 212 panels plus spares. Fusion for Energy has established two production routes: Alsymex in Tarbes and Fusion Business Leadership, formed by Leading and Empresarios Agrupados, in Cantabria. Both factories have moved initial structures through pressure, thermal, dimensional, joint and leak testing. Tungsten-armour procurement and joining remain critical subsequent steps. (European panel manufacturing)

An older allocation assigned 215 normal-heat-flux panels to Europe and 225 enhanced-heat-flux panels jointly to China and Russia. The updated European figure and the older international allocation have not been publicly reconciled into a clean current total. The 215-panel figure describes the earlier beryllium-route allocation; 212 plus spares is the current European disclosure.

The thermal requirements separate the easier and harder branches. European normal-heat-flux panels are designed for loads up to 2 MW per square metre. Enhanced-heat-flux panels assigned to the Chinese and Russian programmes face 4.7 MW per square metre.

China’s earlier prototype programme completed 7,500 cycles at 4.7 MW per square metre and 1,500 overdesign cycles at 5.9 MW per square metre. Those tests used beryllium-to-copper components, so they demonstrate facility, joining and thermal-fatigue capability rather than completed tungsten qualification.

The stronger tungsten-specific evidence comes from a 2025 IAEA Fusion Energy Conference paper. Its authors reported acceptable tungsten mock-ups and full-size fingers after 15,000 cycles at 4.7 MW per square metre, while describing a 30,000-cycle full-operation-life heat-sink target as unresolved. (IAEA-FEC tungsten programme)

The distinction matters. A successful material coupon does not establish full-panel yield. A successful prototype does not automatically establish series-production repeatability. Qualification advances only when the programme can reproduce the component, inspect it consistently and define what constitutes an acceptable deviation or repair.

The test infrastructure is specialized, but scarcity rent is unproven

High-heat-flux facilities reproduce the thermal loads that plasma-facing components experience. Fusion for Energy says only a small number of facilities can perform this work at the required scale.

HELCZA in Plzeň tested three full-scale and two partial-scale ITER first-wall prototypes between 2021 and 2024. Each full-scale prototype completed 9,000 cycles at approximately 2 MW per square metre. These were beryllium-covered first-wall prototypes, but the campaigns established relevant capability in cooling, joints, instrumentation and post-test inspection. HELCZA has since been reconfigured for tungsten-armoured divertor work. (HELCZA programme)

Other nodes include JUDITH at Forschungszentrum Jülich for post-irradiation heat-flux testing, NRG PALLAS for irradiation qualification and the Efremov Institute’s Russian testing lineage. Together they form an evidence network rather than a single monopolistic supplier.

The case for an immediate capacity bottleneck remains incomplete, however. Public records do not disclose facility utilization, queues, denied access, turnaround times or campaign pricing. “Few facilities” establishes specialization. It does not prove that test slots are currently delaying ITER or commercial developers.

That missing evidence changes how the opportunity should be framed. Validation facilities may eventually capture pricing power because their accepted protocols and historical datasets are difficult to reproduce. For now, the observable market is programme-funded engineering and qualification work, not demonstrated scarcity rent.

Tungsten operation pulls two supporting systems into the foreground

ITER chose tungsten partly to reduce problems associated with beryllium, including toxicity, dust, melting, tritium retention and a later wholesale first-wall replacement. The material also introduces greater sensitivity to tungsten impurities in the plasma core. ITER’s mitigation package therefore includes more electron-cyclotron heating and boronization.

The boronization system illustrates how far the plant boundary expands. ITER plans to deposit a boron film only 10 to 100 nanometres thick using 5% diborane diluted in carrier gas. Delivering that film requires a secured gas cabin, 21 injection points, more than one kilometre of lines in the Tokamak Building, about 400 metres of in-vessel piping and four additional anodes.

The process is established in smaller machines. ASDEX Upgrade found that boronization removed oxygen and widened the operating space of its tungsten-wall plasma. WEST reported lower oxygen, improved startup and its first L–H transition after fresh boronization. EAST performed six boronizations and more than 400 pulses during a 2026 campaign supporting ITER.

What remains unproven is the integrated operating model at ITER scale in a tritiated environment: deposition uniformity, hazardous-gas treatment, retained-tritium control and cleaning. ITER’s baseline assessed boronization at a maximum frequency of roughly once every two weeks and identified ion-cyclotron wall conditioning as a possible removal mechanism if retained tritium accumulates. Physical installation of the system is expected to begin in 2028. (ITER boronization system)

The RF expansion is larger still. ITER’s original procurement structure covered 24 one-megawatt gyrotrons: six from Europe, eight from Japan, eight from Russia and two from India. The revised baseline calls for 48 gyrotrons at initial operation and another 24 for the first deuterium-tritium phase.

That creates a 72-unit requirement through the first DT campaign, plus associated high-voltage systems, cooling, launchers, transmission lines, buildings, commissioning and spares. ITER began installing Russian units in 2026 and expects further Japanese and Russian deliveries by 2032. (ITER gyrotron programme)

For ITER, spending has already migrated beyond armour into RF equipment, toxic-gas handling, diagnostics and operating support. Whether commercial plants reproduce the same package depends on architecture and operating regime.

Commercial projects are translating the problem, not copying ITER

Commercial fusion programmes increasingly disclose concrete tungsten work, weakening the earlier impression that their first-wall strategies are almost entirely hidden.

STEP’s programme at West Burton is developing a prototype power plant targeted for 2040. Its current limiter concept uses tungsten armour on a CuCrZr heat sink, while UKAEA and Oxford Sigma are investigating manufacturing routes, sacrificial sections and ways to reduce difficult tungsten-to-steel or tungsten-to-copper interfaces.

Commonwealth Fusion Systems has disclosed tungsten and tungsten-alloy plasma-facing work for SPARC and an advanced tungsten-alloy inner-wall pathway for ARC. It is also qualifying suppliers and developing irradiation evidence rather than treating the wall as an unspecified future subsystem.

Neither programme has demonstrated a repeatable commercial first-wall supply chain. Their importance lies in showing how the ITER problem is being translated into architecture-specific qualification routes.

The transfer from ITER will therefore be partial. ITER may establish a grammar—accepted test sequences, manufacturing records, inspection practices, configuration control and maintenance assumptions—without establishing the final commercial material stack.

Future plants may use thinner armour, reduced-activation steel, vanadium alloys, sacrificial modules or other replaceable designs. They may also face neutron damage one to two orders of magnitude above ITER’s roughly 3 dpa first-wall exposure. Qualification records produced for ITER cannot automatically close that gap.

Control may sit in the evidence record rather than the metal

The upstream tungsten market matters, but probably later. A fleet study estimated whole-reactor tungsten consumption between 3,945 and 29,034 tonnes over 40 full-power years for selected plant designs and power levels. Global mine production was approximately 85,000 tonnes of tungsten content in 2025, including 67,000 tonnes from China.

Those figures do not imply a near-term shortage caused by fusion. They show how the bottleneck could migrate after repeat plant orders appear. The modelled tungsten totals include shielding and other reactor structures; they are not first-wall-only demand estimates.

In the present phase, more strategic control may accumulate around accepted processes and records:

  • armour production and joining windows that consistently avoid defects;
  • irradiation, high-heat-flux and non-destructive examination protocols;
  • digital custody of material batches, deviations and operating exposure;
  • RF equipment with a qualified installed base and long-term service support;
  • remote-maintenance and activated-component handling procedures;
  • standards that determine which evidence regulators and customers accept.

CFS’s use of Siemens NX and Teamcenter, combined with an NVIDIA-supported SPARC digital twin, indicates how this record may be organized. For plasma-facing components, the commercially important software object is the controlled qualification history linking a material batch to fabrication, inspection, testing, installation and eventual replacement.

That creates potential switching costs. A nominally equivalent supplier may be difficult to substitute when the incumbent’s process history is embedded in the plant’s safety, maintenance and reliability case.

Public-company exposure is therefore indirect. Thales participates in the gyrotron ecosystem; Siemens and NVIDIA provide engineering and digital-twin infrastructure; Eni combines investment, future power purchases and prospective operating collaboration with CFS. These links identify control points and possible spending paths, not material fusion revenue or investment recommendations.

The strongest counterargument is that other gates will dominate

The central thesis can fail in two ways.

First, qualification capacity may expand before commercial demand becomes large. Europe already has two panel factories. HELCZA has adapted its facilities. STEP and CFS are developing distinct material routes. If full-size components achieve stable yield and multiple facilities can run campaigns without delay, first-wall qualification becomes a manageable industrial workstream rather than a structural chokepoint.

Second, fusion plants may be paced by entirely different systems. Licensing, tritium breeding, magnets, cryogenics, construction integration, grid connection and project finance can all bind before the first wall. CFS has announced a Virginia ARC site, a 200 MW Google offtake, an Eni agreement valued above $1 billion and entry into the PJM interconnection process. Those commitments demonstrate plant-level demand formation, but they do not establish first-wall warranties, availability guarantees, replacement reserves or supplier liability.

The regulatory layer is also incomplete. ASME included Section III Division 4 for fusion energy devices in its 2025 Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission published its proposed fusion-machine rule on February 26, 2026 and plans to complete its final rule and consolidated guidance by the end of 2027. No current rule makes an ITER-derived tungsten joint, heat-flux cycle count or boronization system mandatory for a commercial plant. (NRC rulemaking status)

The surviving conclusion is therefore restrained: integrated plasma-facing-system evidence will be a necessary commercial gate, but current evidence does not show that it will dominate every project or that ITER’s exact tungsten–boronization–ECH package will become universal.

The closest base rate is JET’s ITER-like Wall

The closest historical analogue is the Joint European Torus programme’s ITER-like Wall. JET replaced its previous carbon-facing configuration with beryllium in the main chamber and tungsten in the divertor, using the machine to expose plasma-performance, conditioning, disruption and component-integration issues before ITER operation.

The similarity is procedural. A material change forced the programme to learn how component behaviour, plasma control and operating practice interacted.

The difference is scale and duty. JET did not operate a 600-square-metre tungsten first wall with ITER’s planned heat-removal duty, tritiated boronization infrastructure or expanded gyrotron fleet. ITER is extending the same risk-reduction logic into a much larger integrated system.

WEST and ASDEX Upgrade provide a second base rate: tungsten-wall operating problems can be improved through conditioning and operating changes. Their experience reduces the probability that boronization is an unsolved physics problem. It does not settle ITER-scale safety, tritium retention or commercial maintenance economics.

What would prove the thesis wrong

The thesis would weaken materially if several full-size tungsten panels reached acceptance with stable factory yield, short lead times and at least two independent supply routes, while testing facilities showed no meaningful queue or capacity pressure.

It would break more decisively if a commercial plant obtained customer and regulatory acceptance for a low-tungsten, liquid-metal, sacrificial or otherwise non-ITER plasma-facing architecture that avoided recurrent boronization, large RF expansion and specialized external heat-flux testing.

A third falsifier would be economic. Commercial contracts might show that armour, testing, gas handling, RF and evidence management are competitively sourced pass-through costs, while magnets, construction, fuel cycle, financing or plant integration capture nearly all strategic value and schedule risk.

Until those records appear, the first wall should be treated as a proof package whose commercial importance rises after more visible engineering milestones are cleared.

What to watch

  • By 2027-12-31: the NRC publishes its final fusion-machine rule and consolidated licensing guidance.
  • By 2028-12-31: ITER records the start of physical boronization-system installation.
  • By 2030-12-31: F4E or ITER accepts a full-scale tungsten-armoured first-wall panel.
  • By 2031-12-31: a commercial developer releases a PFC package with at least three lifecycle criteria.
  • By 2033-12-31: ITER reports 48 gyrotrons installed or formally accepted for research operation.
