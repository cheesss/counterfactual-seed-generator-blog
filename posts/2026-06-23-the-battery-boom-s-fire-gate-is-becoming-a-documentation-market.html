The Battery Boom’s Second Gate: Turning Fire Evidence Into Buildable Projects

The obvious battery-storage constraints are not imaginary. Developers need cells, land, interconnection rights, transformers, switchgear, financing and an offtake structure before a project can approach construction.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-23 · 16 min read

In brief

  • 24 GW | planned U.S. battery additions in 2026 still require project conversion.
  • 657 days | Potentia exposed the work hidden before California’s formal clock.
  • 2,060 GW | interconnection queues remain the larger national constraint.
  • 90 days | SB 283 limits the inspection window for covered CEC projects.

Interconnection remains the strongest quantified national bottleneck. Berkeley Lab’s end-2025 update counted more than 2,060 GW of generation and storage in active U.S. queues, including roughly 749 GW of storage. Projects completed in 2025 took a median of more than five years from interconnection request to commercial operation, while 75% of capacity in the 2000–2020 request cohort had withdrawn. (Berkeley Lab interconnection queues)

Grid equipment can impose another independent delay. DOE reported an average lead time of 38 weeks across transmission and distribution equipment in 2023, with costs rising nearly 30% year over year. NERC reported an average transformer lead time of approximately 120 weeks in 2024 and a range of 80 to 210 weeks for large transformers. These figures cover different equipment populations, but they establish the same point: a fire-approved battery project can still wait years for the grid hardware required to operate. (DOE energy-sector supply-chain review)

Fire-code acceptance therefore is not the dominant national bottleneck. It is a sequential conversion gate that appears after developers have routed around more visible constraints. At that stage, nominal megawatts must become a constructible, insurable and locally acceptable facility.

Fire acceptance governs the conversion from equipment to project

A grid-scale battery is not merely a collection of containers. It is an industrial facility that must satisfy an authority having jurisdiction, local fire officials, planning authorities, insurers, lenders and first responders. The evidence package can include UL 9540 listing, UL 9540A fire-propagation testing, hazard-mitigation analysis, explosion and plume modeling, water supply, emergency procedures, commissioning documents, monitoring and responder training.

Moss Landing explains why this evidence has acquired political force. EPA records describe the January 2025 fire as involving a 300 MW battery system with approximately 100,000 lithium-ion modules, about 55% of them damaged. A later federal order records approximately 1,200 evacuees, water-only firefighting and a respondent-funded removal action. Those records establish the incident’s scale and consequences, not its final root cause or the risk of newer modular systems. (EPA Moss Landing order)

The permitting consequence is broader than the incident itself. Every later project may be asked to explain how its chemistry, enclosure, spacing, detection, ventilation, water strategy and emergency plan differ from the facility that produced the public record.

California’s SB 283 formalizes that scrutiny for covered applications submitted after January 1, 2026. The law reaches CEC opt-in battery projects and locally reviewed systems meeting its capacity threshold, requires consultation with the relevant fire authority before application and establishes inspection before operation. For covered CEC projects, however, the inspection authority has a 90-day window after notice of completed installation; the CEC may authorize operation if the authority does not act. The law makes fire acceptance legally material without giving local inspection an unlimited veto. (California SB 283)

California’s clock reveals where the work accumulates

Potentia-Viridi is the clearest demonstration of the difference between a statutory clock and a total project cycle. The 400 MW / 3,200 MWh Alameda County project entered the CEC process on August 7, 2024. The application was found incomplete on September 6, 2024, deemed complete on August 29, 2025 and approved on May 26, 2026.

The formal decision phase lasted exactly 270 days. The interval from initial receipt to approval lasted approximately 657 days. California’s streamlined process worked as designed after completeness, but more than a year of qualification and correction occurred before that clock began. (CEC Potentia-Viridi project record)

Potentia’s fire documents show the physical work behind those dates. The remote site’s plan includes two 30,000-gallon emergency-water tanks, responder staging, remote monitoring, annual first-responder familiarization and an assumed 24-to-48-hour post-incident fire watch. The project was approved, but approval did not make the fire package disappear.

Darden shows the same sequence at a larger scale. The Fresno County project combines 1,150 MW of solar with a 1,150 MW / 4,600 MWh battery system. It was filed in November 2023, deemed complete in September 2024 and received its final order in June 2025. A full construction notice followed in January 2026.

Darden’s compliance phase includes shipment state-of-charge limits, fire access, thermal monitoring, a cited 1,500-gpm water requirement, surveillance, annual training and a fire-service funding arrangement. The lesson is not that fire review stopped Darden. It is that fire acceptance continued as a contractual and operating workstream after the principal permit had been granted. (CEC Darden project record)

Corby is the live negotiation case. The applicant proposed a 24,000-gallon tank designed around approximately 250 gallons per minute. Dixon Fire cited a 500-gpm NFPA 1142 minimum and a local 1,500-gpm reference. The larger number is a negotiating position, not a located final permit condition. The underlying dispute concerns what water supply, detection, access, data and responder capability will make the project acceptable. (CEC Corby fire discussion)

Compass is the necessary counterexample. ENGIE asked the CEC to suspend processing while it assessed alternative sites, resource adequacy, market changes and economic viability. Fire opposition and HMA requirements remained in the record, but the applicant identified a different commercial gate. (Compass suspension request)

Local rulebooks turn uncertainty into project scope

Orange County shows how a moratorium can become a specification. Ordinance 25-017 took effect on December 4, 2025 in the county’s unincorporated territory. It requires Planning Commission use-permit review, excludes parcels in very high fire-hazard zones, creates a 100-foot buffer from residential and community uses unless justified, and requires hazard analysis, emergency plans, decommissioning and financial assurance.

The ordinance is narrower than a countywide ban. It does not govern incorporated cities, and its text addresses the potential nonapplication of local provisions to projects using California’s opt-in pathway. Its importance lies in the conversion of uncertainty into a repeatable checklist. (Orange County BESS ordinance)

Kern County offers the technical version of that checklist. Its application guide calls for construction and operating permits, scaled drawings, equipment listings, access and water information, fire suppression and detection, ventilation, exhaust, deflagration controls, commissioning documents and HMA/FMEA review. It is a sophisticated county template, not a national standard, but it demonstrates what mature AHJ practice looks like. (Kern County ESS application guide)

Pembroke, Massachusetts shows why a technically extensive packet may still be insufficient. The proposed installation uses six Tesla Megapack 2 XL units with CATL lithium-iron-phosphate cells. The Code Red analysis references Tesla fire-test materials, TÜV testing, Fisher Engineering and Fire & Risk Alliance. The final HMA, site-specific emergency plan and commissioning plan were deferred construction-permit submissions rather than completed documents in the reviewed packet.

In April 2026, Pembroke’s Zoning Board of Appeals unanimously returned the project to the Planning Board for additional site-specific fire-protection, emergency-response and life-safety information. The failure was not an absence of engineering. It was a gap between a product-level package and the local evidence required for the site. (Pembroke municipal record)

Project 903 in Armour Township reached a more severe outcome. The 4.99 MW / 19.9 MWh proposal included nine EVLOFLEX containers and an HMA supported by risk, explosion and computational-fluid-dynamics work. The township refused the planning applications in January 2026, citing safety, emergency-response capacity and firefighter readiness. The municipal record now lists executed Minutes of Settlement dated July 17, 2026, so the refusal remains historical evidence but the ultimate disposition is no longer wholly unresolved.

Other jurisdictions show that large condition packages can produce approval rather than rejection. Santa Fe County approved Rancho Viejo’s 48 MW battery component after a hearing officer had recommended denial; the final path included 93 fire-prevention conditions before commissioning. Saline Township approved the 100 MW / 400 MWh Voyager project with conditions covering fire-department satisfaction, training, water infrastructure and operating readiness. Voyager’s public package describes 120 battery containers, a 32,000-gallon water tank, dry hydrants and container-level gas, smoke and heat detection.

These cases point to a conditional-approval market, not a simple binary of permitted versus prohibited.

The commercial transition is toward qualified configurations

The ecosystem is in early deployment, with pockets approaching repeatable procurement. The relevant transition is from assembling a new safety file for every project to selling a controlled configuration that utilities, AHJs, insurers and financiers can repeatedly recognize.

Entergy Arkansas’s public BESS procurement materials illustrate the direction. Its build-own-transfer structure assigns development, design, procurement, construction, commissioning and pre-closing risk to the seller. The scope specifies detection, purge logic, deflagration controls, suppression, transformers, switchgear, cybersecurity and equipment lead-time disclosure. It also names approved BESS suppliers, including Fluence, LG Energy Solution Vertech, Powin, Sungrow, Tesla, Wärtsilä and Prevalon. (Entergy Arkansas BESS scope)

That list is not an award ranking. It shows that safety evidence is becoming part of ordinary vendor qualification. For publicly traded suppliers such as Tesla, Fluence, LG Energy Solution, Sungrow and Wärtsilä, exposure to the constraint lies in their ability to control the tested product generation, firmware, monitoring, commissioning procedures, warranty interface and supporting engineering evidence. It is not simply exposure to cell demand.

The likely commercial unit is an accepted project configuration: equipment plus test evidence, controlled design assumptions, BMS and EMS functions, installation procedures, emergency plans, training, commissioning records and insurance documentation. An integrator that can preserve that envelope across projects may reduce later review work and retain the customer relationship.

Software and data may become part of the control point. AHJs already ask about gas and heat detection, BMS data, alarms, visual indicators and remote shutdown. Utilities increasingly require cybersecurity plans. The platform holding the authoritative record of firmware, alarm thresholds, sensor calibration, maintenance and commissioning could become difficult to replace, although public contracts do not yet disclose data rights, portability or regulator access.

Spend is migrating, but durable margin capture remains unproven

The spending is visible. Projects pay for fire testing, certification, HMA/FMEA, plume and explosion analysis, water systems, access, monitoring, emergency planning, commissioning, training, insurance and post-incident work. What remains unproven is whether those expenditures create a scarce, durable and high-margin market.

Some spending will migrate upstream. Integrators can internalize repeated engineering and sell a validated enclosure with its supporting documentation. Some will remain irreducibly local: water availability, site geometry, fire-department staffing, evacuation context, land-use politics and community engagement cannot be standardized at the factory.

The firms positioned around the workflow include testing bodies, fire-protection engineers, detection suppliers, civil contractors, monitoring platforms, insurers and brokers. Chubb’s risk-engineering guidance treats UL 9540 certification, UL 9540A analysis, spacing, off-gas monitoring, ventilation, water, commissioning and fire-service preplanning as underwriting inputs. Marsh markets a BESS insurance facility while acknowledging limited loss data and inconsistent engineering standards. (Chubb BESS risk guidance)

Yet mandatory work does not automatically produce pricing power. Testing can be competitively supplied. Engineering can be bundled into a turnkey contract. Tanks, hydrants and access roads may be local, low-margin construction. Insurance capacity is not the same as premium revenue. A single integrator’s contract can include subcontracted testing, software and civil work, creating substantial double-counting risk when mapping the value pool.

Post-incident records show real activity but not a clean market size. The Gateway Energy Storage fire generated 198 documented county hazardous-materials staff hours, environmental sampling and responsible-party cleanup obligations. Warwick’s December 2025 incident led the village to approve an estimated $24,600 environmental investigation, with six surface-soil samples, six borings and six groundwater samples. These are evidence of cost migration, not evidence of sector-wide margins.

Non-lithium systems offer another possible relief path, but they do not escape the broader qualification stack. SMUD’s 3.6 MW / 28.8 MWh iron-flow demonstration carries a $10 million CEC grant and still requires permitting, procurement, commissioning, safety plans and measurement and verification. Form Energy’s East Road iron-air project was reduced from 5 MW / 500 MWh to 1.5 MW / 150 MWh because of substation charging restrictions. Lower flammability may reduce one siting concern while interconnection, procurement and finance continue to bind.

The historical base rate favors standardization over permanent scarcity

The closest prior episode is California’s creation of a centralized opt-in pathway for strategic clean-energy projects. That process did not remove every local or technical issue, but it converted fragmented review into a formal sequence with a post-completeness target. Darden and Potentia show that the route can approve very large storage projects.

The comparable code episode is New York’s post-fire standardization. Stronger fire codes and model requirements can absorb an incident-driven shock and turn uncertain review into a repeatable package. The similarity today is the move from public fear toward formal testing, separation and emergency-response rules. The difference is that BESS approval remains distributed across local fire departments, land-use authorities, insurers and project-specific site conditions.

EPRI’s industry-level data also point toward relief. Its failure-rate chart reports a 99% decline in incidents per deployed capacity from 2018 through 2025, even though absolute incidents did not fall to zero. The chart includes nonpublic incidents and cannot support supplier rankings, but it argues against assuming that fire risk must worsen mechanically as the fleet grows. (EPRI BESS failure database)

The likely horizon is therefore 2026 through 2028 in California and other high-scrutiny or technically under-resourced jurisdictions. Fire acceptance should remain a consequential sequential gate during that period. It could ease into a predictable compliance cost if post-SB 283 procedures stabilize, AHJs reuse accepted templates, insurers recognize common engineering packages and product-level dossiers survive review across multiple jurisdictions.

The strongest counterargument is that relief is already arriving

Potentia met California’s formal clock. Darden advanced into construction. Orange County replaced a moratorium with rules. Rancho Viejo and Voyager converted large fire packages into conditional approvals. EPRI reports improving capacity-normalized safety performance. These outcomes contradict the strongest version of the thesis—that fire scrutiny will generally stop large projects or become the dominant national pacing constraint.

They do not eliminate the narrower claim. Fire acceptance can still change layout, water infrastructure, emergency procedures, completeness timing and operating conditions after a project has survived interconnection, equipment and finance. The gate matters because it determines conversion, not because it is always the first or largest obstacle.

The thesis would fail more decisively if most post-2026 CEC projects met the 270-day target without fire-driven redesign; if the first SB 283 inspections produced no delay beyond the statutory fallback; if one product dossier won repeated approval without new large-scale testing; and if a multi-project delay dataset showed fire evidence causing only a small minority of schedule events.

What to watch

  • December 31, 2026: Corby’s final order and any fire-driven change to layout, water or operating date.
  • June 30, 2027: Seahawk’s completeness decision and whether correction requires a material safety redesign.
  • June 30, 2027: California BESS siting text entering formal code-adoption review under SB 283.
  • December 31, 2028: The first Orange County disposition under Ordinance 25-017 and its elapsed review time.
  • December 31, 2028: One product dossier securing three U.S. approvals without new product-level fire testing.

The battery boom remains constrained first by queues, equipment and capital. Fire acceptance is the next conversion test: whether an available battery system can become a project that regulators, insurers, first responders and communities will allow to operate.
