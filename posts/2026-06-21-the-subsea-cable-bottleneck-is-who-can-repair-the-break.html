When Subsea Cable Repair Readiness Becomes the Bottleneck

Global connectivity depends on an industrial service most people never see: the ability to find a damaged cable, dispatch the right ship, secure permission to enter the right water, recover the cable from the seabed, splice it, test it, and put it back into service.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-21 · Updated 2026-06-28 · 10 min read

In brief

  • Submarine cables carry more than 99% of international data.
  • Most cable faults are accidental, and ordinary breakages are usually repaired without a national crisis.
  • The bottleneck shows up when faults cluster or hit contested, shallow, or politically sensitive waters.
  • TeleGeography and Infra-Analytics project 48% more deployed cable-km by 2040 while roughly two-thirds of maintenance ships reach end-of-service life.
  • In those stressed cases, a ready ship, permits, and insurance can matter more than raw fiber capacity.

That repair chain used to look like marine maintenance. It now looks like strategic infrastructure.

ITU says submarine cables carry more than 99% of international data exchange and reported more than 200 cable repairs worldwide in 2023. ICPC describes a network of about 500 cable systems and roughly 1.8 million kilometers of infrastructure, with about 150 to 200 telecom cable faults per year, mostly from anchors and fishing rather than sabotage. (ITU submarine cable resilience) and (ICPC cable maintenance facts)

That base rate argues against panic. The cable system fails often and gets repaired often. The sharper question is what happens when faults cluster in politically sensitive waters, conflict zones, or shallow chokepoints where repair access, insurance, permits, and trusted suppliers matter as much as the cable itself.

The scarce asset is readiness

TeleGeography and Infra-Analytics put a hard number on the maintenance problem. Their report summary projects a 48% net increase in deployed cable kilometers by 2040, while roughly two-thirds of cable maintenance ships reach end-of-service life by then. They estimate that about $3 billion would fund 15 replacement ships and five additional ships to sustain current service levels and avoid repair delays. (TeleGeography maintenance outlook)

That forecast changes the bottleneck. Capacity is not just fiber pairs, landing stations, and repeaters. Capacity also means ships, crews, spares, depots, ROVs, ploughs, marine survey capability, permits, insurance, and a maintenance agreement that gives a cable owner priority when several systems need attention.

The timing is the uncomfortable part. The same analysis finds that roughly two-thirds of today's maintenance ships reach end-of-service life by 2040, just as deployed cable kilometers rise by nearly half. A fleet that is shrinking through retirement while covering more route-kilometers is a setup for longer repair queues. A maintenance agreement matters because it converts ship availability into contracted priority. The scarce, ownable asset is not a cable but a claim on a ship's time.

SubCom says it operates eight purpose-built cable ships and fifteen submersible tools. (SubCom cable systems overview) NEC says it has laid more than 450,000 kilometers of submarine cables and offers design, supply, installation, testing, and maintenance. (NEC submarine cable systems) Nokia's ASN transaction announcement says France is taking control of Alcatel Submarine Networks, which it describes as having more than 750,000 kilometers of deployed optical submarine cable and fleet-based marine and maintenance operations. (Nokia on the French-state ASN deal)

This is where sovereignty enters. A state that treats cable repair as a private telecom service will depend on whichever ships, insurers, crews, and permits remain available in a crisis. A state that treats repair as strategic infrastructure will care about domestic or trusted vessel access, supplier control, landing rules, spares, and naval or coast-guard coordination.

The policy system has noticed

The European Union has moved from concern to operational planning. The Commission's cable-security work covers prevention, detection, response, recovery, and deterrence. In 2025, EU work on mapping and coordinated risk assessment covered capacity, ownership, technical specifications, landing sites, incidents, maintenance, repair processes, physical and cyber threats, supply-chain dependencies, legal factors, and administrative factors. (European Commission cable security toolbox)

Reuters reported that the Commission proposed boosting surveillance and creating a fleet of vessels available for emergency undersea cable repairs. That idea moves cable repair out of the background. The policy object becomes a reserve or trusted repair capacity, not only a better map of cables. (Reuters on the EU emergency repair fleet)

The United States is hardening the licensing side. The FCC's 2025 submarine cable landing rule requires more detailed system information and adds foreign-adversary, covered-equipment, cybersecurity, and physical-security controls. A cable landing license now sits closer to national security review than routine telecom paperwork. (Federal Register submarine cable landing rules)

The cable owner needs more than redundancy

Hyperscalers understand the demand side. Meta's Project Waterworth is a multi-billion-dollar, multi-year subsea system spanning more than 50,000 kilometers with 24 fiber pairs, and Meta explicitly links the project to AI-era connectivity. It also says the design will use deep-water routing and enhanced burial in high-risk shallow areas. (Meta on Project Waterworth)

Google's Firmina shows a different resilience design. Google says Firmina was designed to run from a single power source at one end if other power sources become unavailable. That is a clever way to design around certain failure modes. It does not replace the need to repair physical infrastructure when a cable breaks. (Google on Firmina)

The repair problem becomes more acute in contested waters. Reuters' Hormuz report makes the point directly: physical cable repair may be technically routine, but conflict-zone decisions can depend on vessel-owner risk tolerance, insurers, mines, and permits to enter territorial waters. Satellites cannot replace subsea cables at comparable cost and volume. (Reuters on Hormuz cable repair risks)

That is the narrow bottleneck. A cable can have redundancy on paper and still face a repair delay if the right ship cannot enter the area, if war-risk insurance fails, if a permit stalls, if a supplier lacks trust clearance, or if multiple incidents compete for the same marine assets.

Maintenance agreements become strategic contracts

The Atlantic Cable Maintenance Agreement gives a glimpse of the institutional layer. ACMA describes itself as a cooperative maintenance agreement with more than 60 members and one of the largest dedicated maintenance fleets in the business, with three cable ships positioned for its region. That kind of agreement converts ship availability into a shared resilience product. (ACMA maintenance agreement overview)

The same logic applies to cable depots, spare repeaters, joint kits, route surveys, cable burial, marine domain awareness, and trusted suppliers. The owner of the cable wants bandwidth. The operator of a national economy wants repair assurance.

Who controls the constraint

The repair base is a small, specialized world, and that concentration is the point. Cooperative maintenance agreements pool and reserve vessels: ACMA has more than 60 members but only three dedicated cable ships for its region, and Global Marine says it has performed roughly a third of all fiber-optic cable maintenance operations globally. Orange Marine and Elettra's six ships have laid more than 220,000 kilometers and carried out nearly 550 repairs. (ACMA maintenance agreement overview) and (Global Marine on the ACMA extension)

Several of those shares and repair counts are operator-reported, and basin-by-basin vessel utilization and day-rate data are not public. Current scarcity is therefore inferred rather than directly measured.

The vertically integrated suppliers still matter because they hold lifecycle capability, not just cable inventory: SubCom has eight cable ships and fifteen submersible tools, NEC says it has laid more than 450,000 kilometers of cable, and Nokia says ASN has more than 750,000 kilometers deployed and is moving under French-state control. (SubCom cable systems overview), (NEC submarine cable systems), and (Nokia on the French-state ASN deal)

Above the commercial layer, sovereignty is closing in. The FCC's 2025 landing-license rule adds foreign-adversary and covered-equipment controls, the European Commission is planning emergency repair capacity, and USNI reported that the United States keeps two US-flagged cable ships on 24-hour notice. (Federal Register submarine cable landing rules), (European Commission cable security toolbox), and (USNI on wartime cable repair) So the real control points are not just ships but approved ships, approved crews, approved equipment, landing-license conditions, war-risk insurance, and permission to enter territorial waters. In clustered faults or contested waters, the party with assured access to an approved ship, crew, insurance, and permits can shape how fast a break is repaired.

The counterargument matters

The system is not collapsing. ICPC's data cut against the most alarmist version of the story: cable length has expanded while reported fault counts have remained broadly within the 150 to 200 annual range, and most incidents still come from anchors and fishing rather than hostile action. The UK Parliament's national-security report also says business-as-usual breakages are generally well managed and do not create an imminent threat to UK connectivity. (ICPC cable maintenance facts) and (UK Parliament cable security report)

There is even a way the policy response backfires. The FCC is tightening landing-license review and trusted-equipment rules at the same time it tries to streamline deployment; if compliance, route-data disclosure, and foreign-adversary due diligence grow heavy enough, the practical result could be fewer cables on fewer routes. The EU's emergency-fleet idea points the other way: pair better monitoring and surveillance with faster repair so scarce ships are needed less often. (Federal Register submarine cable landing rules) and (European Commission cable security toolbox)

That makes the bottleneck conditional. It is not a claim that cables are breaking everywhere or that sabotage explains most damage. It is a claim that repair capacity becomes strategic when more traffic concentrates in higher-capacity systems, more projects serve AI and cloud demand, and more faults occur near waters where permission, insurance, and sovereign trust slow the response.

The danger lives in the tails, not the average. The UK Parliament report warns that traffic is concentrating into fewer, higher-capacity cables, which turns each one into a higher-value target, and the TeleGeography forecast shows the maintenance fleet aging into exactly that concentration. (UK Parliament cable security report) and (TeleGeography maintenance outlook)

The thesis bites when faults stop being isolated and accidental. Reuters reported in January 2025 that weather kept Taiwan's coast guard from boarding a suspect vessel after cable damage and that repairs were expected only weeks later. Reuters' Hormuz report shows a different version of the same problem: vessel-owner risk, insurers, mines, and territorial-water permits can stall a technically routine splice. (Reuters on Taiwan cable damage) and (Reuters on Hormuz cable repair risks)

USNI's 2022 cable-security-fleet analysis says the United States has only two US-flagged cable ships on 24-hour notice and warns that wartime force majeure, flag, crew, and insurance constraints can break peacetime maintenance assumptions. (USNI on wartime cable repair) Redundancy on paper does not help if every nearby ship is committed, uninsurable, or barred from the water. That is the condition under which a repair queue, not a fiber shortage, becomes the thing that decides whether a country stays connected.

The question to watch is not only how many new cables are announced. Watch cable-ship orders, emergency fleet plans, long-term maintenance agreements, landing-license conditions, state ownership of suppliers, depot buildouts, spare-kit inventories, route-hardening language, and war-risk insurance treatment.

Bandwidth gets the attention because it is easy to sell. Repair readiness is harder to see. In a stressed network, the decisive asset may be the right ship, in the right jurisdiction, with the right crew, permission, insurance, and spare parts before the outage becomes a strategic event.
