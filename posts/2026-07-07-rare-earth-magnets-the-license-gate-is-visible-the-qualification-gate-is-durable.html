Rare-Earth Magnets and the Moving Industrial Bottleneck

China’s April 2025 export controls made the rare-earth magnet problem look like an administrative shortage. Beijing required licences for specified heavy rare earths, magnet materials and related products. Chinese rare-earth magnet exports then fell to 1,239 tonnes in May 2025 before recovering to 5,952 tonnes in December.

That rebound matters because an export licence can restore an already qualified supply route much faster than a new mine, refinery or magnet plant can enter production. Ford-linked suppliers received streamlined year-long licences in late 2025. Yet customs data later showed no dysprosium- or terbium-oxide exports to Japan from November 2025 through May 2026. The system could reopen for one customer group while remaining closed for another. (Reuters licences) Source: Reuters Japan

This makes licensing the first gate during an acute disruption. It does not make licensing the only gate. A buyer that wants a durable non-China route must still assemble separated oxides, metals, alloys, specialised equipment, repeatable magnet production and customer approval.

The International Energy Agency’s 2026 assessment shows why the mining frame is incomplete. China accounted for roughly 60% of mined magnet rare earths in 2024, but about 91% of refined output and 94% of sintered permanent magnets. Concentration increases after extraction. (IEA: Executive summary)

The physical sequence is longer than “mine-to-magnet” suggests. Ore must be beneficiated. Mixed rare-earth material must be separated into individual oxides. Oxides must be reduced to metals, converted into controlled alloys and strip cast. Magnet plants must mill, align, press, sinter, heat treat, machine, coat and test the material. The customer then approves the process and the finished part. The Department of Energy maps the same sequence from raw-material production through separation, metal refining, alloying, magnet making and downstream products. Source: DOE assessment

Dysprosium and terbium matter most in demanding high-temperature and high-coercivity grades. Producers can reduce their use through grain-boundary diffusion and better recipes, but those methods increase dependence on process control, specialist equipment and tacit manufacturing knowledge. The IEA identifies limited non-China availability of equipment such as strip casters, alignment presses and grain-boundary-diffusion systems. (IEA pathways)

The defensible thesis is therefore narrower than a claim that the West lacks rare-earth assets. Traceable non-China material becomes strategically useful only when the full sequence converts physical output into recurring customer-approved production.

MP provides the clearest realised industrial clock. General Motors and MP announced the Fort Worth arrangement in December 2021. A Texas permit record in 2022 described electrowinning, casting, milling, pressing, sintering and finishing. Fluor reported commercial NdPr metal and trial magnet production in January 2025. MP then reported magnet manufacturing on commercial equipment in December 2025. The sequence took years before the larger 10X campus even reached groundbreaking. Lynas and Neo show the same separation between physical output and commercial maturity. Lynas has produced saleable Dy/Tb outside China, but its first three reported quarters were small and volatile. Neo built Narva in roughly 500 days and produced more than one million magnets, yet still described automotive output as qualification material in May 2026, with two to three commercial programs expected later in the year. (Lynas) (Neo)

Qualification is the scarce commercial asset

Noveon and e-VAC disprove the strongest version of the bottleneck thesis. Western firms can manufacture and ship NdFeB magnets. Noveon has named relationships with GM, ABB and Nidec. e-VAC has shipped from Sumter. These are not laboratory demonstrations. (Noveon-GM) (ABB) (e-VAC)

The missing evidence concerns breadth and repetition. Public disclosures rarely identify accepted part numbers, PPAP approvals, rejected lots, process yields, scrap rates or the number of released customer platforms. A first shipment proves that a route exists. It does not show that the supplier can make several grades at stable yield, pass process changes through customer approval and deliver for multiple production periods.

Automotive PPAP requires evidence that a production process can repeatedly meet engineering specifications at actual production rates. It does not impose a universal qualification duration. Neo’s case may take roughly a year from qualification shipments to anticipated commercial launch; Noveon’s customer deliveries moved faster. Qualification is therefore a customer- and platform-specific clock, not an automatic multi-year delay. (AIAG PPAP)

The scarce commercial asset is the approved combination of material source, alloy, recipe, equipment route, coating, test record and customer file. Changing one element may trigger new evidence or requalification. That gives OEM quality teams and incumbent suppliers control even when upstream capacity expands.

The value split is less clean than the scarcity story

Governments are underwriting more than ore. MP’s DoD package includes a $110/kg NdPr price floor, a $400 million preferred-equity investment, a $150 million HREE separation loan, up to $1 billion of committed financing and a long-term magnet-offtake structure. Lynas’s Japanese arrangement uses the same $110/kg floor logic and gives Japanese buyers priority access to 50%–75% of heavy-rare-earth oxide output. Iluka secured a A$1.65 billion non-recourse government loan for Eneabba. (MP-DoD) (Iluka: Eneabba)

These arrangements reveal two constraints. The first is physical: separation, metallurgy, equipment, yield and qualification. The second is economic: non-China projects may struggle to finance and operate against China-linked pricing without risk sharing.

MP’s first-quarter 2026 Magnetics segment generated $21.1 million of revenue and $9.6 million of adjusted EBITDA, but the reported revenue came from magnetic precursor products rather than clearly disclosed recurring finished-magnet deliveries. Its Materials segment also recognised $42.3 million of price-protection income. The numbers confirm downstream commercial activity and contractual risk transfer; they do not yet establish independent finished-magnet margins at scaled utilisation. (MP Q1 2026)

Value may migrate toward separators, metallisers and qualified magnet makers. It may also remain with governments that absorb risk, OEMs that control production release, or incumbents that retain customer files and process knowledge. The proposed software, testing, traceability, maintenance and logistics markets remain plausible rather than demonstrated: no dominant qualification-software vendor, specialist service network or disclosed recurring service pool has emerged from the public record.

For public-market exposure, MP, Lynas, Iluka, Energy Fuels and Neo sit closest to the physical control points. Fluor has indirect exposure through project execution at MP and Iluka. GM, ABB and Nidec are demand anchors and potential beneficiaries of resilience rather than obvious owners of bottleneck rent. These are descriptions of industrial exposure, not investment recommendations.

The counterargument is getting stronger

The first counterargument is that administrative licensing may remain more important than Western industrial capacity. If China grants broad, durable licences, customers can continue using qualified incumbent suppliers rather than paying to duplicate the chain. The export valve could reopen before alternative projects achieve competitive scale.

The second is that missing downstream capabilities can be bought. Energy Fuels’ proposed acquisition of VAC, alongside its planned ASM transaction, would combine White Mesa separation with operating metal, alloy and magnet businesses. VAC has reported more than one billion magnets shipped, more than 1,000 customers and approximately 85% customer-specific output. A completed and integrated acquisition could import qualification history and process knowledge faster than a greenfield project could build them. The transactions remain conditional, and no traceable White Mesa-to-finished-magnet route has yet been demonstrated. (Energy Fuels–VAC)

The third is substitution. Automakers are testing magnet-free motors, ferrite systems and rare-earth-reduced architectures. GM and Stellantis have invested in Niron’s iron-nitride magnet approach. These alternatives need not replace NdFeB everywhere to reduce demand at the margin. They remain less proven for compact, high-temperature and high-performance applications, but a few successful mass-market launches could weaken the Dy/Tb thesis substantially. (Reuters: India revs up alternate ev motor tests china curbs rare) Source: AP

Defense procurement is also less deterministic than the headline suggests. From January 1, 2027, DFARS extends covered-country restrictions across the NdFeB chain, but the rule preserves exceptions for certain commercially available items, U.S.-processed recycled magnets and nonavailability determinations. Regulation can create demand for compliant supply, yet waivers may remain the practical route when qualified material is unavailable at the required quality, quantity, form or price. (DFARS)

The historical base rate argues against permanence

The closest prior episode is the 2010 China-Japan rare-earth shock. Japan responded over the following decade with stockpiling, supplier diversification, recycling, substitution and trade pressure. A 2026 historical review estimates that China’s share of Japanese rare-earth imports fell from roughly 90% in 2010 to below 60% by 2020. (historical review)

That base rate argues against treating the current constraint as permanent. Industrial systems adapt when prices, policy and supply risk remain elevated long enough.

The difference is that the present dependency reaches further downstream. The 2025 controls affected processed materials and magnets, while the IEA also identifies constraints in equipment and process know-how. Demand now spans EV traction motors, industrial automation, robotics, wind, aerospace and defense. Rebuilding separation, metallisation, magnet manufacturing and customer approval in the same cycle is more complicated than adding a new mine or stockpile.

The relief window runs through the late 2020s

The most defensible horizon is 2026–2030, with the pacing gate changing over time.

During 2026–2027, selective Chinese licensing and limited non-China Dy/Tb output remain the first-order variables. Neo’s expected program launches will test whether qualification converts into automotive revenue. Energy Fuels’ conditional 2027 Phase 1 plan will test whether pilot purity becomes tonne-scale commercial supply. Iluka’s 2027 commissioning target will test whether a large construction project can move into operating separation. (Energy Fuels guidance)

During 2027–2028, successful oxide production would move the bottleneck toward metal reduction, alloying, strip casting, equipment performance and process yield. Commissioning alone would not settle the issue. The relevant evidence would be on-spec material shipped to third parties and converted into magnets outside China.

During 2028–2030, the decisive scarcity should be recurring customer-approved magnet volume from traceable non-China streams. MP’s 10X campus targets commissioning from 2028, but subsystem commissioning, first magnet output and accepted production lots are separate milestones. The sector could reach repeatable procurement across several programs, or remain a collection of strategically supported projects with limited utilisation and opaque customer acceptance.

What would prove this wrong

The thesis fails if China normalises broad multi-year licences across major U.S., European, Japanese and Korean chains, and both magnet and Dy/Tb flows remain stable for four consecutive quarters without recurring customer disruption.

It also fails if several ex-China suppliers clear the chain faster than expected: recurring commercial Dy/Tb shipments, documented non-China metal and alloy conversion, stable magnet yields and customer-confirmed program deliveries within one model-year of qualification samples.

A third failure path is economic. Non-China material may become technically available but remain dependent on price floors, subsidised financing and guaranteed public demand. In that case, the binding constraint is bankability and cost competitiveness rather than chemistry or qualification.

Substitution would provide the cleanest demand-side refutation. Three major automakers launching high-volume magnet-free, ferrite, iron-nitride or HREE-free systems without material penalties in cost, efficiency, thermal performance or mass would reduce the need to clear the existing chain.

The narrower conclusion survives: Western magnet capability exists, but broad sequential clearance has not yet been demonstrated. The scarce deliverable is not a mine, a refinery opening or a first shipment. It is repeated, accepted production from a traceable material route at economics that can persist.

What to watch

  • By June 30, 2027, Neo reports a commercial Narva automotive shipment or program-specific revenue.
  • By December 31, 2027, Energy Fuels ships one third-party, on-spec commercial tonne of Dy or Tb oxide.
  • By December 31, 2028, two ex-China producers report commercial Dy/Tb shipments in two consecutive quarters.
  • By December 31, 2029, three non-China magnet producers report recurring named-program deliveries in consecutive periods.
  • By December 31, 2029, MP formally begins 10X commissioning rather than reporting construction progress alone.
