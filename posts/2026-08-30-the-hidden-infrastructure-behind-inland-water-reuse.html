The Hidden Infrastructure Behind Inland Water Reuse

The visible story in drought-resilient water infrastructure is the purification plant: reverse osmosis, ultraviolet treatment, pumps, wells and new potable-water capacity. Inland projects have another system behind that system. Reverse osmosis does not eliminate salts, PFAS or other rejected constituents. It puts them into a smaller, more concentrated stream that still needs somewhere to go.

The membrane plant is only half the system

That residual outlet is the constraint worth watching. As inland utilities add brackish desalination and potable reuse, some projects will find an existing sewer, regional wastewater plant or suitable injection formation. Others will need dedicated pipelines, wells, high-recovery equipment, evaporation or zero-liquid-discharge systems. The difference can change capital allocation, operating control and sometimes usable plant capacity.

Phoenix offers a striking planning example. The city is developing advanced purification at Cave Creek, North Gateway and the 91st Avenue wastewater complex as drought and changing climate patterns threaten the surface-water sources that provide roughly 98% of its supply.

At North Gateway, Phoenix’s August 2024 unit-cost study modeled an 8-MGD Phase I with $164 million of advanced water treatment and $360 million of concentrate management, in October 2023 dollars. Wastewater collection, wastewater treatment and treated-water conveyance brought the modeled phase total to $1.07 billion.

The important point is not that North Gateway has already been delayed by brine; it remained in design when Phoenix announced $179 million of federal support in August 2025. The point is that residual handling was already large enough in the planning model to exceed the front-end purification line by more than two to one. Phoenix’s figures are planning-level estimates, not awarded construction contracts.

Phoenix’s approved infrastructure plan tells the same story at buildout: $16 per gallon of daily capacity for advanced treatment, $29 for concentrate management and $8.94 for treated-water conveyance. On a difficult inland site, the waste-side architecture can command more planned capital than the membrane process that produces the clean water.

Phoenix shows how geography changes the capital stack

Cave Creek is the useful comparison because it sits inside the same utility system. Arizona’s 2026 draft Aquifer Protection Permit describes an 8-MGD facility whose RO system is designed around 80–85% recovery. Instead of building a dedicated residual-treatment train, Cave Creek sends its concentrate into Phoenix’s wastewater network and onward to the 91st Avenue treatment system.

Construction began in August 2024, and Phoenix expects nearly 7 MGD of purified water by 2027, followed by a required year-long demonstration before direct distribution. The residual problem has not disappeared. A regional receiver has absorbed it into another asset’s hydraulic, salinity and permit envelope.

North Gateway lacks the same cheap-looking answer in Phoenix’s planning tables, which is why dedicated concentrate management appears as a large capital line. That contrast is more useful than a generic claim that inland desalination is “brine constrained.” The constraint depends on who owns compatible downstream capacity.

The same principle appears elsewhere. Goodyear, Arizona, historically sent roughly 0.5 MGD of concentrate from its Bullard brackish-water facility to the sewer. A federal pilot wetland could remove some metals and nutrients but did not solve total dissolved solids, leaving the receiving system as the practical outlet.

San Antonio’s H₂Oaks facility takes another route. The San Antonio Water System says the plant recovers roughly nine of every ten gallons of brackish feedwater and uses two deep injection wells for the residual salts and minerals.

El Paso proves the outlet can become an operating gate

The strongest evidence comes from the Kay Bailey Hutchison Desalination Plant in El Paso. KBH sends concentrate through roughly 22 miles of pipeline to remote deep injection wells.

A 2015 City Council presentation said expansion was limited by concentrate-disposal capacity. Four years later, the problem moved from planning to operations. Injection well JDF-3 developed a casing leak, failed mechanical-integrity requirements and could not be used until repaired. El Paso Water’s 2019 board materials said the desalination plant could not operate at full capacity without that well.

The proposed engineering and compliance contract was only $472,180, a small number beside a desalination plant. But it controlled access to a much larger productive asset. This is what a physical constraint looks like in utility infrastructure: the scarce item may be a compliant disposal well rather than another membrane skid.

El Paso is now pursuing another expansion. The 2026 Region E water plan calls for increasing KBH from 27.5 to 33.5 MGD and estimates the broader strategy at about $101 million. The plan says a partial concentrate-pipeline replacement is required to handle the higher volume and pressure associated with expansion. The utility separately describes a longer-term objective of reaching 42 MGD.

That does not establish that residual capacity is binding current average production. TWDB’s November 2024 database reported about 4.2 MGD of average desalinated output, an 82.5% membrane recovery rate and roughly 1.25 MGD of concentrate. The record supports a narrower conclusion: El Paso has repeatedly had to expand, repair and maintain its residual infrastructure before it can treat the desalination plant as fully usable capacity.

Higher recovery does not make the residual disappear

Utilities have spent years looking for ways to shrink the liquid stream. El Paso’s history shows why pilot recovery numbers need to be separated from commercial operation.

A DuPont DesaliTec pilot at KBH reported 94% recovery, while earlier Texas research explored configurations in the 95–99% range. Yet the current state database still reports about 82.5% membrane recovery for the operating plant. A technically successful high-recovery test has to survive scaling, chemistry, maintenance, energy costs and solids management before it replaces an established disposal route.

El Paso also tried to turn the waste stream into a resource. In 2015, Enviro Water Minerals contracted to operate an adjacent brine-treatment and mineral-recovery facility intended to recover water and produce mineral commodities. El Paso Water’s 2021 board record says EWM failed to operationalize the process before its 2020 contractual deadline; its lender took the facility, which was later sold to Critical Materials Corporation.

The successor arrangement remains relevant, but current public utility materials describe its potential prospectively: more than 2 MGD of returned water at full capacity and a lower-cost disposal route. Public records in the research did not establish sustained present throughput or uptime.

Palm Coast, Florida, offers a better historical base rate for a completed transition. Water Treatment Plant 2 evaluated concentrate alternatives in 2008–09, obtained a zero-liquid-discharge construction permit in 2012, implemented the system in 2015 and received state clearance in early 2017.

The clock matters: roughly a decade separated early alternatives work from accepted full-scale operation. And the waste did not vanish. Palm Coast converted liquid concentrate into lime precipitation, ultrafiltration, sludge thickening, dewatering and off-site solids. By 2022, the city was procuring engineering for a second lime-sludge thickener because that equipment had become critical to ZLD operation and needed redundancy.

That is the closest historical analogue in the record. The similarity today is the conversion of a difficult liquid outlet into another equipment-and-service stack. The difference is that newer potable-reuse projects may also have to account for PFAS chemistry, while western inland projects operate under different geology, energy economics and water portfolios.

PFAS is an overlay, not yet a national capacity shock

PFAS can make the residual problem harder because reverse osmosis concentrates contaminants it rejects. But the public evidence does not support treating PFAS as a uniform national disposal crunch.

The Water Research Foundation’s Project 5323 began in June 2025 to test granular activated carbon, ion exchange and modified organoclay on PFAS-bearing potable-reuse RO concentrate. The $314,266 project runs through 2027. Orange County Water District records also identify Kennedy Jenks and Ovivo/E2metrix in related separation-and-destruction research.

That is qualification-stage evidence, not repeat procurement. The report found no established fleet of full-scale inland municipal RO-concentrate systems repeatedly buying PFAS destruction services.

Federal policy reinforces the distinction. EPA’s April 2026 destruction-and-disposal guidance is non-binding and does not create PFAS-specific disposal requirements. EPA identifies underground injection, landfills and certain thermal systems as available large-scale approaches, but it also says site conditions matter and national data do not show which injection facilities can or will accept off-site PFAS waste.

The nominal infrastructure is substantial: EPA counted 886 Class I injection wells nationally in FY2023. Yet those wells are geographically concentrated. A national well count says little about whether a Phoenix, El Paso or other municipal waste stream meets a nearby operator’s acceptance profile.

Thermal capacity has the same problem. Clean Harbors reported ten active incinerators at five facilities and practical annual capacity of 631,721 tons at year-end 2025. That is real hazardous-waste infrastructure, but it is not a disclosure of available capacity for municipal PFAS-bearing RO concentrate.

Emerging destruction technologies remain earlier in the commercialization sequence. Nasdaq-listed 374Water has municipal supercritical-water-oxidation deployments, including work with Orange County Sanitation District, Olathe, St. Cloud and Orlando. Those projects demonstrate movement from laboratory work toward municipal deployment, but the cited feeds include biosolids and other concentrated wastes rather than a repeatable inland potable-reuse concentrate business.

Who controls the constraint

The most important control points sit outside the membrane hall.

Utilities control whether they own compatible wastewater or injection infrastructure. Receiving wastewater systems control hydraulic and salinity headroom. State regulators such as ADEQ and TCEQ determine whether aquifer-protection and underground-injection systems remain permitted. Well-service firms preserve mechanical integrity. EPC firms decide whether a new plant can exploit an existing receiver or needs dedicated concentration equipment.

Waste companies control another layer through acceptance criteria, logistics and thermal or disposal capacity. Analytical laboratories and operators determine whether the residual remains inside the chemistry and validation envelope required by the selected route.

That structure changes where spending appears. Phoenix books hundreds of millions under concentrate management. El Paso’s constraint appears in injection-well diagnostics and a replacement pipeline. Palm Coast’s former liquid-discharge problem later shows up as sludge-thickening redundancy.

Public-company exposure therefore spans several different parts of the chain rather than one pure-play category. Clean Harbors has hazardous-waste transport, disposal and incineration exposure. 374Water is trying to commercialize destruction systems. Xylem markets PFAS treatment using activated carbon, ion exchange and mobile systems. DuPont supplies membrane and ion-exchange technologies and participated in the KBH high-recovery pilot.

Those are mechanism exposures, not evidence that inland reuse concentrate is material to any company’s total revenue. The report found no public filing that isolates U.S. inland potable-reuse concentrate as a reporting segment.

The strongest counterexample is already operating in Texas

A constraint thesis should fail where the supposedly scarce outlet is available and something else limits production. H₂Oaks is that case.

SAWS says its brackish-desalination system can produce 11,200 acre-feet per year but distributed about 5,700 acre-feet in 2025. Its energy strategy says desalination treatment is roughly three times as energy-intensive as treatment associated with its aquifer-storage-and-recovery system and recommends reducing desalination output when cheaper water is available.

In San Antonio, the residual route works. Two injection wells handle the concentrate, while portfolio economics and energy can determine how hard the desalination plant runs. That is powerful counterevidence against any national claim that concentrate disposal must dominate inland water projects.

Cave Creek makes a related point. Its near-term gate is potable-reuse validation and commissioning, not a dedicated brine plant, because Phoenix can route the concentrate into its regional wastewater system.

The thesis is therefore local and architectural: residual handling can become a binding project constraint when a utility lacks an inexpensive compatible receiver. It is not an argument that every inland membrane project faces the same problem.

Three paths could decide where the value moves

The first path is the Phoenix North Gateway pattern. Difficult inland sites preserve dedicated concentrate infrastructure through final design, and residual management continues to represent 20% or more of the relevant purification-and-residual capital package. Confirmation would come from final designs and awarded procurements at additional projects, not from planning studies alone.

The second path is network relief. Utilities secure sewer, regional wastewater or injection capacity and avoid much of the site-specific concentration equipment. Capital then shifts into conveyance, receiver upgrades, well maintenance, permits and recurring service charges. Cave Creek and H₂Oaks show why this can dominate where infrastructure and geology cooperate.

The third path is sustained high recovery. Moving from 82.5% recovery to 95% reduces the liquid reject fraction from 17.5% of feed to 5%, roughly a 71% reduction. Moving from 90% to 95% halves it. That arithmetic makes the opportunity clear, but it does not prove lower total cost: the rejected mass remains and has to move through precipitation, dewatering, thermal destruction, landfill or another solids route.

The decisive commercialization threshold is therefore not a headline recovery percentage. It is multiple municipal systems operating at or above roughly 95% recovery for long periods while publishing complete energy, chemical, maintenance and solids-disposal performance.

The constraint should be easier to judge by the end of the decade

Several gates have dated settlement points.

Cave Creek is expected to begin producing purified water around 2027 and then complete its required demonstration period. If its sewer route remains intact through commissioning, it strengthens the case that shared receivers can remove a major source of site-specific residual capital.

WRF Project 5323 is also scheduled to finish in 2027. Its public results should clarify whether PFAS-bearing reuse concentrate creates a practical media-use and cost problem large enough to move from research into full-scale procurement.

El Paso’s expansion horizon runs toward 2030 and explicitly includes concentrate-pipeline work. If the 33.5-MGD configuration reaches full operation only after those residual modifications, it will extend an unusually clear multi-decade example of disposal infrastructure pacing usable water capacity.

North Gateway is the larger financial test. If final design preserves something close to the current dedicated concentrate-management package, Phoenix will have moved the constraint from planning arithmetic toward real procurement. If engineers find an inexpensive receiver route and the line collapses, the strongest prospective capital example weakens substantially.

What to watch

  • North Gateway final design retains concentrate management at 20% or more of relevant AWP capital.
  • WRF Project 5323 publishes full PFAS-removal, media-use and treatment-cost results in 2027.
  • Cave Creek completes commissioning and its required demonstration without redesigning the 91st Avenue residual route.
  • KBH’s 33.5-MGD expansion commissions with the planned concentrate-pipeline modifications by the 2030 horizon.
  • Three municipal systems sustain at least 95% recovery for 12 months with complete solids and O&M records.

The purification plant will remain the most visible part of inland reuse. The better test of project difficulty is often one step downstream: whether the concentrated residual already has a permitted, maintainable and economic destination.

El Paso shows that the destination can control usable capacity. Phoenix shows it can command more planned capital than advanced treatment itself. Cave Creek and H₂Oaks show how existing networks and geology can relieve the gate. Palm Coast shows that even successful relief usually replaces one residual system with another.

PFAS may tighten that architecture in selected places, but the evidence still points to a heterogeneous constraint rather than a national shortage. The next few years should reveal whether residual handling remains a site-specific engineering problem—or becomes a recurring category of infrastructure in its own right.
