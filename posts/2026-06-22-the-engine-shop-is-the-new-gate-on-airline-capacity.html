The Engine Shop Is the Next Gate on Airline Capacity

Airline capacity is normally discussed through aircraft deliveries. Boeing has production and certification problems. Airbus has a backlog measured in years. Airlines also face scarce pilots, congested airports, air-traffic-control limits and closed airspace.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-06-22 · 15 min read

Yet an airline does not operate an order book. It operates aircraft with serviceable engines.

The engine constraint appears precisely because airlines have tried to route around the more visible airframe constraint. They keep older aircraft flying longer, raise utilization and take delivery of fuel-efficient aircraft powered by newer engines. That increases the burden on maintenance systems already coping with durability problems, mandatory inspections, parts shortages and a shortage of qualified repair capacity.

The relevant unit is no longer simply the aircraft tail number. It is the engine serial number, remaining cycles, applicable service bulletins, spare coverage, shop position and expected return-to-service date.

That makes the engine shop a sequential gate, not a universal ceiling on aviation. Airlines can compensate with spare engines, leased aircraft, schedule changes and higher utilization. Wizz Air has done exactly that. But routing around the problem is costly, and the constraint remains material for exposed fleets and for Airbus’s A320-family production ramp.

The narrower conclusion survives scrutiny: certified engine return-to-service capacity is currently pacing parts of the aviation system, especially the Pratt & Whitney GTF fleet. It is not the only gate, and it may not remain the binding one after 2027.

A manufacturing defect became a fleet-planning problem

Pratt & Whitney’s geared-turbofan crisis shows how an engineering problem propagates through the commercial system.

RTX disclosed in September 2023 that a powder-metal manufacturing condition would require approximately 600 to 700 incremental PW1100G shop visits between 2023 and 2026. The company expected accelerated removals, increased aircraft-on-ground levels, additional parts output and a $3.0 billion to $3.5 billion pre-tax operating-profit impact. (RTX GTF fleet update)

The sequence matters. A material condition triggered service instructions. Regulators converted those instructions into mandatory inspection and replacement work. Engines then entered a maintenance system whose output depends on parts, approved repairs, trained mechanics, test cells and paperwork—not simply an empty bay.

JetBlue’s disclosures show the result at operator level. The airline expected roughly 200-day shop visits for PW1500G engines and about 300 days for PW1100G engines, while reporting four GTF-related aircraft grounded at both December 31, 2025 and March 31, 2026. A visit measured in hundreds of days changes the economics of spare engines, fleet planning and aircraft utilization.

Wizz Air provides a larger fleet example. Its GTF-related groundings fell from 42 aircraft at March 31, 2025 to 30 one year later and 24 by June 5, 2026. Management expected 15 to 20 grounded aircraft at the end of fiscal 2027 and complete recovery by the end of calendar 2027. (Wizz final results)

ITA Airways illustrates the legal and bargaining consequences. Its chief executive said in June 2026 that engine problems had grounded almost 20% of an 80-aircraft fleet and estimated damage at approximately €150 million. The airline was considering legal action; no filed case or adjudicated liability had yet been established. Source: ITA engine dispute

Maintenance is therefore no longer a back-office cost center. It affects schedules, aircraft orders, compensation packages, service agreements and litigation strategy.

The binding asset is completion throughput

“Engine-shop capacity” can be misleading. An engine may enter a facility without leaving it on time.

The real output measure is completed, tested and certified engines returned to customers. A shop can add induction slots while accumulating work-in-progress because engines are waiting for turbine blades, disks, approved repairs, technical instructions or test-cell time.

IATA and Oliver Wyman estimated that aircraft supply-chain failures could impose $11.3 billion of airline costs in 2025. The estimate included $4.2 billion of delayed fuel savings, $3.1 billion of additional maintenance, $2.6 billion of excess engine leasing and $1.4 billion of inventory expense. It is a modeled estimate for the broader aircraft supply chain, not an audited engine-MRO loss account.

The report nevertheless identified a clear operating problem: 75% of surveyed MRO respondents said engine and APU turnaround had increased, and some engine visits that historically took 30 to 60 days were extending beyond 300 days. Piece-part availability was among the leading impediments. (IATA–Oliver Wyman report)

This distinction determines which investments provide real relief. New buildings help only when supported by qualified mechanics, repair instructions, component capacity, tooling, material inventories and engine testing.

It also explains why used serviceable material matters. Pratt is expanding USM inventories. Airlines and MRO providers are seeking approved alternatives to new OEM parts. Teardown firms can convert grounded or distressed aircraft into engines and components that return other aircraft to service.

Business Insider documented nearly new Spirit A320neos being dismantled for parts. Cirium data cited in the article indicated that a pair of GTF engines could lease for approximately $400,000 per month. That figure is a reported transaction-market indication, not a comprehensive lease-rate index, but the teardown decision still reveals the hierarchy of scarcity: a serviceable propulsion system can have more immediate economic utility than a young intact aircraft. (Spirit A320neo teardown)

New capacity is arriving, but announcements are not redeliveries

The supply response is substantial. Pratt, GE Aerospace, Safran, MTU, Iberia Maintenance and other providers are adding shops, component repairs, test cells, tooling and trained labor.

Figures are drawn from company disclosures, airline filings and the licensed-MRO announcements cited throughout the report.

MTU’s Fort Worth induction is stronger evidence than a construction announcement because a customer engine has entered the workflow. It still does not prove repeatable output. The relevant milestone is the first tested and certified redelivery, followed by repeat completions.

Safran’s Hyderabad facility has a substantial physical and workforce plan, including a target of 300 LEAP visits a year and more than 100 technicians and engineers trained annually. Those are capacity targets. The commercial proof will be actual completed visits, utilization and customer allocation.

Safran’s Querétaro complex opened with LEAP overhaul, component repair and test capabilities. Its longer-term target reaches as many as 350 annual LEAP visits and tests by 2030. That figure should be read as designed capacity, not forecast revenue or achieved throughput.

The same discipline applies to durability improvements. CFM has deployed more than 1,200 LEAP-1A high-pressure-turbine durability kits and expanded use of its reverse-bleed modification. In July 2026 it secured FAA and EASA certification for the LEAP-1B durability package, with full new-production cutover targeted for early 2027.

Certification is a necessary milestone. The economic result depends on whether the new hardware produces longer time on wing and fewer unscheduled removals in airline service.

Airbus shows how maintenance reaches new production

The aftermarket constraint also reaches airframers.

Airbus expects engine availability to influence its A320-family ramp through 2026 and 2027. The company has targeted 70 to 75 A320-family aircraft per month by the end of 2027 before stabilizing around 75.

Airbus has added final-assembly capacity, including a new Toulouse A320-family line. But a final-assembly line cannot deliver an aircraft without engines. Airbus identified Pratt supply as a pacing input even while adding physical airframe capacity. (Airbus Q1 2026 results)

The report cannot prove that every repaired engine directly displaces a new-production engine. Parts, personnel and manufacturing resources are not perfectly interchangeable, and GE Aerospace has disputed a simple zero-sum interpretation.

The narrower mechanism is sufficiently supported: engine OEMs must expand new production while supporting a large installed fleet, mandatory campaigns and growing aftermarket demand. Failure in any part of that system can delay either aircraft already in service or aircraft awaiting delivery.

That makes Airbus’s achieved monthly output a more useful indicator than factory square footage. The thesis weakens sharply once Airbus sustains at least 70 A320-family aircraft per month and states that propulsion supply is no longer pacing the ramp.

The money follows availability, but OEMs retain control

The current value migration is better described as rebundling than displacement.

Engine OEMs control design authority, proprietary parts, technical data, service bulletins and much of the customer relationship. Long-term service agreements turn episodic overhaul spending into contracted revenue while transferring part of the maintenance-cost and performance risk back to the OEM.

GE Aerospace’s Commercial Engines & Services business reported approximately $171.4 billion of service remaining-performance obligations at March 31, 2026. That figure includes long-duration obligations associated with existing and future fleets; it is not current MRO revenue or an incremental market-size estimate.

Pratt continues to bundle engine sales with EngineWise service. British Airways selected GTF engines for 33 firm and 30 option A320neo-family aircraft in July 2026 and paired the selection with a 12-year service agreement beginning with deliveries in 2027. The transaction demonstrates repeat procurement despite the current GTF disruption. It does not disclose queue priority, turnaround guarantees or contract economics. (British Airways GTF selection)

OEM control is not absolute. CFM and IATA renewed an aftermarket agreement through February 2033 covering access to manuals, approved third-party parts and repairs, independent MRO providers and warranty treatment based on root cause. CFM’s mature CFM56 system already supports more than 23,000 engines through approximately 40 shops, with CFM itself performing about one-third of overhauls. (IATA–CFM agreement)

That base rate matters. A large engine fleet can evolve into a distributed maintenance ecosystem rather than remain permanently trapped in an OEM-owned queue.

But independent shops may capture labor and facility revenue while OEMs retain higher-value control through parts, repairs, data, software and long-term contracts. Lessors monetize the time value of immediately available engines. USM and teardown firms monetize inventory scarcity. PMA and DER providers can capture replacement-part and repair spend only where regulators, airlines, lessors and warranties accept their products.

The same grounded engine can generate an OEM shop visit, lessor rent, USM sales and additional airline inventory. Those should not be counted as four separate markets. Much of the activity is overlapping expenditure generated by one unavailable asset.

Wizz is the strongest counterexample

Wizz Air prevents the thesis from being stated too strongly.

The airline expanded available seat-kilometers by 8.5%, passengers by 10% and its fleet by 13.4% during a period in which dozens of GTF-powered aircraft remained grounded. Deliveries, leased capacity, operational workarounds and higher utilization allowed the carrier to grow around the bottleneck.

That means engine maintenance is not an absolute capacity ceiling.

It is instead a productivity tax and a fleet-specific sequential constraint. Wizz could grow, but its grounded aircraft represented capital that could not produce scheduled capacity. The company required OEM support, a long-term agreement and a multi-year recovery plan extending through the end of 2027.

The recovery path is now visible. Groundings fell from 42 to 30 and then 24. Pratt reported higher shop output. New repair, inspection and material capacity is entering the system.

This is the strongest case that the crisis may resolve as a catch-up cycle rather than become a permanent industrial bottleneck. If GTF turnaround returns to 60–90 days before the LEAP fleet generates its larger maintenance wave, the engine-shop thesis should be narrowed to a temporary, engine-family-specific disruption.

The historical base rate is more optimistic than the crisis narrative

The closest historical analogue is the CFM56 aftermarket.

CFM56 became one of the world’s largest commercial-engine fleets. Its maintenance system ultimately developed dozens of competing shops, broad used-material flows, independent repair capability and a mature spare-engine market.

The similarity is installed-base scale: LEAP is following CFM56 into a large global fleet requiring regional maintenance capacity and repeat shop visits.

The difference is technical and temporal. LEAP and GTF use newer materials and operate under tighter thermal and mechanical conditions. Their early durability modifications are arriving while aerospace supply chains remain constrained and airlines are maintaining older fleets longer. The industry must expand repair capacity while simultaneously supporting current operations and increasing new-engine production.

The historical base rate therefore argues against permanent scarcity, but not against a difficult transition. The central race is whether capacity, parts and durability improvements mature before the next shop-visit wave replenishes the queue.

What would prove the thesis wrong

The thesis fails if the full return-to-service system normalizes, not merely if more shops open.

Cross-operator evidence would be decisive: Wizz below 10 GTF-related grounded aircraft, JetBlue reporting PW1100G turnaround at or below 90 days, and another materially exposed carrier reporting no engine-driven capacity loss by the end of 2027.

Completion data would be even stronger. Four consecutive quarters in which GTF and LEAP completions match or exceed inductions, without growth in engines waiting for parts, would show that the industrial stack has balanced.

The market should confirm the same change. Spare-engine lease rates should decline, spare-pool availability should improve and teardown decisions should stop favoring the extraction of young engines.

Finally, durability fixes must reduce maintenance demand in real airline service. Production cutover and installed kits are intermediate milestones. Longer time on wing and lower unscheduled-removal rates are the operating outcome.

What to watch

  • 2027-03-31: Does JetBlue still report PW1100G shop visits above 180 days?
  • 2027-03-31: Has Wizz reduced GTF-related groundings to 20 aircraft or fewer?
  • 2027 results: Does Airbus still call Pratt a pacing factor for A320 production?
  • 2027-06-30: Has CFM completed LEAP-1B durability-kit production cutover?
  • 2027-12-31: Have two new LEAP facilities reported completed customer redeliveries?
  • Four consecutive quarters: Do engine completions exceed inductions without rising parts-waiting queues?
