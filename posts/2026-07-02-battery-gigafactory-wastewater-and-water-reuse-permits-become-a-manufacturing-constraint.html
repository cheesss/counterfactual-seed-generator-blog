Battery Gigafactory Wastewater And Water Reuse Permits Become A Manufacturing Constraint

Battery manufacturing is usually framed as a fight over cathode chemistry, lithium supply, dry rooms, grid power, skilled labor, subsidies, and final EV demand. Those constraints are real. They are also the ones most visible in earnings calls and industrial-policy speeches.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 14 min read

In brief

  • 0.425 MGD caps HL-GA's permitted non-process discharge into North Bryan's system.
  • 0.85 MGD is Savannah's temporary bridge while Bryan County finishes its own plant.
  • $105 million marked a 2023 De Soto civic breakdown of water, sewer, and fire upgrades.
  • 2.6 MGD is De Soto's expanded wastewater-treatment capacity after the March 2026 buildout.
  • 6,240 m³/day is the reclaimed-water capacity Lantania is building for PowerCo Sagunto.

The visible fight is chemistry; the local fight is the sewer

In local permit files, another clock appears: water intake, sewer capacity, industrial pretreatment, reclaimed-water systems, RO concentrate, sludge, offsite residues, and the public agency willing to sign the discharge path. Water reuse lowers freshwater draw. It does not make salts, metals, organics, solvents, conductivity spikes, pH excursions, sludges, spent media, and concentrated brines disappear.

That changes the gating question. It is no longer only where the plant gets water. It is who accepts the concentrated remainder, under what permit, at what flow, with what monitoring data, and at what cost.

Battery plants are built as synchronized systems. A cathode line, anode line, dry room, coating process, formation area, utility plant, pretreatment train, sewer connection, receiving POTW, sludge route, and offsite waste vendor all have to arrive together. A plant can buy around one missing supplier. It cannot run around a missing residual path without adding another approval, contract, operating cost, or schedule risk.

Georgia shows the gate in operating documents

The clearest U.S. case in the report is HL-GA Battery Company in Bryan County, Georgia, the Hyundai Motor Group and LG Energy Solution cell venture beside Hyundai's Metaplant. The public company story is a 30 GWh battery plant. (Hyundai joint-venture announcement)

The local water story is a signed pretreatment permit that authorizes only specified non-process streams - RO concentrate, non-contact cooling water, boiler blowdown, and sanitary wastewater - into the system tributary to North Bryan Water Reclamation Facility. The permit caps flow at 0.425 MGD, sets a 1,500 mg/L TDS daily maximum and a 2,400 uS/cm conductivity daily maximum, and requires regular monitoring across metals, organics, nutrients, COD, BOD, TSS, oil and grease, and cyanide. (Georgia EPD permit)

The response-to-comments record is even more revealing. Georgia EPD says battery process wastewater is containerized and shipped offsite, while the sewer permit covers non-process waste streams. The same record points to offsite routes for cathode slurry hazardous waste, electrolyte waste, anode and cathode nonhazardous waste, recycling, land disposal, incineration, and hauling. The public sewer permit is only one leg of the system. The other leg is a logistics network of incinerators, landfills, recyclers, and haulers that must accept the residuals the plant cannot put into the POTW. (Georgia EPD response record)

The Georgia file also shows how the local-government layer becomes the schedule bridge. Bryan County's North Bryan Water Reclamation Facility was permitted at an initial 4.0 MGD monthly average with expansion to 8.0 MGD, but a Savannah sewer-service amendment describes temporary handling through Savannah while Bryan County finished its new facility. (Georgia EPD response record)

Savannah conditionally agreed to accept up to 0.85 MGD monthly average from the Hyundai-area flows and kept the right to raise rates or suspend service under noncompliance conditions. A factory ramp, in other words, was partly connected to a temporary municipal contract and a receiving plant's spare capacity. (Savannah sewer amendment)

That is not a claim that the Georgia plant is doomed by wastewater. It is a claim that the water story is not a generic sustainability badge. It is a set of binding documents, named receiving systems, named residual outlets, and commissioning-time contingencies. If those hold, the constraint relaxes. If first-discharge characterization shows a higher-strength stream than expected, the bottleneck can return during ramp.

Ahead-of-time infrastructure can defuse the problem

BlueOval SK shows the opposite pattern. Wastewater is present in the record, but the project documents suggest that local infrastructure was deliberately oversized or staged ahead of the battery plants.

The DOE environmental assessment for Stanton, Tennessee, describes a 45 GWh facility with about 1.2 MGD of water demand and 0.33 MGD of wastewater, an onsite wastewater-treatment capacity of 5.1 MGD, a new 36-mile pipeline to the Mississippi River, and a water-reuse system that sends treated wastewater back for partial reuse. For Glendale, Kentucky, the same assessment lists about 2.4 MGD of water demand and 0.66 MGD of wastewater against 8.1 MGD of existing municipal wastewater capacity. (DOE BlueOval SK assessment)

That cuts both ways. It confirms that water and wastewater are first-order workstreams in battery manufacturing. It also weakens any claim that they are always the binding constraint. Where a megasite authority, county, DOE-backed borrower, and municipal utility coordinate early, the wastewater gate can become a solved construction package rather than a late-stage surprise.

The remaining bottleneck can still migrate. By late 2025 and 2026, the Ford-SK On partnership itself had become a moving target as the parties moved to unwind BlueOval SK ownership. Reuters reported Ford taking the Kentucky plants, SK On the Tennessee plant, and a flexible Tennessee production start, while Ford's filing laid out the disposition agreement. That is a demand-and-capital-allocation problem, not a sewer problem. (Reuters on BlueOval SK split) (Ford filing)

De Soto puts a municipal price on the ramp

De Soto, Kansas, shows the infrastructure-spend version of the same point. Panasonic's battery plant did not arrive on an empty field and ask for a routine connection. The city's Panasonic project page ties road, water, sewer, tower, and fire-station work to the factory. (City of De Soto Panasonic project page)

A 2023 civic breakdown on that page listed $38 million of water-treatment upgrades, $26 million of sewer-treatment upgrades, $20 million of sewer lines, and a $9 million water tower, among other items. Later city material on the same page shows a larger capital-improvements package of roughly $248.45 million, including a $54.5 million water-treatment facility, a $43.3 million sewer-treatment facility, and $40.0 million of sewer-system expansions.

The contractor record makes the time pressure explicit. Burns & McDonnell says De Soto's wastewater-treatment expansion doubled capacity from 1.3 MGD to 2.6 MGD and began operations in March 2026. A separate project record says Garney and HDR delivered a $43 million water-treatment expansion that increased city capacity to 8 MGD. Those are not marginal line items. They are industrial-enabling assets built on a factory clock. (Burns & McDonnell De Soto WWTF) (WaterOnline on De Soto WTP)

The control point in De Soto is not just Panasonic. It is also the city council, sewer district, state regulator, design-build contractor, construction manager, process engineer, and long-lead electrical supplier. The spend path runs through municipal infrastructure as much as through cell-making equipment. That is one reason public markets can miss the bottleneck: the decisive procurement can sit in a council agenda packet or an engineering press release rather than a battery-company investor deck.

Europe shows the same problem after approval

PowerCo's Sagunto gigafactory adds the reclaimed-water version. Lantania said in April 2026 that it would build a reclaimed-water treatment plant for the site with 6,240 m³/day of capacity, taking water from the municipal wastewater treatment plant for industrial use. Volkswagen's own release described Sagunto as a 40 GWh cell factory with room to expand. The relief node here is concrete: a named water contractor, a treatment capacity, and a link between municipal effluent and battery operations. (Lantania on Sagunto) (Volkswagen on PowerCo Sagunto)

Tesla's Grünheide factory shows why the issue does not end at final approval. Brandenburg's 2022 permit announcement described a 536-page approval package for up to 500,000 vehicles per year, more than 23,700 pages of submitted material, more than 400 ancillary provisions, an in-house wastewater pretreatment plant, and 96 provisions related to water protection, wastewater disposal, precipitation water, discharge limits, and treatment methods. (Brandenburg permit summary)

Later reporting on Tesla's expansion and local wastewater-limit complaints shows the issue staying politically and technically live after approval. That is the important point for readers. Permitting is not just a one-time entitlement risk. It is also an operating-evidence burden that can stay active while a site expands. (Reuters on Tesla expansion) (Smart Water Magazine on WSE complaint)

Northvolt Ett pushes the same lesson into operations. Northvolt's 2023 report listed five environmental noncompliance cases, including treated wastewater at Northvolt Ett with excessively high temperature and pH and a Northvolt Labs case involving treated process water to a municipal wastewater treatment plant with too high a lithium concentration. The fines were small relative to the plant. The signal was not. Residual handling remains a live operating proof after startup. (Northvolt 2023 report)

Who controls the residual path

The root cause sits deeper than any one permit. Battery manufacturing needs high-purity water for process and utility systems, but the plant's internal reuse equipment concentrates what it removes. Reverse osmosis, deionization, cooling towers, boilers, scrubbers, cleaning systems, and pretreatment steps all create side streams. A reuse system reduces freshwater intake by creating cleaner water and dirtier residuals.

That is why the problem fits residual handling better than simple permitting. The permit is the visible checkpoint. The scarce item is the documented right to keep moving the residual every day during ramp: a POTW permit that can handle the load without pass-through or interference, an offsite TSDF or landfill contract, an incineration route, a recycling outlet, and the lab data that prove the stream matches the route.

Control of that path is fragmented. State regulators, municipal utilities, offsite waste vendors, water-treatment EPCs, haulers, and the plant operator's environmental team all hold part of it. Markets report battery capacity in GWh and capex in billions. Residual systems are reported in MGD, mg/L, conductivity ceilings, temporary sewer amendments, and waste-routing tables. A 0.425 MGD permit can look trivial beside a 30 GWh factory until the plant has to prove the stream stays inside the promised envelope.

The counterarguments are real

The strongest counterargument is straightforward: in several named cases, wastewater volumes are small relative to host capacity. Group14's Moses Lake anode-material facility expects roughly 100,000 gallons per day of wastewater against the Dunes WWTP capacity described in the DOE record. Sila's Moses Lake silicon-anode plant has already reached operations. (Group14 DOE assessment) (Sila Moses Lake announcement)

BlueOval's Kentucky wastewater estimate sits below listed municipal capacity, and De Soto solved the infrastructure problem with money and schedule discipline. In those cases, wastewater looks more like a necessary workstream than the binding bottleneck. (DOE BlueOval SK assessment)

The second counterargument is stronger: bottlenecks migrate. HL-GA's 2025 startup delay was publicly tied not to water but to an immigration raid and the loss of specialized workers installing battery equipment. Reuters reported a minimum two-to-three-month delay after about 475 workers were arrested. (Reuters on HL-GA raid)

BlueOval's restructuring points to demand, subsidies, and balance-sheet strategy. Group14's DOE record flags silicon-containing gas supply as a possible scarce input. Dry rooms, fire-code validation, solvent recovery, power supply, interconnection, workforce certification, and customer qualification can all dominate. (Reuters on BlueOval SK split) (Group14 DOE assessment)

The claim here is narrower than "wastewater always blocks gigafactories." The better reading is that water reuse often pushes the constraint one layer deeper, into the receiving path for the residuals that reuse leaves behind. Sometimes that path is solved early. Sometimes it remains a live gate. The job is to tell the difference from the operating documents, not from the slogan on the project website.

What to watch

This thesis gets weaker if the next wave of battery sites receives industrial pretreatment permits with no unusual flow caps, no special focus on conductivity or TDS, no offsite process-wastewater routing, and no public challenge. It also gets weaker if first-discharge characterization and routine monitoring show stable compliance through ramp, or if municipal utilities build enough reclaimed-water and wastewater capacity ahead of schedule that residual handling becomes a routine construction package.

The practical watch list is mundane:

  • pretreatment permits and sewer-service amendments that name allowed streams, flow caps, and receiving facilities
  • 90-day first-discharge characterization reports and routine discharge-monitoring results
  • consent orders or complaints tied to conductivity, TDS, lithium, nickel, pH, COD, TOC, or sludge handling
  • reclaimed-water EPC awards and municipal wastewater-capacity additions delivered before factory ramp
  • waste manifests, TSDF routes, landfill routes, or incineration routes for process wastewater, sludge, and spent media
  • signs the bottleneck migrated instead to labor, demand, input supply, power, or equipment commissioning

The implication is restrained but useful. The next battery-capacity announcement should be read beside a second capacity number: the receiving capacity for the residue left by the factory's water system. A gigafactory is not only a GWh asset. It is also a municipal-industrial water system with a residual stream, and the parties that can prove, price, and control that stream hold a quiet piece of the manufacturing schedule.
