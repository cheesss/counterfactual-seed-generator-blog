Battery Gigafactory Wastewater And Water Reuse Permits Become A Manufacturing Constraint

Bottleneck shape: residual-handling. CSG_VISUAL_BLOCK_0 Battery manufacturing is usually framed as a fight over cathode chemistry, lithium supply, dry rooms, grid power, skilled labor, subsidies, and final EV demand. Those are real constraints.

By Bottleneck Briefs · Published 2026-07-02 · 14 min read

The visible fight is chemistry; the local fight is the sewer

Bottleneck shape: residual-handling.

Battery manufacturing is usually framed as a fight over cathode chemistry, lithium supply, dry rooms, grid power, skilled labor, subsidies, and final EV demand. Those are real constraints. They are also the constraints most visible in earnings calls and industrial-policy speeches. In local permit folders, another clock appears: water intake, sewer capacity, industrial pretreatment, reclaimed-water systems, RO concentrate, sludge, offsite process residues, and the public agency willing to sign the document that lets a plant discharge.

The thesis is narrow: battery gigafactories can advertise water reuse and still be limited by the residuals left after the reuse system works. Reuse lowers the freshwater draw. It does not make the salts, metals, organics, solvents, conductivity, pH excursions, sludges, spent media, and concentrated brines disappear. It moves the gating question from “where will the plant get water?” to “who accepts the concentrated remainder, under what permit, at what flow, with what monitoring data, and at what cost?”

That distinction matters because battery factories are built as large, synchronized systems. A cathode line, anode line, dry room, coating process, formation area, utility plant, wastewater pretreatment train, sewer connection, local POTW, sludge route, and offsite waste vendor have to arrive together. A plant can buy electrode-coating equipment from one group of suppliers and hire a cleanroom contractor from another. It cannot run around a missing discharge path for non-process wastewater, floor-cleaning residues, or out-of-spec industrial effluent. It can store, haul, dilute, pretreat, or stage ramp-up, but each workaround creates another approval, contract, operating cost, or schedule dependency.

Named cases show where the constraint moves

The clearest U.S. case in the search set is HL-GA Battery Company in Bryan County, Georgia, the Hyundai Motor Group and LG Energy Solution battery-cell joint venture beside Hyundai’s Metaplant. The public company story is a 30 GWh battery plant supplying Hyundai, Kia, and Genesis EVs. The local water story is more mechanical. Georgia EPD’s signed pretreatment permit authorizes HL-GA to send only specified non-process streams — RO concentrate, non-contact cooling water, boiler blowdown, and sanitary wastewater — to the sewerage system tributary to the North Bryan Water Reclamation Facility. The permit caps flow at 0.425 MGD, sets a TDS daily maximum of 1,500 mg/L and a conductivity daily maximum of 2,400 µS/cm, and imposes daily or weekly monitoring across metals, organics, nutrients, COD, BOD, TSS, oil and grease, and cyanide. It also requires notification before expansions or physical changes that could change pollutant character. [GA-HL-permit]

The response-to-comments record is more revealing than the permit table. Georgia EPD states that the battery process wastewater is containerized and shipped offsite, while the permitted sewer discharge covers non-process waste streams. The same document identifies offsite routes for cathode slurry hazardous waste, electrolyte waste, anode and cathode nonhazardous waste, recycling, land disposal, incineration, and hauling. That means the public sewer permit is only one leg of the operating system. The other leg is a logistics network of incinerators, landfills, recyclers, and haulers that must accept the residuals the plant cannot put into the POTW. [GA-HL-RTC]

The Georgia case also shows how the local government layer can become the practical schedule bridge. Bryan County’s North Bryan Water Reclamation Facility was permitted at an initial 4.0 MGD monthly average with expansion up to 8.0 MGD, but a Savannah sewer-service amendment describes temporary handling through Savannah while Bryan County finished its new facility. Savannah conditionally agreed to accept up to 0.85 MGD monthly average from the Hyundai-area flows and reserved the right to raise rates or suspend service under noncompliance conditions. A factory ramp, in other words, was partly connected to a temporary municipal contract, a receiving plant’s capacity, monitoring stations, and the right to plug a transfer line. [GA-HL-RTC] [SAV-SEWER]

That is not an argument that the Georgia battery plant is doomed by wastewater. It is an argument that the water story is not a generic sustainability feature. It is a set of binding documents, named receiving systems, named residual outlets, and operating contingencies. If those documents hold, the constraint relaxes. If first-discharge characterization or routine monitoring reveals a higher-strength stream than expected, the bottleneck returns at the worst time: during commissioning.

The counterweight: infrastructure built ahead of the plant

BlueOval SK shows the opposite pattern: wastewater appears in the record, but the project-level documents suggest that local infrastructure was deliberately oversized or staged ahead of the battery plants. The DOE environmental assessment for the Stanton, Tennessee, battery plant describes a 45 GWh facility with roughly 1.2 MGD of water demand and 0.33 MGD of wastewater, an onsite wastewater treatment capacity of 5.1 MGD at the megasite, a new 36-mile pipeline to the Mississippi River, and a water reuse system returning treated wastewater for partial reuse by the adjacent assembly plant. For Glendale, Kentucky, the same assessment lists about 2.4 MGD of water demand and 0.66 MGD of wastewater against 8.1 MGD of existing municipal wastewater capacity. [DOE-BOSK-EA]

This cuts both ways. It confirms that water and wastewater are first-order engineering workstreams in battery manufacturing. It also weakens any broad claim that they are always the binding constraint. Where a megasite authority, county, DOE-backed borrower, and municipal utility coordinate early, the wastewater gate can become a solved construction package rather than a late-stage surprise. The remaining bottleneck may migrate to demand, joint-venture structure, dry-room commissioning, equipment validation, or labor. By late 2025 and 2026, the Ford/SK On partnership itself became a moving target as the parties moved to unwind BlueOval SK ownership: Ford would take the Kentucky plants, SK On the Tennessee plant, and the Tennessee production start remained flexible in the Reuters account. That is a demand-and-capital-allocation constraint, not a sewer constraint. [REUTERS-BOSK-SPLIT] [SEC-FORD-BOSK]

De Soto shows the price tag

De Soto, Kansas, provides the infrastructure-spend version of the same point. Panasonic’s battery plant did not merely appear on an empty field and ask the city for a water connection. The City of De Soto’s public material lists road, water, sewer, tower, and fire-station upgrades tied to the Panasonic project. A 2023 civic breakdown listed $38 million of water treatment upgrades, $26 million of sewer treatment upgrades, $20 million of sewer lines, and a $9 million water tower, among other items. Later city materials show a larger capital-improvements package of roughly $248.45 million, including a $54.5 million water-treatment facility, a $43.3 million sewer-treatment facility, and $40.0 million of sewer system expansions. [DESOTO-CITY] [DESOTO-BY-NUMBERS]

The contractor records make the time pressure explicit. Burns & McDonnell says De Soto’s wastewater treatment expansion doubled capacity from 1.3 MGD to 2.6 MGD, began operations in March 2026, and was driven by the $4 billion Panasonic battery facility and tighter nitrogen and phosphorus requirements. A separate water-treatment record says Garney and HDR delivered a $43 million expansion that increased the city’s water treatment capacity to 8 MGD. Those are not marginal line items. They are industrial-enabling assets built on a factory clock. [BMCD-DESOTO-WWTF] [WATERONLINE-DESOTO-WTP]

The control point in such a case is not just the battery company. The city council, sewer district, state water regulator, design-build contractor, construction manager, process engineer, and long-lead electrical supplier all hold pieces of the ramp. The spend path runs through municipal infrastructure, not only through cell-making equipment. That makes the constraint easy for public markets to miss: the decisive procurement may sit in a council agenda packet, an engineering press release, or a state permit fact sheet rather than a battery-company investor deck.

Europe is running the same experiment

PowerCo’s Sagunto gigafactory in Spain adds the reclaimed-water version. Volkswagen’s battery unit is building the Sagunto site as a major European cell factory. Lantania announced in April 2026 that it would build a reclaimed-water treatment plant for PowerCo’s gigafactory with 6,240 m³/day of capacity, treating water from the municipal wastewater treatment plant for industrial use. The relief node is concrete: a named water-infrastructure contractor, a treatment capacity, and a connection between municipal effluent and battery cooling or utility demand. [LANTANIA-SAGUNTO] [VW-POWERCO-SAGUNTO]

Tesla’s Grünheide factory shows why the issue does not end at final approval. Brandenburg’s 2022 permit announcement described a 536-page approval package for up to 500,000 vehicles per year, aluminum and surface-treatment operations, battery-cell production, an in-house wastewater pretreatment plant, more than 23,700 pages of submitted material, and more than 400 ancillary provisions. It specifically identified 96 provisions related to water protection, wastewater disposal, precipitation water, limits for discharge into the wastewater pressure pipe, and treatment methods. Later reports about Tesla’s expansion and local wastewater-limit complaints show the post-approval stage remains politically and technically live. [TESLA-BB-PERMIT] [REUTERS-TESLA-EXPANSION] [SMARTWATER-TESLA-WSE]

Northvolt Ett offers a different lesson: permits can be in place and still leave operating evidence as the gating proof. Northvolt’s 2023 sustainability report reported five environmental noncompliance cases, including treated wastewater at Northvolt Ett with too high temperature and pH, and a Northvolt Labs case involving treated process water to a municipal wastewater treatment plant with too high a lithium concentration. The fines were small relative to the plant, but the signal is large: the residual-handling problem persists after commercial operation starts. A permit is not a static license. It is a continuing evidence burden. [NORTHVOLT-2023]

Who controls the constraint

The root cause sits deeper than any one permit. Battery manufacturing needs high-purity water for process and utility systems, but the factory’s internal reuse equipment concentrates what it removes. Reverse osmosis, deionization, cooling towers, boilers, scrubbers, cleaning systems, and pretreatment steps all create side streams. A water-reuse system reduces fresh intake by creating cleaner water and dirtier residuals. The irreducible constraint is the documented receiving path for those residuals: a POTW permit that can handle the load without pass-through or interference, an offsite TSDF or landfill contract, an incineration route, a recycling outlet, and the lab data that proves the stream matches the route.

That is why the constraint should be classified as residual-handling rather than simple permitting. A permit is the visible checkpoint. The scarce item is the right to keep moving the residual every day during ramp. The owner of that right is fragmented across state regulators, municipal utilities, offsite waste vendors, water-treatment EPCs, haulers, and the plant operator’s environmental team.

Markets can miss this because capacity is reported in GWh and capex in billions, while residual systems are reported in gallons per day, mg/L, µS/cm, temporary sewer amendments, priority-pollutant scans, and waste-routing tables. A 0.425 MGD permit does not sound like a strategic variable beside a 30 GWh factory. Yet if the plant cannot prove that the 0.425 MGD stream stays within the promised envelope, or if the offsite process wastewater route becomes expensive or unavailable, the production ramp can slow before a single electrode recipe becomes the binding issue.

The counterarguments

The strongest counterargument is straightforward: in several named cases, wastewater volumes are small relative to municipal capacity. Group14’s Moses Lake anode-material facility expects roughly 100,000 gpd of wastewater against the Dunes WWTP capacity described in the DOE record, and the Sila Moses Lake silicon-anode plant has already reached operations. BlueOval’s Kentucky wastewater estimate sits well below listed municipal capacity. De Soto solved the infrastructure problem with money and schedule discipline. For those projects, wastewater may be a necessary workstream rather than the binding bottleneck. [GROUP14-DOE-EA] [SILA-MOSES-LAKE] [DOE-BOSK-EA]

The second counterargument is stronger: bottlenecks migrate. HL-GA’s 2025 startup delay was publicly tied not to water but to an immigration raid and the loss of specialized workers installing battery equipment. Reuters reported a minimum two-to-three-month delay after roughly 475 workers were arrested. BlueOval’s JV restructuring points to demand, subsidies, and balance-sheet strategy. Group14’s DOE record flags silicon-containing gas supply as a possible scarce input. Dry rooms, fire-code validation, solvent recovery, power supply, interconnection, workforce certification, and customer qualification can all dominate. [REUTERS-HLGA-RAID] [REUTERS-BOSK-SPLIT] [GROUP14-DOE-EA]

What would prove this wrong, and what to watch

The idea becomes weaker if three things happen across the next wave of plants. First, large battery sites receive industrial pretreatment permits with no special flow caps, no unusual TDS or conductivity focus, no offsite process wastewater routing, and no public challenge. Second, first-discharge characterization and discharge monitoring reports show stable compliance through ramp. Third, municipal utilities build enough reclaimed-water and wastewater capacity ahead of schedule that residual handling becomes a routine engineering package. That would move the constraint back toward equipment, demand, or labor.

The documents to watch are mundane: pretreatment permits; local sewer-service amendments; POTW expansion schedules; special conductivity, TDS, lithium, nickel, phosphorus, pH, COD, and TOC limits; 90-day first-discharge characterization reports; priority-pollutant scans; consent orders; waste manifests; TSDF and landfill routes; reclaimed-water EPC awards; and city infrastructure reimbursement agreements. In a market accustomed to tracking cell chemistry announcements, these documents look administrative. They are often closer to the physical ramp.

The restrained implication is not that wastewater is the universal Achilles’ heel of battery manufacturing. It is that the next visible battery-capacity number should be read beside a second capacity number: the receiving capacity for the residue left by the factory’s water system. A gigafactory is not only a GWh asset. It is also a municipal-industrial water system with a residual stream. The companies and public agencies that can prove, price, and control that stream hold a quiet piece of the manufacturing schedule.
